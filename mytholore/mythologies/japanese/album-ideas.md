# Japanese Mythology: Album Ideas

Candidate stories for concept albums. Each one has a clear arc and enough material for 8–12 tracks. When an idea is chosen, copy `_templates/album/` to `mythologies/japanese/NN-<slug>/`, move the idea's notes into its `README.md` and `lore.md`, and mark it **Taken** here.

**Sources key:** **K** = *Kojiki* (712) · **NS** = *Nihon Shoki* (720) · **F** = *Fudoki* · **KM** = *Konjaku Monogatarishū* · **HM** = *Heike Monogatari* · **OZ** = *Otogizōshi*

## 1. Yomi: The Land of the Dead · **Taken** (`01-yomi/`)
**The story.** Izanagi and Izanami stand on the Floating Bridge of Heaven, stir the ocean with a jewelled spear, and the brine that drips from it forms the first island. They marry and give birth to the islands of Japan and to many kami. The last child is **Kagutsuchi**, the fire god, and his birth burns Izanami to death. In grief Izanagi beheads the child. Then he follows his wife into **Yomi**, the land of the dead. She has already eaten the food of Yomi and cannot simply return. She asks him to wait, and **not to look at her**. He breaks a tooth from his comb, lights it, and sees her rotting body, crawling with maggots and with eight thunder gods seated on it. He flees. Izanami, shamed, sends the hags of Yomi (*Yomotsu-shikome*) and an army of the dead after him. He throws his headdress and comb behind him, and they turn into grapes and bamboo shoots. He throws three peaches at the pass, *Yomotsu Hirasaka*. Then he blocks the pass with a great boulder. Through the stone they divorce: she vows to kill a thousand of his people each day, and he answers that he will cause fifteen hundred to be born. He bathes to wash off Yomi's pollution. From his left eye is born **Amaterasu**, from his right eye **Tsukuyomi**, and from his nose **Susanoo**.
- **Possible tracklist arc:**
  1. The Floating Bridge (creation, the spear in the brine)
  2. The Leech Child (the first, rejected child, sent away in a reed boat)
  3. Kagutsuchi (fire born, the mother burned)
  4. The Sword and the Blood (Izanagi slays his son)
  5. Descent (the road into Yomi)
  6. Do Not Look (the comb, the light, what he sees)
  7. The Hags of Yomi (the chase)
  8. Yomotsu Hirasaka (peaches, the boulder, the vow of the dead)
  9. Misogi (purification)
  10. Three Noble Children (sun, moon, storm)
- **Why it works:** it moves from creation to catastrophe to horror to rebirth. It suits a doom-to-epic sound: slow and funereal in Yomi, then a symphonic finale.
- **Sources:** K (Book 1, Chamberlain §§I–XI); NS (Book 1, with many variants).
- **Care:** tell it as the *Kojiki* tells it. The imagery of Izanami's decay is canonical. Don't make her a generic "demon queen".

## 2. Susanoo: The Storm Exiled
**The story.** Susanoo weeps and rages until the mountains wither, and he is banished. Before he leaves, he visits his sister **Amaterasu** in Heaven. They hold a contest of oaths, bearing children from each other's sword and jewels. Then Susanoo runs wild: he breaks the rice-field dikes, defiles her hall, and throws a flayed piebald horse through the roof of her sacred weaving hall, killing a weaving maiden. Amaterasu shuts herself inside the rock cave **Ama-no-Iwato**, and the world falls dark. The eight million gods gather. They hang a mirror and jewels on a sacred tree and set roosters crowing, and **Ame-no-Uzume** dances on an upturned tub until the gods roar with laughter. Curious, Amaterasu peeks out, sees her own light in the mirror, and is pulled out into the world. Susanoo is punished and cast out of Heaven. In Izumo he meets an old couple weeping: the eight-headed, eight-tailed serpent **Yamata no Orochi** has eaten seven of their eight daughters. He turns the last daughter, **Kushinada-hime**, into a comb and wears it in his hair. He sets out eight vats of eight-times-brewed sake, and the drunken serpent falls asleep. He hacks it apart until the river runs red, and in its tail he finds the sword later called **Kusanagi**, one of the three imperial regalia.
- **Possible tracklist arc:** The Weeping God · Oath of Swords and Jewels · Defilement · The Cave of Heaven · Eight Million Gods · The Dance of Uzume · Cast Down · The Eighth Daughter · Eight Vats · Yamata no Orochi · The Sword in the Tail · Eightfold Clouds (his marriage poem, *Yakumo tatsu*)
- **Why it works:** it has a flawed, violent hero, a cosmic blackout, and a dragon slaying. The Orochi track can be the album's big epic.
- **Sources:** K (Book 1, §§XIII–XIX); NS (Book 1).
- **Care:** Ame-no-Uzume's dance is sacred comedy, the origin of *kagura* dance. Treat it as joyous, not sexualised. Amaterasu is a living deity at Ise, so the cave scene should be reverent.

## 3. The Great Lord of the Land (Ōkuninushi)
**The story.** Ōnamuji is the youngest of eighty brothers, who treat him as their porter. On the road they meet a **white hare** of Inaba that has been skinned by sea creatures. The brothers cruelly tell it to bathe in salt water. Ōnamuji tells it to wash in fresh water and roll in cattail pollen, and heals it. The hare prophesies that he will win the princess Yagami-hime. The jealous brothers kill him twice: once with a red-hot boulder dressed as a boar, and once by crushing him in a split tree. His mother's prayers bring him back both times. He flees to the underworld realm of **Susanoo**, falls in love with Susanoo's daughter **Suseri-hime**, and survives his trials: a room of snakes, a room of centipedes and wasps, and a burning field that a mouse helps him escape. He ties the sleeping Susanoo's hair to the rafters and escapes with his wife, his sword, his bow and his singing *koto*. Susanoo shouts his blessing after him: become **Ōkuninushi**, the Great Lord of the Land. Ōkuninushi builds the land with the tiny god **Sukunabikona**. Then Heaven demands it. Envoys come and fail. Finally the thunder-sword god **Takemikazuchi** sits cross-legged on the point of a sword planted on the beach at Inasa. Ōkuninushi's son **Takeminakata** wrestles him, loses, and flees to Suwa. Ōkuninushi yields the land to Heaven, and asks only for a great hall, **Izumo Taisha**, where he still rules the unseen world.
- **Possible tracklist arc:** The Hare of Inaba · Eighty Brothers · The Burning Boar · Split Tree · Descent to Ne-no-Kuni · The Room of Serpents · The Burning Field · Hair Bound to the Rafters · The Great Lord · Sukunabikona · The Sword on the Waves · Ceding the Land
- **Why it works:** the underdog becomes a king and then loses his kingdom. It is the most emotional arc in the *Kojiki*.
- **Sources:** K (Book 1, §§XX–XXXII).
- **Care:** Ōkuninushi is actively worshipped at Izumo. The land-ceding (*kuni-yuzuri*) should be tragic but dignified, and never framed as a justified conquest.

## 4. The Palace Beneath the Waves (Umisachi and Yamasachi)
**The story.** Ninigi, Amaterasu's grandson, descends to Mount Takachiho carrying the mirror, the jewel and the sword. He marries **Konohanasakuya-hime**, the blossom princess, but sends back her sister **Iwanaga-hime**, the rock princess. Because he does, human lives are brief as blossoms rather than lasting as stone. Konohanasakuya gives birth inside a burning hut to prove the children are his. Two of them are the brothers **Umisachi** (luck of the sea) and **Yamasachi** (luck of the mountain), who trade tools. Yamasachi loses his brother's fishhook, and Umisachi refuses any replacement. An old sea god sends Yamasachi in a basket-boat to the palace of **Watatsumi**, the dragon king of the sea. There he marries the princess **Toyotama-hime**, and lives three years before he remembers the hook. It is found in the throat of a sea bream. He returns with the jewels of the flowing and ebbing tide and humbles his brother. Toyotama-hime comes ashore to give birth and asks him **not to look**. He looks, and sees a great sea creature (a *wani*, a sea dragon) coiling in the birthing hut. Ashamed, she returns to the sea and closes the road between the worlds forever.
- **Possible tracklist arc:** Descent of the Heavenly Grandson · Blossom and Stone · Born in Fire · The Lost Hook · The Basket Boat · Watatsumi · Three Years Under the Sea · The Tide Jewels · Do Not Look (II) · The Closed Sea
- **Why it works:** an oceanic, atmospheric album with a love story and a mirror of the Yomi taboo. It is a softer, more mystical record in the cycle.
- **Sources:** K (Book 1, §§XXXIII–XLIII); NS.
- **Care:** the descent to Takachiho is the ancestral myth of the imperial line. Tell it as myth, with no "divine nation" framing.

## 5. Yamato Takeru: The White Bird
**The story.** Prince **Ousu** is so violent (he tears his own brother apart) that his father the emperor fears him and sends him away to fight. He kills the **Kumaso** chieftains by disguising himself as a woman at their feast, and the dying chief gives him the name **Yamato Takeru**, "the brave of Yamato". He tricks and kills Izumo Takeru with a wooden sword. He is sent at once to subdue the east. His aunt, priestess of Ise, gives him the sword **Kusanagi** and a small bag. On a plain his enemies set the grass on fire around him. He mows down the grass with the sword, lights a counter-fire with the flint from the bag, and escapes. At sea a storm threatens his ships, and his wife **Ototachibana-hime** gives herself to the waves to calm them. Weary and proud, he leaves the sword behind and goes to fight the god of **Mount Ibuki** barehanded, insulting the god in the shape of a white boar. He is struck with sickness, and dies far from home, singing of the land he will not see again. His soul rises as a **great white bird** and flies west.
- **Possible tracklist arc:** Ousu · Feast of the Kumaso · The Wooden Sword · Sent East · Kusanagi · The Burning Plain · Ototachibana (the sacrifice) · Ibuki · Nobono (the dying poems) · The White Bird
- **Why it works:** a classic tragic-hero war epic, violent and doomed. It is well suited to melodic death metal.
- **Sources:** K (Book 2); NS (Book 7, Emperor Keikō), where the account differs.
- **Care:** he is an imperial prince used in nationalist history. Keep the focus on the tragic man, not the conquest.

## 6. The King of the Oni (Shuten-dōji)
**The story.** Under Emperor Ichijō, girls of the capital vanish. The diviner **Abe no Seimei** names the culprit: **Shuten-dōji**, king of the oni, who lives on **Mount Ōe**, drinks blood as sake and eats human flesh. The warrior **Minamoto no Raikō** and his **Shitennō** (the Four Heavenly Kings: Watanabe no Tsuna, Sakata no Kintoki, Urabe no Suetake and Usui no Sadamitsu), with Fujiwara no Hōshō, disguise themselves as mountain monks (*yamabushi*). Gods give them a sake that is poison to demons. They are welcomed into the oni fortress and drink with the demon king, who tells them the story of his life. Then, when he is drugged, they behead him. His severed head flies up and bites at Raikō's helmet.
- **Possible tracklist arc:** The Vanished Daughters · Seimei's Divination · Raikō and the Four Kings · The Mountain Monks' Road · The Gods' Sake · The Iron Palace · The Demon's Confession · The Beheading · The Head That Bites · Return to the Capital
- **Why it works:** it's a heist-and-slaughter tale with feasting oni. Very metal, and full of set pieces.
- **Sources:** OZ (*Shuten-dōji*), Noh play *Ōeyama*.
- **Care:** low. Some versions make the oni sympathetic (outcasts, foreigners), which is worth considering for a twist.

## 7. Nine Tails (Tamamo-no-Mae)
**The story.** A courtesan of impossible beauty and learning, **Tamamo-no-Mae**, becomes the favourite of the retired **Emperor Toba**. He falls mysteriously ill. The court diviner (**Abe no Yasunari** in most versions) reveals her as a **nine-tailed fox** that had earlier ruined kings in India and China. She flees to the plain of **Nasu**, where warriors hunt her down with arrows and swords. Dying, she becomes the **Sesshō-seki**, the "killing stone", which poisons all who touch it, until a monk, **Gennō**, breaks it and frees her spirit. (The real stone at Nasu split in 2022, which is a gift for the album's promotion.)
- **Possible tracklist arc:** The Woman Who Knew Everything · The Emperor's Fever · Light From Her Skin · Unmasked · Nine Tails · The Hunt at Nasu · The Killing Stone · Gennō's Staff · What Was Freed
- **Why it works:** a seductive, sinister atmosphere, a femme-fatale narrator, and a hunt climax.
- **Sources:** OZ (*Tamamo no sōshi*); Noh play *Sesshōseki*.

## 8. Dan-no-ura (The Fall of the Heike)
**The story.** The **Taira** (Heike) clan rules the court, and falls in the Genpei War (1180–1185) against the **Minamoto**. The last battle is at sea, at **Dan-no-ura**, in 1185. As the tide turns and the fleet is lost, the grandmother of the child **Emperor Antoku**, **Nii-no-Ama**, takes the six-year-old in her arms with the sacred sword and leaps into the sea: "There is a capital beneath the waves." The Taira drown by the hundreds. The legends go on. Crabs with the faces of samurai (*Heike-gani*) crawl along the strait. Two centuries of ghost stories follow, among them **Hōichi the Earless**, a blind biwa singer whom the ghosts summon to perform the tale for them. A priest paints sutras over his whole body to hide him from them, but forgets his ears.
- **Possible tracklist arc:** The Bells of Gion (the opening lines on impermanence, a public-domain text) · Pride of the Taira · The Genpei War · Yashima · The Turning Tide · A Capital Beneath the Waves · Dan-no-ura · Heike-gani · Hōichi · Sutra Skin · The Ears
- **Why it works:** an elegy for a whole clan, with a ghost-story coda. The biwa is the natural lead instrument.
- **Sources:** HM (the Kakuichi text); *Kwaidan* (Hearn) for Hōichi.
- **Care:** these are historical events with legend around them. Keep the history right.

## 9. The Three Great Onryō
**The story.** Three real men, wronged in life, became Japan's three great vengeful spirits (*Nihon san dai onryō*):
- **Sugawara no Michizane**, a scholar-minister slandered and exiled to Dazaifu, where he died in 903. Plague, drought and lightning followed. A bolt struck the palace in 930 and killed his accusers. He was pardoned in death and worshipped as **Tenjin**.
- **Taira no Masakado** declared himself the "new emperor" in the east and was killed in 940. His head, displayed in the capital, would not decay. It is said to have flown back east, and his burial mound in Tokyo is still feared and honoured.
- **Emperor Sutoku**, defeated in the Hōgen Rebellion of 1156 and exiled to Sanuki, copied sutras in his own blood and sent them to the capital. When they were refused, he cursed the court and became a great **tengu** (a mountain demon).
- **Possible tracklist arc:** three movements of three to four tracks each (Exile at Dazaifu → The Thunderbolt → Tenjin; The New Emperor → The Head That Flew → The Mound; The Hōgen War → Sutras in Blood → The Tengu King), and a final track, *Appeasement*.
- **Why it works:** an anthology held together by one theme (injustice turned into a curse), with three distinct sounds.
- **Care:** Michizane (Tenjin) is widely worshipped as the god of learning, and Masakado's mound is a place of reverence. Tell the legends without mocking the worship.

## 10. The Bell of Dōjōji (Kiyohime) *(EP)*
**The story.** A young monk, **Anchin**, on pilgrimage, stays at an inn, and **Kiyohime**, the innkeeper's daughter, falls in love with him. He promises to return, and flees instead. She pursues him. When he crosses the **Hidaka River** and the ferryman refuses her, she plunges in and becomes a **serpent**. Anchin hides beneath the great bell of **Dōjōji** temple. The serpent coils around the bell, and her fury burns it red-hot until the monk inside is ash.
- **Arc (6–8 tracks):** The Guest · The Promise · Flight · The River Hidaka · Serpent · The Bell · Ash
- **Why it works:** an obsession story with a horror ending. It suits a compact EP, doom into black metal.
- **Sources:** *Dōjōji engi emaki*; Noh play *Dōjōji*; *Konjaku* variant.

## 11. The Night Parade of One Hundred Demons (Hyakki Yagyō)
**The story.** On certain nights, anyone who walks the streets of Kyoto may meet a procession of **yōkai**. It includes the **tsukumogami** (old tools and objects that come alive after a hundred years), oni and fox spirits, the long-necked rokurokubi, the umbrella ghost, and the snow woman. Anyone who sees the parade dies, unless protected by a sutra.
- **Arc:** an anthology, with a frame track (*The Parade Begins*), one yōkai per track (Tsukumogami, Kasa-obake, Rokurokubi, Yuki-onna, Nurarihyon, Nue, Gashadokuro…), and a finale (*Dawn*).
- **Why it works:** it is flexible, visual (ideal for per-track art) and grotesque. It could run to two volumes.
- **Sources:** the *Hyakki Yagyō Emaki* scrolls (Muromachi period); Toriyama Sekien's catalogues (1776–1784); *Kwaidan*.
- **Care:** low. This is folklore, but keep each creature accurate, because AI mixes up yōkai freely.

## 12. Moon Princess (Kaguya-hime)
**The story.** A bamboo cutter finds a tiny girl shining inside a stalk of bamboo. She grows into **Kaguya-hime**, the most beautiful woman in the land. She sets her five noble suitors impossible tasks: the Buddha's stone bowl, a jewelled branch from the mountain of Hōrai, the robe of the fire-rat, the jewel from a dragon's neck, and the cowry shell of a swallow. All five fail, cheat or die trying. Even the emperor cannot keep her. She is a princess of the **Moon**, and on the night of the full moon a heavenly host comes for her. She leaves the emperor a letter and an elixir of immortality. He has both burned on the highest mountain, closest to Heaven, and the smoke still rises from **Fuji**.
- **Arc:** The Shining Bamboo · Five Suitors (five tracks, one per task) · The Emperor · The Robe of Feathers · The Burning of the Elixir
- **Why it works:** a melancholy, symphonic album about wanting what cannot stay.
- **Sources:** *Taketori Monogatari* (10th century).

## 13. Three Hundred Years (Urashima Tarō) *(EP)*
**The story.** A fisherman saves a turtle, which carries him to **Ryūgū-jō**, the palace of the dragon king under the sea. He lives there in joy with the princess **Otohime**. When he longs for home, she gives him a jewelled box, the **tamatebako**, and tells him never to open it. His village is gone, and three hundred years have passed. He opens the box. White smoke rises, and he becomes an old man in a moment. In the oldest version (the *Tango Fudoki*) he simply withers and is lost. In the later *Otogizōshi* version he turns into a crane, and Otohime into a turtle.
- **Arc (6–8 tracks):** The Turtle · Ryūgū-jō · Otohime · The Seasons of the Sea · The Box · Three Hundred Years · Smoke (Crane)
- **Why it works:** a short, haunting EP about time and loss. It is the "do not open" counterpart to the "do not look" motif.
- **Sources:** *Tango no Kuni Fudoki*; *Man'yōshū* poem; OZ.

## Other Seeds (not yet developed)
- **Tsukuyomi and Uke Mochi:** the moon god kills the food goddess, and the sun swears never to face the moon again, which is why day and night are apart. It would make a strong single track or an interlude on #2.
- **Momotarō and Kintarō:** too much like children's stories as they stand, unless reinterpreted.
- **Yuki-onna and the Kwaidan tales:** a ghost-story EP.
- **Abe no Seimei:** the onmyōji, a diviner and sorcerer, as the narrator of a sorcerer's album.
- **Separate traditions for separate folders:**
  - **Ainu:** Okikurmi, Kotan-kor-kamuy, and the bear-sending ceremony (a living tradition that needs great care).
  - **Ryūkyū:** Amamikyu, the creator goddess, and the *noro* priestesses.
