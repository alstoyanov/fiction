# Japanese Mythology

**Folder:** `mythologies/japanese/`
**Region / culture:** the Japanese archipelago. The myths of the *kami* (the gods and spirits of Shinto) were first written down at the Yamato court in the early 8th century. Around them grows a large body of later legend, Buddhist tales and folklore about yōkai (supernatural creatures and spirits), from the Heian period (794–1185) to the Edo period (1603–1868).
**Living tradition?** **Yes.** Shinto is a living religion, and many figures in these myths are worshipped at active shrines. Amaterasu is enshrined at Ise, Ōkuninushi at Izumo, and Sugawara no Michizane at every Tenmangū shrine. See **Sensitivity** below, and `guides/lyrics.md` §Sensitivity.

## The Mythology in Brief
Japanese myth begins with the *Kamiyo*, the Age of the Gods. Izanagi and Izanami stir the sea from the Floating Bridge of Heaven and give birth to the islands and their gods. Izanami dies giving birth to fire and descends into Yomi, the land of the dead. From Izanagi's purification afterwards come the sun goddess Amaterasu, the moon god Tsukuyomi and the storm god Susanoo. Their quarrels, their exiles and their descendants' descent to earth make up the core cycle.

Beside that court mythology runs a second world. It holds oni (demons or ogres), fox spirits, vengeful ghosts, dragon kings under the sea, and heroes who fight them, told in medieval tales, Noh plays and Edo-period ghost stories. It is full of material for metal: death and pollution, the underworld, impermanence, curses, serpents, and the dead who will not rest.

## Primary Sources
- ***Kojiki*** (*Record of Ancient Matters*, 712). This is the main narrative of the Age of the Gods. We use Basil Hall Chamberlain's translation (1882, public domain) and Gustav Heldt's (2014).
- ***Nihon Shoki*** (*Chronicles of Japan*, 720). This gives parallel and variant versions, often several per episode. We use W. G. Aston's translation (1896, public domain).
- ***Fudoki*** (provincial gazetteers, 8th century). Local myths, including an early version of Urashima from Tango province.
- ***Konjaku Monogatarishū*** (about 1120). Buddhist and secular tales, oni and ghosts.
- ***Heike Monogatari*** (*Tale of the Heike*, 13th–14th century), the war epic of the Genpei War, sung to the biwa.
- ***Otogizōshi*** (Muromachi-period short tales). Shuten-dōji, Urashima Tarō, Tamamo-no-Mae.
- **Yōkai scrolls and Edo collections:** the *Hyakki Yagyō Emaki*, Toriyama Sekien's illustrated yōkai catalogues, and Lafcadio Hearn's *Kwaidan* (1904, public domain).

## Themes That Suit Metal
- **Death and pollution (*kegare*) and purification (*misogi*):** the descent to Yomi, and the boulder between the worlds.
- **Exile and wrath:** Susanoo, and the vengeful ghosts (*onryō*) of Michizane, Masakado and Sutoku.
- **Monster-slaying:** Orochi, the oni king of Ōeyama, the nine-tailed fox.
- **Impermanence (*mujō*):** the fall of the Heike, the drowned child emperor, the sea full of ghosts.
- **Forbidden looking:** Izanagi in Yomi, Toyotama-hime giving birth, the box Urashima opens. The motif recurs throughout Japanese myth.

## Sound Palette
- **Subgenre anchor:** symphonic power metal and epic heavy metal, with folk colour. Gothic symphonic metal for the Yomi and onryō material.
- **Traditional instruments and colours:** taiko ensemble drums, shamisen, koto, shakuhachi, biwa (the instrument the *Heike* was sung to), and the shō and hichiriki of gagaku (court music) for the scenes in Heaven.
- **Vocal approach:** a powerful clean male lead, an operatic female voice for goddesses, and huge choirs. Noh-style chant for the dead and the gods, and spoken word for narration. No harsh vocals.
- **Language(s):** English, with Japanese names and short phrases (chants, invocations, titles). Check every phrase in `lore.md`.

## Visual Palette
- **Colours:** sumi ink black, vermilion, indigo, gold leaf, and the gray-white of fog.
- **Motifs and symbols:** great waves, storm clouds, serpents and dragons, masks (Noh and oni), mirrors, swords, and magatama (curved jewels).
- **Art style:** ukiyo-e woodblock prints, sumi-e ink wash, and Heian yamato-e scroll painting. Keep a consistent style per album.
- **Avoid:**
  - **The Rising Sun flag and militarist imagery.** It carries imperial-war connotations across East Asia.
  - **Real shrines shown as ruined or desecrated,** and imagery of the imperial household.
  - **Mixing in Chinese or Korean motifs** that AI models often blend in, such as Chinese dragons with the wrong number of claws, or hanzi-style pseudo-text.

## Sensitivity
- **Shinto is practised today.** Treat the kami as gods, not as mascots. Retelling the *Kojiki*'s own stories is fine. Inventing insults to them, or staging a mock ritual, is not.
- **The founding myths were used for state ideology before 1945.** The descent of the heavenly grandson and Emperor Jimmu's conquest were taught as history to justify imperial rule. If we use them, we tell them as myth, with no nationalism and no "divine nation" framing.
- **Real people who became gods or ghosts** (Michizane, Masakado, Sutoku, the Heike) were historical figures, and some are still revered. Tell their legends, and keep to the record where the record exists.
- **Ainu and Ryūkyū (Okinawan) mythologies are separate traditions,** not part of Japanese myth. If we cover them, they get their own folders (`ainu/`, `ryukyuan/`).
