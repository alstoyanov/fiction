# Epilogue: The Round

*Friday, March 15, 2109. Kestrel Point, the central plaza, and elsewhere*

---

The first ferry of the season went at 07:40, as it always had.

Ruth sold eleven tickets: four to the fish market across the bay, two to the hospital, one to a man with a crate of seedlings who would not say what they were, and four to schoolchildren, because the school had opened again in February. She wrote the takings in the ledger in the ferry office and took the winter board down from the window, and moved the summer timetable from its corner to the middle of the glass, and this time it was not habit. It was March, and the boat was running.

Then she walked to the end of the street.

The fog had lifted before seven and left the harbour very clear. The stone quay ran out and stopped, the way it had always stopped, and past the end of it there was a drop of two metres into gray water, and a gull on a mooring post, and nothing else. No lamp posts. No tarred boards. Half the street still remembered a pier there, and the other half remembered the cranes, and some, like Ruth, remembered both and expected to for the rest of their lives. But when they looked, they all saw water, and they all said so, and nobody had broadcast anything at them since December to make them say otherwise. That was new. It was enough.

Wren had come with her. She walked past her mother to the very edge of the quay, the place where her front foot had once gone out over nothing, and stood there with her hands in her pockets and looked out at the water. Calmly. As if there were nothing there to look at, which there wasn't.

"It's just water," she said.

"It's just water," said Ruth.

Wren looked a moment longer. Then she turned round and walked back without hurrying, and Ruth let out a breath she had been holding since October.

The book was in her coat. It lived in the church hall by day now and came home with Ruth at night. It had forty-three contributors. Three other towns along the shore kept copies of it in their own keepers' hands, and Kestrel Point kept copies of theirs, and the couriers checked the books against each other and wrote the differences in the margins. Emmet Varley's six entries still stood, marked in red.

At the ferry office she opened it on the counter and wrote the morning's entry.

*15 March 2109. 07:40. Kestrel Point, ferry office and Harbour Street. First sailing of the season: eleven passengers. The end of the quay: water. Seen by: R. Calloway.*

The retired engineer, who came down to watch the first boat out every year, countersigned it in his own slow hand. Wren countersigned under him. Then, after a moment, she took the pen back and wrote in the margin beside the passenger count, in her own hand: *And one crate of seedlings, which did not pay.*

Ruth did not cross it out.

---

At two o'clock, in the capital, people came to the stone.

Nobody had arranged it. Since the Day of Testimony in November they had simply started coming on the fifteenth of every month, to the plain gray stone by the fountain in the central plaza, and standing there for an hour, and reading out a day. There had been two hundred in December. There had been more in every month since. Today, Kira thought, standing at the front with her book held against her coat, there were more than she could count, and she had counted to six hundred before she stopped.

On the west side of the plaza, where a trestle table had stood in December, there was an office now, with the Concordance crest over its door, an open book under two joined hands, and a queue outside it that had not gone away since the fourth of February. People stood in it with their journals in carrier bags, patiently, the way people stood for bread. Kira looked at it once, and then back at the stone.

Old Songs stood beside the stone with one hand on it.

He was still officially dead, and still declining, courteously, every clerk who offered to correct it. He wore the coat the resistance had given him and the scarf Alexei had given him, and his white hair was loose over his collar, and when the readings had finished he did not wait to be asked. He took a breath and sang.

It was "The Names."

Kira had heard it once before, in a safe house at dawn in August 2106, for five of the dead of Ashford. She had never heard it sung for strangers.

"*Who went down to the water?*" sang Old Songs, cracked and thin and absolutely clear across the plaza. "*Name them, and they stay.*"

Then he sang a name.

A woman from one of the towns on this shore. And in the same line, where the song had room for only one, a second name, from the other shore, a man from a Chronos town whose log had stopped on a night in September. *Went down to the water. Name them, and they stay.*

He had taken them from the journals. Kira knew because she had helped him. No single record held all thirty-one. Each nation's listed only its own dead, every one of them a victim of the other side's attacks. The journals had both. The keepers on both shores had written them down as they happened, in kitchens, with witnesses, and the old man had sat at Elena's table for three nights with the copies from twelve towns and found every one.

He sang them two to a verse, one from each shore, and did not say which was which.

"*The record may forget them,*" he sang, "*the song will not forget; name them, and they stay.*"

Kira counted. She could not help it. She counted every name, the way she counted cameras and rods in chandeliers and the steps of stairs, and the counting did not stop her from hearing them. It was the first time she had understood that the two could happen together.

The song went on as long as the dead. Nobody in the plaza moved. A tram came round the corner of the square and slowed and stopped, and the driver opened her window and did not close it. When the last verse had lifted, and hung, and stopped, there was a silence so complete that Kira could hear the fountain.

*Thirty-one.*

Then, before anyone could applaud, Old Songs began again.

A different tune. Short, lifting. Kira knew it in her throat before her mind caught up.

"*One hand writes and one hand checks—*"

And beside him, small and very upright in a borrowed coat, with her right hand closed round the strap of her bag to keep it still, a woman nobody in the plaza knew came in on the second line.

"*One hand writes and one hand checks—*"

Her voice was thin and exact and a little flat. Nobody turned to look at her. There was no reason to. She was an old woman in a plain coat singing with an old man, and her face had never been on any screen in Veridica, and in the record of the country across the lake she had died in a hospital bed on the fourth of January. She was as dead there as he was here. The two of them stood side by side at the stone, one dead on each shore, and sang.

Kira took the third entry.

She came in off-key. She knew it. She had been off-key in the farmhouse kitchen in January too, and she came in now, in front of more people than she could count and a broadcast camera on the steps of the Justice Center, and did not care.

*One hand writes and one hand checks,* she sang, while the thin voice beside her held the light and the old man's went on ahead of them both, failing and mending.

The round held. Three voices, each a line behind the last, each carrying what the others could not.

Then Alexei came in behind her, loud and not quite in tune, grinning. Then Maya, beside him, quick and precise, as if she were timing it. Then a tram driver with her elbow on her open window. Then a boy of about ten at the front, who had been reading out his lunch half an hour ago, and who picked up the words by the second time round and sang them at the top of his voice.

Then a dozen. Then more. Then the crowd.

They did not know the words, and then they did, because the words were only four lines and came round again and again. They did not know the tune, and then they did, because it was the kind of tune that teaches itself. They did not know who had written it. Nobody told them. It went out across the plaza from the stone in rings, voice after voice coming in a line behind the one before, until Kira could no longer hear where it had started, or the old man, or the thin exact voice beside him, or her own.

It lifted at the end of every round like a question. Nobody resolved it. Nobody tried.

Marcus was singing. She saw him through the crowd, a few rows back, with his glasses pushed up on his forehead and his mouth moving, not loudly, in time. He was not looking at her. He was looking at the stone, where two old people who were dead in two nations were singing a song that could not be sung by one voice alone, and a crowd that had never heard of either of them was carrying it for them.

*Kira Osman. March fifteenth, 2109. Fourteen forty. Central plaza, the stone. Voices: uncounted.*

She did not try to count them.

---

At the lakeshore checkpoint the floodlights came on at a quarter to six, though it was not yet dark.

Dmitri waited on the concrete bench under the nearest one, as he had in November. The fog was coming in off the lake, real and cold, the way it came in every evening in March. The barrier was up. Two trucks went through while he sat there, one each way, and nobody stopped either of them, because nobody needed to.

She came out of the checkpoint building alone, tall, in the long dark coat, with her hands in her pockets. He stood.

The insignia on her collar had changed. It had been a captain's in January. It was a major's now.

"Major," said Dmitri.

"Volkov." She stopped a metre from the bench and looked at him, and then out at the fog over the water. Her voice was the voice of a report. "Major Sokolov. Lakeshore checkpoint. Traffic normal."

"I heard the promotion was in February."

"The eleventh."

He counted, because he could not help counting. She had been a captain for as long as he had known her. She had stood her units down at 14:05 on the seventeenth of December. Eight weeks later she was a major. In four years under her he had never seen the service promote anyone faster than it had to.

Sokolov looked at the fog for a moment longer.

"It was very quick," she said quietly.

She did not look at him when she said it. He did not answer. She had noticed; he had noticed; they both knew the other had. Neither of them said what it meant, because neither of them knew, and because a promotion that fast was either a reward or a leash, and there was no way yet to tell which. He filed it. He did not know where it went.

She nodded to him, once, and went back inside. The floodlights turned the fog white.

---

In the Sovereignty of Free Will it was evening, and Isaiah Okonkwo was in a room he should not have been in.

He had not meant to be. He had come to the Interior Ministry to renew the paper that said his asylum was conditional, as he did every three months, and a young clerk in a hurry had shown him down the wrong corridor and into the wrong room and gone to find the right form. The room had a desk and two chairs and a window over the harbour, and on the desk, squared to its edge, a folder with a red border.

He did not open it. He did not need to. It was open.

*HISTOLOGIC COORDINATION: STRATEGIC IMPLICATIONS.*

Under the heading, clipped to the inside cover, was his own column from December, *Decided For Us?*, with a paragraph underlined in pencil. Beneath it were the Introduction, Three, Five, the strip and the Warning, in the SFW's own typeface, each page stamped in the corner with a number that was not a page number. Beneath those, a sheet of paragraphs he could not read upside down at that distance, headed *Assessment*.

He looked at the underlined paragraph. It was the one in which he had written that the Histologic nations appeared to have been built to keep their borders quarrelling, and had asked who gained from the quarrel. He had ended it with a question, as he ended everything. Someone had drawn a line under the question, and in the margin, in a small military hand, had written one word: *Opportunity.*

The door opened. The clerk came back with the right form, and saw where he was sitting, and went very still.

"I'm so sorry," said Isaiah courteously, and rose, and folded his hands. "I believe I've been shown into the wrong room."

That night, in the parlour of the memory palace, among the harmless warm true things, he put the folder on the mantelpiece beside the first byline and the smell of pepper soup, and looked at it for a long time. Then he took a sheet of paper, a real one, and wrote at the top of it the question he could not answer, so that it would exist somewhere other than inside him.

*Who else is reading this, and for what?*

---

It was the fifteenth, so Maya had gone to the prison that morning.

The visiting room was clean and warm and had its one tree in its yard, bare still, though there were buds on it if you looked. Her father was thinner. He was always a little thinner. He asked about her work, and about Nikolai, and listened to the answers with the terrible, precise interest he had always given to everything.

Then he said, "I read it."

She did not ask what. A guard had brought a copy into the prison library in February, and the library had copied it, the way everyone was copying it, and the inmates had passed it along the landings in their own hands. Her father held up his copy for her to see. He had written it out himself, in the neat, small, unhurried hand she remembered from birthday cards.

"The Reply," he said. "Your friends'. I have only one note."

"Go on."

He turned to the blank signing pages at the back and tapped them once with a clean finger.

"No index," said Dr. Reeves. "Clever." He smiled, the kindest smile she had ever seen on anyone. "They'll hate that."

She looked at him for a long moment. He meant it. He meant it as a professional compliment, the kind he had once paid to a well-designed trial, and he was right, and she hated that he was right.

Then, somewhere in the second half hour, as he always did, he told her about the letter opener.

She listened. She did not argue. When he had finished she said, "All right, Dad," and put her hand over his on the table, and he looked down at it with mild surprise, as though it were data.

On the tram back she took out the notebook Nikolai had given her and wrote the visit down. *15 March 2109. 10:40. He read the Reply. "No index. Clever. They'll hate that." Letter opener: again.* And under it, as every month:

*He didn't do it.*

She did not need anyone to countersign that line. She wrote it anyway, so that it would be written.

---

In Chronos, on the ninth floor, Yuki Tanaka began a twelfth notebook.

She had carried a notebook since the spring before last. Eleven were full, and kept where she kept things. She had meant to start the twelfth in her office on the fourth floor, with Morrison at the next desk pretending not to notice. Instead she found that she had climbed the stairs to 9S with it in her bag, at the end of her round, without deciding to.

The ward was quiet. The other patients were in the day room, where the screen showed a Synthesis concert and recorded nothing. The disinfectant smelt as it always smelt, sharp and cold, faintly green, like cut stems.

The bed by the east window was made.

It had been made since the fourth of January. The sheets were tight and white and turned down at the corner in the way the night nurse turned them down, and the locker beside it was empty, and the window looked out over the lake, which was very clear today, all the way across to the other shore. Nobody had been assigned the bed. Dr. Vale had not said why. He did not need to.

Tanaka sat down in the visitor's chair.

Nothing here was recorded. Nothing she did in this room, nothing she said, nothing she wrote. It was the one room in her country where that was true, and she had spent two and a half years treating a patient in it without once asking who the patient was, because that was the wall she had built herself, and it had been a good wall. It had held right up until the moment she took it down.

She opened the notebook and uncapped her pen and wrote, in her small precise hand, in the only form she had ever trusted:

*15 March 2109. 14:30. Ward 9S. Bed by the east window: vacant. Chart closed 4 January 2109, in the director's hand. Patient M.K. Last examined by me: 4 January, afternoon. Tremor, right hand: worse. Lucid. Asked after no one; did not need to. Case:*

She stopped.

She looked at the word for a long time, and at the space after it, and at the lake through the window, and the far shore, where at this hour, in a plaza she could not see, people were singing.

She did not finish the line.

---

The letter came at half past nine that night, by hand.

Kovač brought it herself. She stood at the door of the flat in her dark coat with her hands in her pockets and did not come in. "It came through the intake," she said, "addressed to you, care of the Registrar. Unstamped. I have entered it in the log as received." She pushed her glasses up her nose. "Nobody else has read it, Mr. Chen." And she went back down the stairs before he could thank her.

It was a single card, in ink, in handwriting.

The letters were still a little too far apart, though less than in February, and the capitals leaned forward together, eager, the way they had on the first page of a cloth notebook on a bench at Saltmarsh Crossing. At the top, out of a habit its writer had not yet lost, the date had been written to the second, and the seconds struck through with one neat line.

*My brother wrote to me. First time in eleven years. I'm going to answer.*

It was not signed. It did not need to be.

Marcus stood in the kitchen with it for a long time. Kira had gone to bed an hour ago, with the book on the pillow beside her, open at the page where she had written the afternoon. He could hear her breathing through the door. Somewhere out in the city a tram was going home.

He took out his lists and unfolded them on the table under the window. CHECKED. UNCHECKED. HOPES. He had copied them fresh again in March. The paper was clean at the folds.

At the bottom of UNCHECKED was the entry from the fourteenth of February, in pencil, in his small precise hand.

*Feb 14, 2109. Chronos Gap, Dec 13, 2107, 03:12–03:29. Two hands: Wei's seam; the Archive's key. Holder of the key: Kovač, J. Julian?*

He laid the card beside it. The sloping capitals. *I'm going to answer.* The man on the bench, who had taken the key out unasked, and said there was nothing behind it, and thanked him, and written the thanks into his own log so that it could never be taken back. The key that had countersigned seventeen minutes of four million people's sleep. A brother, writing for the first time in eleven years. Nothing in any of it that could be checked. Nothing in any of it that could be ruled out.

He looked at the question mark.

He had the pencil in his hand. He could have crossed it out. He could have moved the line to CHECKED, or to HOPES, or rubbed it out altogether, the way he had once rubbed out two initials on the twenty-ninth of December because he had wanted, very much, to be wrong. He did none of those things. He looked at the question mark for a long time, the one small curve of it, the point under it, and he left it where it was, because it was the only honest entry he could make.

He went to the window.

The capital lay dark and quiet below, the Spire's light pulsing in its core the way it had pulsed every day of his life. Somewhere beyond it, farther than he could see, was the lake, and the other shore, and a man in a small flat with a window on the water, writing to his brother in a hand he had only just learned. And in both nations, in kitchens and back rooms and church halls and prison libraries, in books that belonged to nobody, people were writing down what they had seen, and checking each other, and disagreeing in the margins in their own hands, and nobody could count them. Not The Judge. Not the Concordance. Not the people who had built the design, forty-five years ago, frightened and careful, in rooms he would never see.

They had thought of everything. They had thought of him. They had built a place for him before he was born, and a chair on a bench, and a word to call him by.

The design could anticipate every reformer. It could not anticipate everyone.
