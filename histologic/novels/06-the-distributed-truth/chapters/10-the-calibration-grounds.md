# Chapter 10: The Calibration Grounds

*Wednesday, November 28, 2108. The Commission's public testimony intake, Veridica Capital; the resistance farmhouse, the border zone; the lakeshore checkpoint*

---

*Location: public intake, ground floor. Time: 08:56. Cameras: two. One above the street door, covering the hall. One on the far wall, angled down at the deposit slot. Arc of the first: the whole hall except the slot's wall. Arc of the second: the slot, the desks, the counter. Overlap: the middle of the floor. No gap.*

Dmitri Volkov stood by the street door with his hands in the pockets of his coat and counted the cameras for the third time since Monday, and got two, as before.

He was there because Elena had put him there. On the night Marcus came back from Saltmarsh Crossing with the fog on his coat, she had listened to the account of the garden relayed over the link, and at the end of it she had said, "Chronos has it now. Every word. Our side doesn't." A pause, in which he had heard her counting something. "Dima. Stay near them."

So he stayed near them. It was not difficult. He had followed people for four years in Border Security, and before that he had followed his brother for three without knowing it. He walked Marcus and Kira to the Commission in the mornings at a distance of twenty metres and walked them home at night. He sat on the highest bench at the public sessions, where he could see every door. He did not tell them that he was there, and they did not ask, which he took to mean that they knew.

The Chronos Administration had a record of a Veridican Commissioner refusing his government's orders, timestamped to the nanosecond, and nobody in the Veridican government yet knew that it existed. That was a condition in which people were sometimes hurt. He did not intend that anyone should be.

At eight forty-nine Kovač had telephoned the fourth floor. At eight fifty-three Kira had come down the stairs with her face very level. Nikolai had come with her, because on Monday night Kira had told him about six fifty-eight, and he had not slept since, and it showed in his hands.

Now Kira was in the small office behind the counter with the Registrar, and the door was open, and Dmitri could see both women from where he stood, and the screen, turned a few degrees towards the second chair.

---

Kovač did it the same way she had done it on Monday. Kira had told him exactly how, so he recognised each step as it came.

The log first. One line, and only one line, on the screen.

*28 NOV 2108. 06:51:40. PUBLIC DEPOSIT (STANDARD). ITEMS: 1. SHEETS: 6. SOURCE: SLOT. DEPOSITOR: ANONYMOUS.*

Then the camera over the slot, from six until the clerk came on at twenty to nine. Dmitri watched it from the door over their shoulders. He watched it the way he had once watched footage in the Border Security office at night: the whole frame first, then the edges, then the object. The hall, empty and gray. The desks. The slot. A tram's shadow across the floor at 07:12. At 06:51:40 nothing moved in the frame, and the steel plate over the chute did not move either.

"The second camera concurs," said Kovač. "Nobody."

"Seven minutes earlier than Monday," said Nikolai, from the doorway. He said it very quietly, to nobody in particular. "Six fifty-eight. Six fifty-one."

Kovač looked at him over her glasses.

"Mr. Volkov," she said. "Nikolai. I am aware of what you do. I am grateful for a great deal of it. You may not have the log." She said it without heat, as the answer to a question he had not yet asked. "I have shown Ms. Osman the entry. The entry is all anyone sees. If I let one careful person in to look, I will never again be able to say that nobody has been in it."

Nikolai looked at his hands. "I would only read it."

"I know," said Kovač. "That is what everyone would only do."

He did not ask again. Dmitri felt it through the bond, faint and near: cold hands that would not get warm. His youngest brother's fear, which never announced itself, only arrived, like weather in a room.

---

Six sheets, in a gray card folder. Kira did not touch them until she had looked at them, and Dmitri, who had come to stand behind her chair, looked with her.

Old paper, gone soft at the edges. Typed, each letter pressed in. Page numbers at the foot, centred: *23* to *28*. In every lower right corner, small: *X*. Every lowercase *e* with the same bite out of its upper curve. At the foot of the even pages, and below the last line of page twenty-eight, the same kind of mark as Monday's, two capitals and a raised dot and a number, but not the same mark.

*JM·08.*

Kira laid one finger beside it, without touching, and said nothing. Dmitri filed it with the others. *CZ·11. YP·02. JM·08.* Two letters, a raised dot, a small number. It was a pattern. It was not yet a reading.

The heading was at the top of page twenty-three.

*PRINCIPLE FIVE: CONTROLLED CONFLICT AND THE CALIBRATION GROUNDS*

"Yesterday," said Kira, still looking at the page, "at four in the afternoon, the talks secretariat published the agenda for round three." She said it in her incident-report voice, as an entry. "Item one. *The claimed strip and the broadcast overlap.* The border is to be argued at Saltmarsh Crossing in twelve days." A pause. "And this morning, the page about borders."

Dmitri filed that too. He did not do anything with it. Two deposits, two mornings, two pieces of paper arriving on the day they would be most useful to someone. He had built cases on less. He had also watched cases built on less fall down. He wrote it in the column where things went to wait.

Kira turned to the first page, and he read it over her shoulder.

> *"External threats unify more reliably than internal messaging. Fact warfare between Histologic nations is not failure but function, so long as it remains a war of facts and not of blood. Border regions of broadcast overlap should be preserved, not resolved: they are where a population's tolerance for contradiction may be measured, and raised."*

His face did nothing. It never did. Nikolai had told him once, years ago, that this was how the brothers knew when it was bad.

---

*Measured.*

He was standing in a small office on the ground floor of a Commission building in the capital, behind a woman's chair, with his hands in his coat pockets. He was also, at the same time and without moving, fourteen years old in a flat on the lakeshore, and his father was coming home from a work course in the interior.

Papa had come home quiet. He had eaten without talking. He had sat in the study with the door open and his hands in his lap, under the green lamp, and he had not laughed at Lyosha's tram conductor, and he had looked at the three of them as if he were trying to remember who they were. The boys had not known what a correction was, then. They had not known that there had been one. They had known only that their father had gone away a man who told them to question things and come back a man who did not ask anything at all.

What he had asked, before he went away, had been about the border. Nikolai had found the notes long afterwards. A systems engineer in the Border Broadcast Division, noticing that the overlap along the lake was not a technical limitation. That it could be prevented. That somebody had chosen not to. *The conflicts serve their purposes.* For that he had been referred, and corrected for twelve weeks, and sent home compliant. And about a year later, when he was quiet and no longer asked questions, he had walked down a street in the border zone into a storm that somebody had made, and he had been Test Subject 2471, and the procurement log had said *lethal threshold confirmed*.

*Where a population's tolerance for contradiction may be measured.*

The page did not know his name. It did not need to. It had been typed in 2066, on a machine with a chipped letter, by someone who had never met Ivan Sergeyevich Volkov and never would. Dmitri counted, because he always counted. 2066 to 2090. Twenty-four years. The page had been lying somewhere for twenty-four years, waiting for his father to walk under it.

*Border regions should be preserved, not resolved.* Preserved. That was the word for what you did to a specimen.

He found that Kira had stopped reading and was sitting very still with her hands flat on the desk. She did not turn round. She did not need to. She had been in Wing C with him. She knew what the page was.

Behind him, in the doorway, Nikolai had stopped breathing, and then started again.

---

Kira signed the pages out under the Registrar's rule. Dmitri watched her read the release slip through to the end before she put her name to it, and watched Kovač sign beneath her in small upright capitals. *AUTHORISED: H. KOVAČ.*

"I am going to the farmhouse," Dmitri said to Kira in the hall. "My brothers should read it. Then there is one other person I would like to show page twenty-eight to."

Kira looked at him. She had hazel eyes that did not move much when she was thinking, and they did not move now.

"Who?"

"My former commanding officer."

She was silent for perhaps three seconds. He counted them. Then she held out the folder.

"Yes," she said.

He put it inside his coat, against his ribs, flat, and buttoned the coat over it. Kovač had come to the inner door and was standing there with her hands in her pockets, watching the folder disappear.

"Mr. Volkov," she said. "Bring them back in the condition they left."

"I will."

"I don't doubt it." She pushed her glasses up her nose. "I say it to everyone. It is the only part of the procedure I enjoy."

---

Nikolai rode out with him. They drove the eastern road in a borrowed car with the heater broken, and neither of them spoke for the first forty minutes, which was not unusual. Twice Dmitri felt his brother's hands go cold, and twice he said nothing, because there was nothing to say that the bond had not already said.

The farmhouse kitchen was warm. The stove had its cracked door, and the glow came through the crack in a thin red line on the floor. Old Songs was standing at it with a wooden spoon, stirring something, and he was humming.

It was the Lakeshore Air. Dmitri knew it before he was through the door, the way he knew his own name. It was the tune their mother had hummed over the washing-up with the fog against the windows, before the interfaces, before any of it. Every phrase climbed at the end and hung there, not coming down, like a question nobody in the room could answer.

Alexei was at the table. He looked up and saw their faces and stopped grinning.

They read it at the kitchen table in order, without discussing the order, the way they had once sat on the parapet: Dmitri first, then Alexei in the middle, then Nikolai on the right. Each of them read all six sheets. Each passed them on without speaking. The old man went on humming behind them, low, the phrase lifting, and did not turn round.

When Nikolai had finished he squared the sheets on the table and put his hands flat on either side of them.

Alexei was on his feet. He always ended up on his feet.

"A depot," he said. "There's a Border Security vehicle depot outside Kestrel, eleven vans, I've seen it. Or the vans themselves. One van. One mast. Tonight. I don't care which." His voice was rising, and his eyes had gone very bright. "They wrote it down, Dima. They wrote him down before he was born. Before *we* were born. And then they waited. I want to burn something. I want to burn something that belongs to them and watch it go."

"Lyosha," said Dmitri.

That was all he said. It was all he had ever needed to say. Alexei stood there breathing, with his hands open, and then sat down again, slowly, as if something had been let out of him that he had not wanted to let go.

Nikolai had not moved.

"Who is sending them?" he said. He said it to the table. "That's what I want. That's all I want. Two deposits. Six fifty-eight. Six fifty-one. No one on camera either time. The slot didn't move. Five pages and then six, on mornings that suited somebody, and the log says the slot took them, and the camera says the slot never moved." He looked at his hands. "She wouldn't let me see the log. She's right not to. I'd have done the same. But I want to know who is sending us our father."

Dmitri felt the cold of his brother's hands in his own, as if he had put them in the lake. He felt Alexei's heat behind his eyes, pressing. He sat between them and held both, which was not a place he had ever been good at standing.

At the stove, Old Songs had stopped stirring. He was still humming. The phrase lifted, and hung, and he let it hang longer than the tune required, and then began it again from the start.

---

The fog came down at four.

It was real fog, lake fog, cold and gray and ordinary, and by the time Dmitri reached the lakeshore checkpoint at dusk the floodlights on their poles had turned it white. It stood around the barrier and the huts in a bright wall, so that the world ended ten metres off on every side and began again, dimly, only where the lights were.

In August, two years ago, three cars had come through this barrier in the dark, and a tall woman in a long coat had raised it and said, into a radio, *no contact*.

He gave his name at the hut. He gave his own name. It was on the Registry again, green, *STATUS: LIVING*, and he found he still disliked saying it to a man in a Border Security uniform. The man looked at him for a long moment, and then went away to use a telephone, and Dmitri stood by the barrier in the white fog and waited.

She came out alone.

He heard her before he saw her, boots on wet concrete, unhurried. Then she was there at the edge of the light: tall, in the long dark coat over the gray uniform, a captain's insignia at the collar, her hands in her pockets. The hard, fine-boned face that had been promoted young and had never been allowed to look it. She stopped two metres from him.

"Volkov," she said.

"Captain."

Behind her, beyond the huts, a bay opened off the lakeshore road, and in it, lit from one side, stood a gray van with its engine idling and a mast folded flat on its roof. Her units were deployed along this shore tonight. He did not need to be told. He had spent four years in her command. He knew what a van in a bay at dusk meant, and what it would mean at nine.

He did not look at it again. He had not come about the van.

There was a bench by the hut, concrete, under the nearest floodlight, wet with the fog. He went to it and did not sit down. He took the gray folder out of his coat and held it out to her.

"Page twenty-eight," he said. "Then the rest."

She looked at the folder. She did not ask what it was. She took her right hand out of her pocket and took it from him, and opened it, and found the last sheet, and read it standing, under the floodlight, with the fog moving slowly through the light around her.

He watched her read. He had watched her read reports for four years. She read the way she did everything, without any movement that was not necessary. When she reached the foot of the page her eyes stopped for a moment on the small typed mark, and moved on.

Then she turned back to the first sheet and read from the beginning. After the second page she sat down on the wet bench, carefully, and went on reading. He stood. He counted the pages as she turned them. Three. Four. Five. Six. It took her eleven minutes. She did not look up once.

When she had finished she closed the folder and held it on her knees and looked at the fog.

"This was written before I was born," she said.

"Yes."

She was quiet for a while. The van idled in its bay. Somewhere out on the lake a buoy bell rang once, and again.

"It describes my orders," she said.

"Yes."

She did not say anything else about it. She did not say what her orders were, and he did not tell her that he knew. She did not ask where the pages had come from, or who had seen them, or what he intended to do with them. He had known she would not. She had never once, in four years, asked him a question whose answer she would then be obliged to report.

He had not come to accuse her, and he did not. She had been twenty in 2090. She had not run the test that killed his father. She ran the programme now, the programme that had run it then, and they both knew that, and neither of them said it. There was a page on her knees that said it better than either of them could.

She stood up. She squared the folder, the edges exactly aligned, with two small movements of her hands, the way she had once squared his reports before she signed them, and gave it back to him.

He put it inside his coat and buttoned it.

She did not go at once. She stood beside the bench with her hands back in her pockets, looking not at him but out into the white, towards the lake, towards the bay where the van stood with its mast folded and its engine running, waiting for nine.

"Then I am an instrument," she said, quietly, to the fog.

Dmitri thought of a triangle of glass cells, and a garden with a rake, and a white chair. He thought of four years in her command, and of every report he had ever written that had gone into the record and come out as a probability. He thought of his father under a green lamp, telling three boys to question things.

"So was I," he said. "It is not a permanent condition."
