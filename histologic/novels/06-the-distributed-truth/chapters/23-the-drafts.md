# Chapter 23: The Drafts

*Monday, January 7, 2109. The Commission's building, Veridica Capital*

---

Wei came to her desk at 08:30, and Maya noted the time because she noted every time, and because she had learned, two winters ago, to note it especially when it was him.

"The Concordance intake," he said. "Scanning, sealing, registration. Every journal that comes in, from every town, at national scale." He set a slim folder on her desk with his right hand. The left hung easy at his side; the scar across its back had gone from pink to white over the year, a thin clean line she could have drawn from memory. "Live by March, if the Act passes on the fourteenth. I'd like you to lead it."

The technicians' floor went on round them. Somebody's kettle clicked off. Across the room two of the new hires were pretending not to listen and doing it badly.

"Lead it," said Maya.

"Lead technician. Your own team, your own budget line. You'll report to me." He smiled, the small, genuine, generous smile that everyone on the floor had learned to want. "You're the best we have at intakes. You always were. You see the shape of a thing before anyone else does." A pause, very slight. "Take your time."

He went back towards the lift, unhurried, and stopped on the way to ask one of the new hires about her mother's operation, by name.

Maya sat and looked at the folder.

*Take your time.* He had said it to her once before, on the ninth floor of the Regional Factorepo, on the twenty-ninth of November 2106, with a drive on her desk that held a carrier signal and a comment on a fridge door. She knew exactly what he had done with the last thing he'd told her to take her time over. She had known since the January after, when she worked out where he had been at 15:20 and where he had not been at 15:32. She had gone on sitting on his floor for two years anyway, because the seven had agreed to hold it, and she had agreed, and written down that she didn't want to.

That wasn't what bothered her. She knew what he was. Something else about it did, something smaller, a stair missing in the dark, and she sat with her hand flat on the folder for a full minute at 08:34 trying to find it, and couldn't.

She opened the folder at 08:35 and went to work, because there was always a next thing.

---

She always read history first.

It was the only habit she had that she was sure was her own and not her father's. He had read results. She read how things got to be results: the log before the finding, the build before the release, the revision history before the document. A thing's history told you what it had been for before anyone decided what to call it.

The Concordance architecture lived in the Technical Director's document store. She had read access to the store because every technician on the floor did; it was where the Commission kept its designs, and Wei believed in openness. *Nothing on this floor should be a secret from the people who build it*, he'd said, on her first day, and she had believed him then, and it was still true. That was the thing about Wei. Almost everything he said was true.

She found the architecture he had presented to the Commission on the eighteenth of December, the day after publication. It was beautiful. She had stood at the back of the session room with the other technicians and watched him present it, and she'd thought it was beautiful then, and it still was. Every registered page scanned, sealed, cross-referenced with every other page that mentioned the same day. Disagreements flagged, "Kestrel-style". Keepers protected. *No journal will ever be lost to a fire or a flood again.* She had watched the room applaud.

She opened the full history.

*Document created: Tue 06 Nov 2108, 08:14. Location: Office of the Technical Director.*

She looked at it.

The sixth of November. She counted back without wanting to. The First Minister had called Marcus up to the Cabinet Office on the evening of the first; the whole floor knew that now, because Marcus had read the orders out on the Justice Center steps on the seventeenth. KINDRED, offered on a Thursday night. And five days later, at fourteen minutes past eight on a Tuesday morning, in the office at the end of this corridor, someone had opened a blank document and begun to design a national index of every journal in the country.

Six weeks before there was anything to answer.

*08:14*, she thought, and something moved in the pit of her stomach, and she didn't know why that either.

She scrolled.

*Revisions: 41.*

She went through them one at a time, as she went through a buffer: from the far end, walking in. Early November, a skeleton: headings, a data model, a line that said *scope TBC*. Mid-November, the sealing layer, the cross-reference engine, the index. Late November, costings. Early December, the keeper-protection clauses, "Kestrel-style" appearing for the first time on the ninth of December, in a comment: *flag disagreements, don't resolve — the lakeshore books do this, people like it.* She thought of the chandlery at Kestrel Point on the news on Christmas Day, a black shell on a harbour street, and her mouth went dry.

The last revision was timestamped *Mon 17 Dec 2108, 11:40*.

Two hours and twenty minutes before publication.

She sat back.

The speech wasn't in the store. The First Minister's speech on the eighteenth, with its crest and its numbered provisions and its budget figure and its *build by many hands*: none of that was here. She didn't need it to be. She had the architecture it announced, finished and signed off at 11:40 on the morning of the day it was supposedly a response to, and the first line of it written in the week the Cabinet Office had asked Marcus Chen to go and make a lonely man love him.

She did not draw the line between those two things. She was a technician. She wrote down times.

---

There was one component she stopped at.

It was in the sealing layer, near the back, where the unglamorous things went. *Section 9, component 9.4: Master Custody and Restoration.* Every registered page, once scanned, would be held as a sealed master in the Concordance's central store. The keeper would get the paper back, stamped. The master would stay. And from the master, a restore path.

*In the event of loss or damage to a registered journal (fire, flood, deterioration, disputed alteration), the journal may be restored in full from the sealed master. Restoration authorised at Technical Director level.*

She read it twice.

*Disputed alteration.* So if a keeper's paper said one thing, and the master said another, the master won, and the paper was restored. Restored meant overwritten. Overwritten from the master, which lived in a store that one office could authorise changes to. *Restorable* meant *editable*. That was all it had ever meant, anywhere she had worked, and she had worked at the Regional Factorepo, where the whole record of the nation could be restored from three backups that turned out not to be three.

She did not think the word *principle*. She thought like a technician: a single point of truth, a single point of write, a single point of failure. Every journal in the country, turned into a copy of a master somebody else kept.

She went back through the history to see when it had arrived. Revision 9, the twenty-first of November. It had been in every draft since.

She took a pencil from the pot on her desk and wrote the component number on the back of her hand, and then, because that was stupid, on a sheet of paper, and folded the paper and put it in her pocket.

---

The sizing revision was the fifteenth.

She almost scrolled past it. It was a dull one: tables, a capacity model, storage estimates for the central store. *Revision 15. Tue 04 Dec 2108, 16:02.* In the header, a single line under *Dependencies*:

*Load estimate: testimony, all sources, including handwritten, national scale. Requested: M. Reeves (Technical). Due: Thu 06 Dec.*

Her own name.

She read the scope line again. *Testimony, all sources, including handwritten, national scale.* She knew it. She knew it word for word. It had been the first line of the work order Wei's office had sent her on the morning of Tuesday the fourth, under the heading *Archive capacity planning*, with a note from Wei's secretary: *nothing urgent, whenever you can this week*. She had assumed it was about the Commission's own archive, the public one, the one that had taken the published pages on the seventeenth. She had thought it was dull. She had done it in two days, because she was good at dull, and sent it back on the Thursday afternoon with a covering note that said *probably an over-estimate, handwritten pages scan heavy*.

Revision 16, Thu 06 Dec, 17:31, had her figures in it. Pasted in whole, her tables, her rounding, the note about handwritten pages scanning heavy, turned into a line item.

She had been building the Concordance since before it had a name.

She got up from her desk at 11:17 and went to the washroom at the end of the floor and stood at the basin with the cold tap running over her wrists until the floor stopped tilting, as it had tilted on the ninth floor of the Factorepo, the stair that wasn't there. Then she dried her hands and went back and sat down and kept reading, because the alternative was to throw the monitor at the wall, and it wasn't her monitor.

---

The Concordance would absorb the Commission's public testimony intake. It said so in the merge section: every deposit ever received at the ground-floor slot, logged, indexed and folded into the central store, so that the national index would begin with a history. That was her job too, now. She had a merge order, signed by Wei's office, that entitled the lead technician to the intake's full records.

She took it downstairs at 14:05.

The public intake was on the ground floor: a counter, writing desks, two cameras, and the steel slot in the wall that anyone could use at any hour. A clerk was stamping forms. An elderly man at one of the writing desks was copying something from one notebook into another with enormous care, his tongue between his teeth. Helena Kovač was standing by the inner door with her hands in the pockets of her dark coat, as if she had been expecting someone and it was just possible it was Maya.

"Ms. Reeves."

"Director. Registrar. Sorry." Maya held out the order. "The Concordance merge. I'm the lead. I need the intake log. All of it."

Kovač took the order and read it. Then she read it again, from the top, all the way down to the signature, while Maya stood there. Nikolai had come down here in November and asked for the same log and been told, quite pleasantly, no. Kovač had kept it closed to everyone since the twenty-sixth. *I would like it to stay the one thing in this building nobody has touched.* Kira had told her that.

Kovač pushed her glasses up her nose.

"Come in," she said.

She printed it herself, on paper, at the machine in her office, standing over it while it ran, and squared the sheets on her desk, and then she sat down beside Maya at the terminal and opened the records underneath, the live ones, and did not leave. She did not look at the screen. She looked at the wall, with her hands folded on the printout, as though she were keeping it from blowing away.

"You'll want the twenty-sixth," she said flatly, "and the twenty-eighth."

---

On the screen they looked like every other deposit.

*Mon 26 Nov 2108, 06:58. Public deposit. Items: 1. Sheets: 5.*

*Wed 28 Nov 2108, 06:51. Public deposit. Items: 1. Sheets: 6.*

The ordinary public-deposit code. The same as the elderly man's at the writing desk would be, when he'd finished copying and pushed his notebook through the slot. The intake desk saw that and nothing else, because that was all the intake desk needed to see.

Maya went underneath.

Every record had a routing layer, the part a desk never saw. It said which device had created the entry and how it had reached the log. For a public deposit, the device was always the same: the slot's own scanner, a sealed unit behind the steel plate that read the item as it dropped, stamped it with a scanner signature and a sequence number, and passed the record up. She pulled a dozen at random from November to check. Scanner signature, sequence number. Scanner signature, sequence number. The elderly man's would look like that.

The twenty-sixth didn't.

No scanner signature. No sequence number. The record had been created at 06:58:14 by a terminal, and the terminal's address sat in a block she knew as well as her own flat: the Ministry network. Not the Commission's public side. Not the intake's own desk. A terminal somewhere on the Ministry network, which ran through every floor of the Ministry and of the buildings it served, this one among them, the Technical Director's office at the end of her own corridor and a hundred other offices besides, had typed a record that said a public deposit had come through the slot, and the slot had never said anything at all.

She checked the twenty-eighth. 06:51:40. The same block. The same absence of a stamp. A different terminal address in the same range, which told her nothing, because addresses in that block were handed out afresh every morning to whoever logged in first.

She could say where the record had been typed. Inside. She could not say which office. And she could not say how five sheets of paper, and then six, had got into a receiving tray in a locked hall at seven in the morning, with a camera on the slot that showed nobody at all.

She sat back.

"It wasn't the slot," she said. "Either time. Somebody made the record from inside. On the Ministry network. The scanner never saw them."

Kovač did not look at the screen. She looked at the wall.

"The tray has two sides," she said quietly.

Maya looked at her. Kovač took her glasses off, and looked at them, and put them back on, and said nothing more, and neither did Maya. There was nothing to say that could be said in a room with a record in it, and this building was nothing but rooms with records in them.

She copied the two routing entries by hand onto the back of the merge order, character by character, and Kovač watched her do it and did not stop her.

---

She got home at 18:40. The flat was cold. Nikolai was away until Wednesday, for reasons he had explained on Saturday in a single careful sentence that explained nothing, and she had let him, because he let her.

She made tea and didn't drink it. Then she got the notebook down from the shelf.

It was the one Nikolai had given her the winter the visits began, a plain gray-covered book of the kind the border-zone shops sold for accounts. On the first page, in his careful hand: *For the fifteenths.* She had written up every visit in it since, on the tram home, the way the journal people wrote things up. Date. Time. What he said. What she said. And under it, every month, the same line.

She turned back to December.

*Sat 15 Dec 2108. 10:40. Visiting room, east block.*

*Thinner. Asked about N. twice. Pleased about N. Told me about the letter opener, second half hour, as always.*

*Told him about the capacity job (the dull one). Every journal in the country, all the handwritten ones too, scanned and indexed, so it could be weighed. How much room it would take.*

*He said: "Give them a trusted index and see what grows. I'd have loved that for Continuity."*

And under it, in her own hand, as always:

*He didn't do it.*

She read it three times.

At the time she'd written it down because she wrote everything down, and because it had been so exactly like him: the bright, pleased, *interested* face across the visiting-room table, a man in a prison chair admiring someone else's apparatus. She had taken it for professional envy. He had always envied good design. She had rolled her eyes and changed the subject to Nikolai, and he had let her, and told her about the letter opener.

*A trusted index.*

He had heard four sentences about a storage estimate and seen the whole thing. From a prison chair, with a seeded murder in his head, three days before anyone in the country had heard the word *Concordance*. He'd seen what it was for. Of course he had. He'd built the last one. You gave people something they trusted, and you put it in front of them, and you watched what grew in it. Continuity had put a face in front of an emptied mind. This put an index in front of a frightened country. *See what grows.*

She had done the sums in two days.

She put the notebook down on the table very carefully, and found that her hands were shaking, and that she was crying, and that she was so angry she could hardly see.

She was angry with Wei, who had asked her to take her time. She was angry with herself for being good at dull, for doing it in two days, for writing *probably an over-estimate* in a helpful little note, as if she were doing somebody a favour. She was angry with her father, who was in a cell for a murder he hadn't committed, and who had looked at her dull job for four sentences and seen straight through it and *admired* it, and said so, and gone back to the letter opener. She was angry that he was the only person in the country who had seen it before she did.

She was angry that he was right. And under the anger, like water under ice, she missed him, the man on the fridge door, and she hated that most of all.

She let herself cry for exactly as long as it took the tea to go cold. Then she wiped her face on her sleeve and sat up.

---

She did not send it.

She thought about it. It would have taken ninety seconds: the timestamps, the component number, the two routing entries, one message to Kira on the secure link Nikolai had built. But a message was a record, and a record could be restored from somewhere, and she had just spent a day reading exactly how.

At 21:10 she got out a pad of plain paper and a pen that wouldn't run out halfway, and copied it all by hand.

Sheet one: the document history. *Created Tue 06 Nov 2108, 08:14, Office of the Technical Director. Revisions: 41. Last: Mon 17 Dec 2108, 11:40.*

Sheet two: component 9.4, word for word. *Restoration authorised at Technical Director level.*

Sheets three and four: revision 15 and revision 16. Her own name in a dependency line. Her own tables, pasted.

Sheet five: the two routing entries, 06:58:14 and 06:51:40, the terminal addresses, the missing scanner stamps. And Kovač's five words, because they were evidence too, of a kind.

Sheet six: the fifteenth of December, 10:40. Her father's sentence, exactly. And under it, because she would not leave it out of anything, ever: *He didn't do it.*

She dated and signed each sheet the same way, *M. Reeves, 7.1.2109*: the name she had kept out of spite, without ever being able to say which of them the spite was for. She folded the six sheets into an envelope, and put the envelope inside her coat against her ribs, the way, Nikolai said, the keeper at Kestrel Point carried hers.

Then she went out into the cold and took the late tram across the city to Kira's, with it there. On paper.
