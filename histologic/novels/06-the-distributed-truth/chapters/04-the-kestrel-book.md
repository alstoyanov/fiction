# Chapter 4: The Kestrel Book

*Wednesday, November 7, 2108. Kestrel Point, the claimed strip*

---

The ferry office had been shut for a week, and it still smelled of summer: diesel and wet rope, and the orange peel the deckhands left in the bin by the door however many times she told them.

Ruth let herself in at eight with the key that had hung on her own hook for nineteen years, and did not turn the heating on, because the heating came out of the season's budget and the season was over. She kept her coat on. The Kestrel Book was in its inside pocket, flat against her ribs, where it had been all night. She took it out and laid it on the counter beside her gloves.

Outside, fog. Real fog, the lake's own, the kind that came in at dusk and went out at ten and left everything beaded. The ferry was laid up at the far end of the quay under its winter covers, and past it, where the stone stopped, there was the drop and the water and nothing else.

The summer timetable was still pinned inside the window. She had pinned it back up herself on the last evening, out of habit. It was faded where the afternoon sun came through the glass, but the numbers were perfectly clear.

*KESTREL POINT — SUMMER SAILINGS. 07:40. 12:10. 17:40. 21:40.*

Under it, on the noticeboard, was the winter card, freight only, which nobody read: weekdays at three, Saturdays at twenty to eleven.

She had end-of-season paperwork to do, and the lost-property ledger, which this year held forty-one umbrellas and a child's shoe. She did not open any of it. She opened the book at the back.

---

She had started keeping the storms in the back pages in June, after the one that came during the school play. It was not an entry, exactly; nobody countersigned it. It was only a list, in her own hand, in the ruled columns she used for everything: date, time of onset, which street, how long. A thing written in columns could be looked at later, and at the time there had been nothing else she could do with her hands.

*12 June. 12:10. Church Lane and the school. 35 min.*

*19 June. 17:40. Harbour Street, lower end. 50 min.*

*3 July. 07:40. Harbour Street, full length. 1 hr 10.*

*3 July. 21:40. Quay and chandlery. 40 min.*

There were sixty-three lines, every one written on the day. She had never read them all through at once. She did it now, slowly, with a pencil held above the page, and then she reached across the counter, unpinned the summer timetable from the window and laid it beside the book.

07:40. 12:10. 17:40. 21:40.

Every storm since June had come at a sailing time. Not near one. At it, to the minute, as near as she could tell with the kitchen clock or the office clock or her own watch. Not every sailing had brought a storm. But no storm had come at any other time.

She sat down on the stool behind the counter.

The last ferry had gone on the thirty-first. The thirtieth, at 21:40, when Wren had walked towards a lamp that was not there. Then there had been nothing left to sail, and the storms had kept the timetable anyway. The second of November, at 07:40, on Church Lane. The fourth, at 12:10, the whole of Harbour Street, Sunday, with the bells just gone.

And none of them had built. She had grown up with the old kind of storm, the kind that came when the two shores' records had quarrelled over something new, and those came on slowly, like a headache, a wrongness that grew over an hour until the streets doubled. These arrived whole, at full strength, as if someone had thrown a switch. And there had been nothing new in either record about Kestrel Point for years. It was the same pier, the same harbour wall, the same few streets, over and over.

Somebody, she thought, is keeping a timetable.

She did not know whose. She let herself think it only as far as that. Then she thought about the van.

Gray. Engine running, lights off. A mast folded flat on its roof. A man in the cab with a flask, not looking at them. She had sold its crews ferry tickets, young men in good boots who paid in exact change and said *thank you, madam*.

She did not know what the mast was for. She did not want to know. She pinned the timetable back in the window, very straight, and put the book in her coat.

---

The old chandlery was halfway up Harbour Street, with its name still painted over the door in letters the salt had nearly taken. The rope-seller kept the front open three mornings a week out of what he called obstinacy. The back room had been the sail loft once. Now it had a long table under the window, eight chairs that did not match, a shelf of lending books, and a paraffin heater the town paid for by a tin on the shelf.

That was where the book lived by day. Ruth brought it in every morning and laid it open on the table under the window, and anyone could come in and read it, and anyone who had seen something could write it in, so long as someone else who had seen it was there to sign.

It was a plain thing to look at: a stout notebook in black cloth, the kind the chandlery had once sold for logbooks, swollen with use and held shut with a rubber band. Inside the front cover, pasted in with flour glue, were the four sheets the courier had left under his saucer in April: the method, in blue ink, in a small hand none of them knew, headed in capitals *HOW TO KEEP A JOURNAL THAT CANNOT BE CHANGED*, and under that, smaller, *after K. Osman*.

*Write in your own hand. Every entry: date, time, place. What happened. Who saw it.*

*Nothing is ever crossed out.*

*Every entry countersigned by a second witness, in their own hand.*

*If you disagree, write your version in the margin, in your own hand, with your initials.*

On the page facing them, the town had signed. Seventeen names, in seventeen hands. The church warden's, in green ink, was the first, because she had been at the next table in the café that day and had picked the pages up from under the saucer before the girl came to clear it.

The rope-seller put his head round the door with his cap on. "Morning, Ruth. You'll have seen the paper."

He held one up. It had come under his door in the night, he said. It had come under everyone's.

---

It was a single sheet, folded once, heavy and very white, the print sharp all the way to the edges. At the top, in capitals:

*THE JOURNALS ARE CONTAMINATION.*

Under that, in a smaller, reasonable type: *Unregistered records spread confusion in a time of war. They are written by the frightened and the misinformed. They are used by our enemies. Only the pure record can protect you.* A crest, a line about something called the League of the Record, and an address in the capital.

She rubbed the corner between finger and thumb, the way she tested the stock for ticket rolls. It was better paper than the ferry office had ever been allowed. Somebody had paid for it.

"All along the street," the rope-seller said. "Three on the church step."

"What did you do with yours?"

"Lit the heater with it. Burns well. I'll say that."

She put the leaflet inside the back cover of the book, where they kept things that had come to the town and ought to be remembered, and wrote on the day's page: *7 Nov 2108. Morning. Harbour Street, all houses; church step. A printed leaflet under every door in the night. Text as attached. Seen by: R. Calloway.* The rope-seller took the pencil from her and signed underneath, and added, in his own crabbed hand, *and I burned mine.*

---

Emmet Varley came in at ten, with the cold on him and a notebook under his arm.

He was a large, soft-spoken man who ran the bait-and-tackle hut on the quay in summer and did not seem to know what to do with himself in winter, and he had been one of the first to sign in April. He wrote more than anyone. He always had something to add. Ruth liked him, the way you like a neighbour who clears your step when it snows, and was slightly tired of him, the way you are tired of anyone who is always so glad to see you.

"The fourth," he said, before he had his gloves off. "I don't think we got it all down."

Nobody had. They had got down the time, 12:10, and the street, and the forty minutes, and the second harbour laid over the first. Emmet had more. He wrote for twenty minutes with the heater ticking, and when he was done it filled a page and a half: the lamps on the pier that was not there, which he said were green, not white; the boats tied up at the second quay, which he said were seven; the bells, which had stopped at twelve and had seemed to him to go on in the second harbour, a little way off, as if across water. He wrote it well, the way he told things in the tackle hut, with the detail you remembered afterwards and could not remember where you had got.

"You were on the office step," he said. "You'll have seen some of it."

She had seen the pier. She had seen the second quay but had not counted the boats. She had seen the lamp posts, not the lamps. So she did what the book told her. She put her initials beside the lines she had seen, *R.C., from ferry office step*, and nothing beside the rest, and in the margin by the lamps she wrote: *Posts only, from where I stood. No light. R.C.*

Emmet read it and nodded as if she had paid him a compliment. "Fair. You'd have had the fog between." He went off down the quay, humming, and she looked at his page and a half a while longer, at how much of it there was.

---

The listening session was at three, in the church hall.

The hall smelled of floor polish and damp hymnbooks. The folding chairs had been set in a horseshoe and the tea urn was muttering on the trestle by the door. Perhaps forty people; Ruth knew all of them. Wren sat on the edge of the stage swinging her feet, because the school had been shut since October and a church hall with strangers in it was the most interesting thing that had happened in a week.

Three had come from the capital: a young man with a recorder, a clerk with a writing pad, and a woman in a plain dark coat who sat at the end of the horseshoe, not the middle, and did not take the coat off.

Ruth knew the face from the broadcasts. The Commission, the Lost Hour, the woman in the crowd at the stone last year with a book held against her chest. She was younger than Ruth had thought, and thinner, her dark hair pinned tight, and when she looked round the hall it was quickly, from one side to the other, the way Ruth counted a queue.

"I'm Kira Osman," she said. "We're here to listen."

And then she did. The young man asked the questions and the clerk wrote, and Commissioner Osman listened. When the fisherman from Church Lane lost his thread halfway through the day his house had been a school, she waited, and did not help him, until he found it again himself.

Ruth read three entries. The school play in June: children in paper crowns who could not find the door of the hall they were standing in. The evacuation of the lower end of Harbour Street in August, when the ferry office had run the boats and Ruth had kept the manifests: *four sailings, fifty-six persons, all accounted for,* countersigned by the skipper.

The third was the thirtieth. She had meant to read something with a bit of comfort in it, but the book fell open at the pier, and she found she had begun.

*Tuesday 30 October 2108. Storm siren 21:40. Third since Sunday. Harbour Street, quay end, at the old pier steps. W. Calloway (14) walked along the quay in the fog towards where the old pier used to be. She said afterwards she could see the boards and a lamp. R. Calloway came down the hill and caught hold of her coat at the edge of the quay. Her front foot was over the water.* And further down: *The pier: I know it is there and I know it was pulled down in 2089. I still know both. Seen by: R. Calloway. Time she got to the edge: 21:50.*

Under it, the engineer's account in his square capitals, and his wife's, each signed. And in the margin beside *21:50*, in the wife's round hand that sloped backwards: *21:52 by my kitchen clock*, and her initials, and the date.

Ruth read the margin too. Both times, both hands. Wren had stopped swinging her feet.

"We wrote both," said Ruth, and cleared her throat. "That's how it's done."

---

Afterwards, while the chairs were being stacked, Commissioner Osman came across the hall and asked if she might see the book.

Ruth hesitated before she gave it to her, and saw the other woman see her hesitate, and not mind.

Osman did not open it at the thirtieth. She opened it at the front. At the method.

She stood very still with the book in both hands, reading the blue ink in the small hand of a courier nobody in Kestrel Point had ever seen again. She read all four pasted sheets, and the seventeen names opposite them, and her face did something brief that Ruth could not name. Not pride. Not sorrow. The look of someone who has heard her own voice come out of a stranger's mouth, in a room where she did not expect it.

"You kept the disagreements," Osman said.

"That's the only part that works," said Ruth.

Osman looked up. For the first time that afternoon she smiled. It was small and quickly gone and it changed her face completely.

"Yes," she said. "It is."

---

Ruth had not meant to show her the back pages. She did it because the hall was nearly empty, and because she had sat alone in the ferry office that morning with sixty-three lines and a timetable and no one to say it to.

"It isn't an entry," she said. "It's just mine."

She laid the back pages flat on the trestle beside the urn, and took the folded timetable from her coat pocket, where she found she had put it without deciding to, and laid that beside them.

Osman read the columns from the top, without skipping. Ruth watched her finger move down the times and stop, for the length of a breath, at the thirtieth. *21:40.* Then it moved on.

"May I copy these?"

She took a small black notebook out of her coat, with a pencil in the spine, and sat on the edge of the stage where Wren had been, and copied all sixty-three lines, date and time and street and duration, in a small, fast, very even hand. It took a quarter of an hour. Wren came and watched her write, and Osman let her.

Then she asked two questions.

"When you say onset. Is it there all at once, or does it come on?"

"All at once. Like a switch. The old ones used to come on."

"And the times. To the minute, or near it?"

"To the minute, as near as I could tell. I'm not a clock." Ruth heard herself. "I'm a ferry office. It's the same thing, most days."

Osman closed the notebook and held it for a moment against her knee, as if weighing it, and looked past Ruth at the window, where the fog was pressing gray against the glass. Ruth waited for her to say what she thought it meant.

"Keep writing it down," said Osman.

That was all. She thanked Ruth, and thanked Wren, gravely, as if Wren were a contributor. She said nothing about Border Security, or vans. Ruth watched her go out into the fog, and was both relieved and let down, and could not have said which more.

---

They walked home the long way, along the quay, because Wren wanted to look at the ferry under its covers.

The lamps along Harbour Street had come on, each in its own soft ball of fog. Wren walked a little ahead with her hands in her pockets, and Ruth did not say anything about the edge of the quay, and did not have to, because Wren stopped well short of it by herself.

The church clock said 17:39.

Ruth looked at her watch, which said the same, and felt her stomach go cold, and reached for Wren's sleeve.

17:40.

It came the way the others had: whole, at once. A pressure behind the eyes, as if someone had laid two thumbs there and leaned. And then a thing she simply knew, the way you know which hand you write with.

Kestrel Point had a charter. It had been granted in 2071 by the Chronos Lakeshore Province, when the town came over to Chronos at its own request. There had been a ceremony in the square outside the church, with a band. She had been nine. She could remember the band, and the charter being read out with its seal, and her mother lifting her up to see.

Nothing in the street moved. It was not aimed at the harbour. It was aimed behind it, at the town's beginning, and it went in quietly, like a new page slipped into an old file.

And beside it, just as clear, just as much hers: no band, no charter, no seal. She had spent the summer she was nine at her grandmother's at the far end of the shore with a broken arm. Kestrel Point had never come over to anyone. It was older than both of them.

Both memories. Both hers. Neither giving way.

Wren had turned round, her face calm in the lamplight, the way it had been on the quay on the thirtieth. "Mum. Was there a band?"

"No," said Ruth. "I don't know. Hold on to me."

It lasted perhaps eight minutes. Then it stopped, the way the new ones stopped, not fading but simply gone, and the harbour was the harbour. Ruth stood on the quay with her hand in the back of Wren's coat, as she had nine days ago, and breathed.

---

Wren went up to bed early without being told, which frightened Ruth more than anything else had that day.

Through the kitchen window she could see the lit windows of the street, and her neighbour came out into the yard in her slippers to put the bin out, and saw Ruth's light, and came to the fence.

"Did you get that one? Twenty to six."

"Yes."

"Funny, isn't it." The neighbour pulled her cardigan round her. "I'd forgotten all about the charter. My dad was in the band. Trombone. It's on the plaque by the church, the date. I must have walked past it a thousand times."

There was no plaque by the church. Ruth had walked past it every day for forty-six years, and there was only the foundation stone with its worn date and a notice about the flower rota. She knew that the way she knew the timetable.

"Night, Ruth," said the neighbour, and went in.

Ruth sat down at the kitchen table and took the book out of her coat. She thought of all the kitchens along Harbour Street tonight, and how many of them now had a band in them, and a plaque. Half, she thought. At least half.

She opened the book at the April pages. The first thing the town had written, the week the courier came, before there had been any storms worth writing down. They had not known what to put in a book like this, so they had put in the town.

*14 April 2108. Church hall. The foundation stone reads 1897, cut at the north-east corner. Read aloud by the warden. Seen by all present.*

*14 April 2108. The old harbour wall, from the ferry office to the chandlery. Rebuilt after the flood of '54. My father worked on it.*

*15 April 2108. The old pier at the end of Harbour Street was taken down in the summer of 2089. I watched the cranes. So did most of the town.*

*15 April 2108. Kestrel Point. Nobody has ever given it to anybody.*

And under each of them, the signatures. The warden's green ink. The rope-seller's crabbed hand. The engineer's, and his wife's. Emmet Varley's big round one. Her own. She counted them without meaning to, running her finger down the margins the way Osman had run hers down the times. Nine different hands, on two April afternoons, writing down a town nobody had yet tried to take from them, because it had not occurred to any of them that anyone would.

No charter. No band. No plaque.

Tomorrow half of Harbour Street would remember something that had never happened, with perfect confidence, and some of them would be angry with her for saying otherwise. She did not remember otherwise. She remembered both.

But the book remembered otherwise, in nine hands, and none of them had crossed anything out.
