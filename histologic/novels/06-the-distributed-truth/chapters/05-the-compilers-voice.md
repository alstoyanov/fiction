# Chapter 5: The Compiler's Voice

*Friday, November 9, 2108. The resistance farmhouse, the border zone*

---

The stove door still had its crack, and he still knew exactly how far to open the damper so that the crack glowed and did not smoke. Two years had not changed that. Very little in the farmhouse kitchen had changed, except the number of people who came through the yard with journals in their coats.

He stood at the stove with a pot of lentils that did not need him, and hummed.

The Lakeshore Air. Low, under the tick of the stove and the sound of the wind in the barn roof across the yard. Every phrase went up at the end, the small lift his grandmother had given it on the pier at home, like a question held out for someone else to answer. He had been holding it out for sixty years. He was seventy now, and still officially dead, and every few months he declined, politely, to be alive again. The record could go on thinking he was furniture.

Outside, the lake beyond the poplars was the colour of an old spoon. There had been storms all week in the towns along the strip, and none here; the farmhouse was too far inland to be worth a broadcast. He was grateful for that, and a little ashamed of being grateful.

He heard the car on the track at three, and knew it by its engine, and did not stop humming.

---

Alexei came in with the cold on his coat and a parcel under his arm wrapped in brown paper, and kicked the door shut behind him with his heel.

"Misha." He put the parcel on the kitchen table and stood back from it with his hands spread, like a conjuror. "Post."

The old man looked at the parcel, and then at Alexei, and lifted an eyebrow. It was a question. Alexei had learned his questions a long time ago, through a meal slot.

"From Tanaka. She met me at the checkpoint this morning, our side, all very proper. Her own papers, her own name, a nice man from Border Security stamping everything twice." Alexei pulled a chair out with his foot and sat in it backwards. "She wrote it down, so I wouldn't get it wrong. She doesn't trust me with messages."

He held out a folded slip of hospital notepaper. The old man took it, wiped his fingers on his apron first.

Tanaka's hand, small and upright and very clear. *From a patient, for Mikhail. She asked that they go home. Y.T.*

"She," said Alexei. "You've got an admirer, Misha. In Chronos." He grinned. "I'd be worried."

The old man looked at him for a long moment over the note. Then he hummed four bars of something that was not the Lakeshore Air. It was an old courting song from the lakeshore, a jaunty, foolish thing about a fisherman who rowed across the water every Sunday to see a girl who would not have him. It went up at the end, like all his tunes, like a question: hopefully.

Alexei laughed so hard he nearly tipped the chair.

---

He unwrapped the parcel at the table, carefully, and smoothed the brown paper flat and folded it and set it aside, because paper was paper.

Two books. Small, the size of a hand, pre-Histologic, bound in cloth that had faded almost out of colour. One green, one a pale soft blue. He knew what they were before he opened them, from the size and the weight and the way the cloth had worn at the corners where thumbs had held them. Border songbooks. There had been one in every house along the shore when he was a boy, on the shelf by the door with the tide tables, and then there had been none, and he had not seen one in forty years.

He opened the green one first.

A school songbook. *Songs of the Lakeshore*, said the title page, *for the Use of Lakeshore Schools*, and inside, the old words and the old tunes set out in the old notation, every page soft with use. Half the tunes he knew. Some he had not thought of in thirty years, and they were waiting exactly where he had left them. He hummed one under his breath, a skipping song his grandmother had used for hanging out washing, and then another, a slow one about the fog, and Alexei sat and listened with his chin on his arms on the chair back and said nothing, which for Alexei was a form of love.

Then he opened the blue one.

It was older: hymns and ferry songs, a church collection. The spine had been mended, neatly, with a strip of new cloth under the old, by someone patient who had cared about the book. He turned it in his hands, reading the object the way Kerr had taught him to, the way he had read ten thousand objects at the Archive before he read a single word of them: weight, wear, repairs, what was original and what was not.

The back endpaper had been lifted and glued down again.

It was very well done. Anyone else would have missed it. But the paper at the edge lay a hair's breadth proud of the board, and the glue had dried a little glossier than the old glue on the front, and when he laid his thumb flat against the inside of the back cover and pressed, very gently, he felt something under it that was not board. A flatness. A thing with edges.

He stopped humming. He did not mean to. He looked at Alexei.

Alexei had stopped grinning.

---

He did it with the thin blade of the butter knife, warmed first against the stove door, sliding it under the endpaper along the glued edge a centimetre at a time. It took a long while. Alexei came round the table and stood at his shoulder and did not offer to help.

Under the endpaper, folded in thirds to fit the board, was typescript.

He lifted it out with the tips of his fingers and laid it on the brown paper and unfolded it.

Three sheets. Good heavy paper, cream going to ivory at the edges, yellowed a little more at the folds. Typed, double-spaced, by a machine and not a printer. At the foot of each sheet, centred, a page number: *2*. *3*. *4*.

An archivist reads the object before the words. He did it now without deciding to.

He looked at the lower right-hand corner of each page, where the photograph of page thirty had carried its small typed numeral, *XI*. There was nothing there. He looked at the foot of page four, where page thirty had carried a short typed mark, two letters and a raised dot and a number. There was nothing there either. The text simply ended.

And then he looked at the letters, and found what he had been afraid of.

A small bite out of the top of every lowercase *e*. The same nick. The same machine. He had stared at it on the photograph of page thirty until he could have drawn it. It was here in every line, the same tiny flaw in the same place, like a fingerprint pressed again and again into wax.

He sat down.

---

He read it twice.

The first time he read it quickly, the way he had read the gray journal in the reading room at twenty-five, because he could not help it. The second time he read it slowly, with one finger moving under the lines, the way he read things he meant to keep.

It began plainly.

*This document assembles principles from seventeen contributors…*

Seventeen. He stopped there, the second time, and let his finger rest on the word. Not one voice, then, as he had half believed for two years. Not one calm, reasonable mind writing essays in a gray journal. Seventeen. And a document that *assembled* them. Somebody had gathered them in and fitted them together, and written this at the front to say what they were for.

It went on, across three pages, in the same even type. But it was the first paragraph that held him. It ended on a sentence that stood a little apart from the rest, the way a text sets the thing it most wants you to keep, and he read it the second time very slowly indeed.

*…Truth, we have learned, cannot be decided by a public. It must be decided for one, and the public must be allowed to believe it decided.*

A design. Not a drift, not an accident of history. A design, with a front page.

He had begun to hum again without noticing, at the start of the second reading, very low, the way he did when he was reading. The Lakeshore Air. He was at the third phrase, the one that climbed, *who will remember the name of the boat*, when he came for the second time to the end of that first paragraph.

And the humming stopped. In the middle of the phrase, at the exact place where the tune began to lift.

Beside him, Alexei went still.

It was the stillness the brothers had, all at once, like a hand closing. The old man felt it without looking. In Wing C, silence from the man with the trolley had meant only one thing: *they're watching*. Alexei had learned it through a slot in a door. Two years in a farmhouse had not unlearned it. His eyes went to the window, to the yard, to the door.

There was nobody in the yard. There was nobody watching. The old man did not look up to tell him so. He was not in the kitchen any more.

---

It was not the words.

The words were cold and clever, and he had read colder and cleverer in the gray journal and carried them for forty years. It was the way the argument turned.

Every paragraph did the same thing. It raised the objection first. It stated it fairly, better than the objector would have stated it, and granted it: yes, a public has always been trusted to decide; yes, that is the tradition; yes, there is a dignity in it. And then, having given the point away with both hands, it turned, gently, on the very thing it had conceded, and used it. It won by giving up the first point. Every time.

He knew that turn. He had lost to it every Thursday for two years.

---

The reading room of the Historical Archive, 2063. Dust and floor polish, and Kerr's eleven o'clock apple, peeled in one curl with the pocketknife, the gloves laid beside it on the desk. The lamp with the green shade on his own desk by the periodicals trolley, and the drawer that stuck. The gray journal on the trolley in its plain cover, catalogued in his own hand: *Periodicals, Governance, 114.*

And on Thursdays, a young woman from the systems department at the university, a theorist, with ink on her fingers. She read the gray journal the moment it came in, closely, slowly, making notes, the way you read something you mean to build on. And she argued with him. About poetry, mostly, across the reading-room table, in whispers, while Kerr at the next desk pretended not to hear and turned the pages of a catalogue he was not reading.

*Rhyme is a compression algorithm,* she had said, the first Thursday. *It lets you carry more in less. That's all it is.*

*It's a kind of prayer,* he had said.

He remembered her looking at him across the table then, with her pen held still. And then she had said: *Yes. All right. Rhyme is a prayer.* Giving it to him, just like that, when he had been ready to fight for it for an hour. *And a prayer is a compression algorithm. The oldest one we have.* And he had sat there with his argument gone out from under him, because she had taken the thing he loved and agreed with it, and then shown him what it was for.

She had done it every Thursday. She conceded the first point, always, with a small dry courtesy, and then she won. He had lost to her for two years and looked forward to it all week.

He had liked her very much. He had never once wondered what she wanted with the gray journal.

And after the gray journal went into a box, she had stopped coming on Thursdays, and he had stopped expecting her, and then there had been Kerr, and then there had been his own first correction, and then there had been forty years. He had kept her in the tunes with everything else. He had never, in all that time, let himself think her name, in case a hood on some long afternoon heard him thinking it.

He had wondered, sometimes, on the bad nights, where she had gone.

---

"Elise," he said.

He said it aloud. His voice came out rough, the way it did when he had not used it for days, and the name was strange in the kitchen, like a coin from another country found in a coat pocket.

Alexei let his breath out slowly. He had not moved from the old man's shoulder. When he spoke it was quietly, as if something in the room might break.

"Who's Elise?"

The old man looked at the pages for a while longer. The stove ticked. Across the yard the barn door banged once in the wind and was still.

"Someone I argued with on Thursdays," he said.

Alexei waited, in case there was more. There was not. After a moment he put a hand on the old man's shoulder, briefly, the way he had in the safe house two years ago on the night of the names, and took it away again, and went to see to the lentils, which had begun to catch.

---

At seven, when the fog had come up off the lake and the yard light made a white room of it outside the window, Alexei took the link into the back room and called Chronos.

The old man listened through the open door. He had spent forty years listening through doors.

"Doctor. It's Alexei Volkov. Yes. They arrived. He's very grateful." A pause. "No. No, he's well. He's fine." Another. "Doctor, can I ask. The books. Where did they come from? Who gave them to you?"

The link was bad, as it always was across the water, and Tanaka's voice came through it small and precise, and he could not hear the words. He could hear the shape of them. He had heard Tanaka's voice do that in Wing C, when a guard asked her about a patient: not colder, only smoother and harder, like a wall that has been planed.

"A patient," Alexei repeated. "Right. No, I understand. Of course." A long pause. "No, you're right. I'm sorry I asked."

He came back into the kitchen with the link in his hand and sat down opposite the old man and spread his hands.

"'A patient.' That's all. She's sorry, but that's all she can say. Confidentiality." He shook his head, half admiring. "She'd have said it to Elena at gunpoint."

The old man nodded. He did not ask anything. He found that there was a thought standing just outside the door of his mind, in the fog, waiting to be let in, and he did not let it in. He did not look at it. He was seventy years old and had learned a long time ago what it cost to hope for a thing before it was written down, and he was not going to begin again tonight, on nothing, on a word a careful doctor used for everybody.

A patient. Tanaka had hundreds.

---

Later he gathered the three pages to put them back in their folds, and noticed the smell.

He had been handling them all afternoon and not noticed it, because it was faint and because the kitchen smelled of lentils and woodsmoke. Now, holding the pages close to fold them, he did. They did not smell of old paper. Old paper smelled of dust and vanilla and damp, of the reading room. These smelled clean. Sharp and cold, and faintly green, like cut stems. A hospital smell.

He held one out to Alexei without a word.

Alexei sniffed it and shrugged. "It's a hospital, Misha. All hospitals smell like that."

The old man was not sure that was true.

He had spent forty years in the kind of building that smelled of disinfectant. He had pushed a trolley down its corridors, and mopped its floors, and lain on its white chairs under its hoods. He knew the smell of Ashford's general wards, and of Wing C, and of the facilities before Ashford, each one different. This was none of them. It was sharper. Greener. Somebody, somewhere, had chosen it.

But he had never been inside a Chronos hospital, and there was no way to know what they used there, or in one ward or another, and the only person who could have told him was on the far side of the water and had just said *a patient* in a voice like a planed wall.

He folded the pages along their old creases and said nothing.

---

Elena came in at nine with the fog on her shoulders and her boots in her hand, from the barn, where she had been counting fuel.

She saw the pages on the table and stopped. She looked at them the way she looked at everything, from left to right, like a soldier checking a patrol. Three sheets. Then the two songbooks. Then the butter knife, and the lifted endpaper, and the old man. Then Alexei.

"Tell me," she said.

Alexei told her. He told it in order, for once, because it was Elena. Tanaka at the checkpoint; the note; the binding; the pages; *a patient*. He did not tell her about the name. The old man noticed that, and was grateful. Elena listened without interrupting, and then picked up the three pages and read the first paragraph standing, and put them down again very squarely on the table's edge.

"Seventeen," she said.

Nobody answered.

"This goes to Kira," said Elena. "Tomorrow. First thing. Alexei takes it."

The old man put his hand flat on the pages. He did not press. He only laid it there, the way you lay a hand on the head of a child to keep it still for a moment while you look at it. Then he lifted one finger.

One night.

Elena looked at the finger, and at his face, for a long moment. He did not know what she saw in it. She had read his file, as she read everyone's. She knew about the Archive, and the gray journal, and Kerr. She did not know about Thursdays. Nobody alive knew about Thursdays.

"One night," said Elena. "Alexei leaves at eight." And she took her boots and went up.

---

Late, when the house was asleep, he sat alone at the kitchen table with the stove glowing through its crack.

The three pages lay in front of him in their folds. He did not open them again. He did not need to. He had them now, the way he had everything: the turn of the argument, the concession, the cold clean sentence at the end of the first paragraph. He could have hummed them.

He did not hum.

He had kept everything he loved in tunes for forty years. Anya, and Katya at three singing the ferry verse wrong. Kerr with the apple. A young woman's inky fingers. He had kept them in tunes because a tune was rote, and the machines skimmed rote like furniture, and a thing that was only sung could not be found by anyone who went looking. A thing written down could be found. That was the first law of every archive, and of every correction room he had ever sat in, and it was why, in forty years, he had never once written down a name he loved.

He had told them all to write it down. In a safe house, two winters ago, he had told them: *Write it down. Many hands.* He had meant it. He had not meant himself.

For forty years he had carried one question for her, and it had been *where did you go*. It had never been the right one. He could feel the right one now, turning over under his breastbone, heavy and patient, and he did not let himself ask it yet, even here, even alone. It would keep. He had kept worse.

He got up and found a pencil in the dresser drawer, and a sheet of paper from Elena's pad, the one she carried and never wrote in, and sat down again.

He held the pencil for a long time.

Then he wrote, slowly, in the hand the Archive had taught him at twenty-four: upright, even, every letter formed the way a catalogue card required, so that anyone who came after could read it.

*Elise Vance.*

The pencil was very loud in the quiet kitchen. When he had finished he laid it down beside the paper, parallel, the way Kerr had laid down his knife.

Then he sat with his hands flat on the table on either side of the name, and looked at it, for a long time, while the stove ticked down and the fog pressed white against the window, and nobody in the world but him knew what it was.
