# Chapter 13: The Table of Contents

*Wednesday, December 5, 2108. The resistance farmhouse, the border zone*

---

He waited for the light.

Alexei had brought the book in last night after ten, out of the fog, with the cold coming off his coat in the warm kitchen like breath. He had put it on the table and stood back from it with his hands in his pockets, grinning, the way he grinned at anything that came from across the lake. "Your admirer again, Misha. Same hands, same bag, same checkpoint. The doctor says, *for the old gentleman who sings on your broadcasts*." He had tilted his head. "I asked her. Same patient. Same answer."

The old man had nodded, and not touched it, and gone on drying the plates.

In November he had opened the blue one at once, the afternoon it came, with Alexei at his shoulder and the fog at the window, and found the voice of someone he had not heard for forty-five years. He did not want to do that again in a hurry. Whatever was in this one, he wanted to see it plainly, in the gray of a winter morning, with the kettle on and the yard full of ordinary things.

So he waited. At seven the window went from black to the colour of slate. At half past, the poplars beyond the barn came out of the fog one by one, like people arriving late to a meeting. He filled the kettle and set it on the stove, and opened the damper the width of two fingers, so that the crack in the stove door glowed and did not smoke, and sat down.

It was a small book. Smaller than the green one or the blue: a pocket collection of lakeshore hymns, the kind a congregation along the shore would once have kept in the rack on the back of every pew, printed before anyone had thought of printing facts. The cloth had been dark red and was now the brown of old brick. He knew most of the hymns in it. His grandmother had sung two of them over the washing, not because she believed them, but because they were good to wash to.

He turned it in his hands and looked at the spine.

It had been re-glued. Neatly, patiently, by someone who knew how. The old glue had been scraped out and new glue laid in along the full length, thin and even, and the cloth pressed back so well that only an archivist would have seen the line where it had been lifted. The same hand, he thought, that had mended the green book and the blue. He had seen enough mending in forty years of archives and prisons to know a hand by its mending.

He took the small kitchen knife from the drawer, the one with the worn wooden handle that Elena used for onions, and slid the point under the edge of the cloth at the head of the spine, and worked it down.

---

It was folded once, lengthwise, and laid along the inside of the spine, where the back of the sewn pages meets the cloth. A strip of paper no wider than his finger. He drew it out with two fingers and laid it on the table and unfolded it.

The kettle began to tick.

It was a strip torn from a longer page, and torn along its length, top to bottom, by someone who had wanted to keep one edge and not the other. The tear was soft and uneven; it had been done by hand, slowly, without a rule. What was left was the right-hand margin of a typed page, and on it, the ends of three lines.

> `IV …… 18`
>
> `V …… 23`
>
> `VI …… 29`

Nothing else.

He sat and looked at it for a long time.

He had catalogued, in his life, more contents pages than he could count. The first thing you looked at in a book, after the spine and the title page, was the contents, because the contents told you what the book thought it was. The column on the left where the headings went, the dots running across like a road to the right-hand margin, the numbers waiting at the end, patient, telling you where to turn. He could have drawn one blind.

This was the right-hand side of one. The dots, the numerals, the page numbers. And where the words should have been, on the left, where the headings were and the book said what each part of itself was called, there was a torn edge and nothing.

He put the tip of his finger on the paper beside the tear, not on it, and followed it down. Someone had torn off the headings and sent him the numbers.

He looked at the type. It was the same machine. He was nearly sure of it and could not prove it, because there was not a single lowercase letter on the strip, not one *e* for him to look at for the bite out of its curve. Capitals and numerals and dots. Whoever had torn it had torn it so that the only words left were numbers. But the paper was the paper he had held in November for one night before it went to Kira: the same weight, the same cream going to ivory at the edge, the same faint, clean, sharp smell under the smell of old glue. He lifted it to his face and breathed it, and did not follow the thought, and put it down.

The kettle boiled. He got up and moved it off the heat, and stood at the stove with his back to the table, and began to hum.

It was the Lakeshore Air. The phrase went up at the end, as it always did, and hung there.

---

Elena came in from the barn at eight, counted the room, counted the strip on the table without touching it, and went to the terminal.

"Osman," she said into it. "You'll want to come out." A pause. "No. Paper." A pause. "Bring him."

Marcus and Kira drove out in the afternoon, with Dmitri. Elena had put Dmitri on them in November, after the garden, the way she put a man on a gate. He came in first, and stood by the door with his back to the wall, and counted the windows. Kira came in behind him with a satchel. Marcus came last, thin and tired in his good Commission coat, with his glasses pushed up on his forehead and the look he had had since the winter of the Doctrine, of a man carrying a list he could not put down.

Kira did not take her coat off. She stood at the end of the table and looked at the strip for perhaps ten seconds without moving, and then she opened the satchel and began to lay things out, in rows, the way she laid out everything.

The photograph of page thirty, small and curled from two years in a coat pocket. The three typed pages from the blue book, which had gone to her in November and which she had copied by hand into her journal, so that they existed twice. Photographs of the pages from the Commission's intake: five sheets, and then six, with a little typed *X* in each lower corner and the same bite out of every *e*. She put them in order. She put the strip down among them, very carefully, with a clean sheet under it, and sat.

The old man watched from the stove. When Kira laid things out, you let her; she was building something, and she would tell you what it was when it stood up.

"Three," Kira said. She put one finger on the last of the five sheets. "Pages thirteen to seventeen. It stops at seventeen." She moved her finger to the strip. "Four begins at eighteen."

Nobody said anything.

"Five." The six sheets. "The pages that came in on the twenty-eighth run twenty-three to twenty-eight." The strip. "Five begins at twenty-three." She said it the way she would have entered a fault in a log: level, and in order, and with nothing added. "The strip is genuine."

"And six," said Marcus.

"Six begins at twenty-nine." Kira's finger went to the photograph, and stopped on the numeral at the foot of the page, and did not move. "Singh's page is thirty."

The old man had known it since half past seven. He had known it the moment he saw the last line, before the kettle boiled. He watched them know it now, one at a time, the way you watch lamps come on in a street.

Dmitri, by the door, did not move at all. Elena, at the end of the table, unfolded her arms and folded them again. Alexei sat down on the bench against the wall.

Marcus picked up the photograph and held it to the window. He did not need to read it. They all knew it. It was the one sentence in the world, the old man thought, that every person in this kitchen could have recited in the dark.

*When the record fails, the people will believe whoever restores it. Arrange to be the one who restores it.*

---

They talked for a long time. The old man listened at the stove with half his mind, the way he had listened to everything in Wing C, through slots and doors.

It was Kira who laid it out. Page thirty had never been a stray page. It was part of something with a contents page. It belonged to a principle that began on page twenty-nine, which was the sixth of at least six, and the first sentence of the blue book had told them what the principles were: *assembled*. *Seventeen contributors*. A design.

And the sixth principle said: *when the record fails*.

The Lost Hour had failed the record. And afterwards, the Doctrine had restored it: trusted testimony, witnesses on steps, the dead declared living. And the Doctrine had needed people to carry it out. It had needed a Commission. It had needed faces that the country trusted, because they had been in Wing C and come back, and it had found them.

"The Commission restores it," said Marcus quietly. "Every day. That's what we're for." He put the photograph down. "It's us."

Kira did not look up. "It's the role," she said. "It's not the people."

"Does the page know the difference?"

She did not answer that.

Marcus took off his glasses, and rubbed his eyes, and put them back on crooked. "XI came out of the Ministry's own correspondence," he said. "That's all I know about who's holding that copy. That's all anyone knows."

He said it and stopped. Nobody went further. There was nowhere further to go: a copy of something had come out of a ministry's letters, and a ministry was a building with a great many people in it, and a page did not say which of them had kept it. The old man watched them come to the edge of that, one by one, and find nothing to stand on, and step back.

Kira had gone back to the numbers.

"Four runs eighteen to twenty-two," she said. "Five pages." She was quiet for a moment, counting them, though there was nothing to count. "Five pages nobody's seen. Under a title nobody knows."

She laid her hand flat on the table, next to the strip, where the torn-off headings should have been. The old man looked at her hand, and at the white paper under it, and thought of a shelf in a reading room where the numbers ran 113, 115, and the fold between them was so clean that the record could not feel it.

---

The screen in the corner of the kitchen came on at six for the evening broadcast. Elena kept it on for the news from the talks, and turned the sound up when the talks came on, and down again when Veridica told them how the war was going.

Tonight it was the talks. The third round opened on Monday, and the Synthesis secretariat had given an interview to set the table for it, as the Synthesis liked to do. And there she was: Professor Park Ji-won, the mediator, small and white-haired, in a knitted jacket, with a cup of tea in front of her that she did not drink and a warm, unhurried voice that the old man had been listening to, one broadcast at a time, since the middle of November.

He had heard her first in a round-up of the talks in November, peeling potatoes at the sink. She had been telling the interviewer that she had once been a young scholar in Veridica's capital. *I used to read in the old capital Archive, you know. Perhaps in the sixties. Perhaps a little later.* He had put the potato down and the knife on top of it, very gently, and stood with his wet hands on the edge of the sink.

The same city. The same room, perhaps. The right age, near enough.

He had said nothing to anyone. It was the kind of thing an old man thinks at a sink. But he had listened to her every time since. He listened now, from the stove, and then he turned the heat down under the pot, and came and sat on the end of the bench under the window with his hands on his knees, and closed his eyes.

He was not listening to what she said. What she said was pleasant and reasonable and about the claimed strip. He was listening to how she said it. That was how he had known the blue book. Not by any word in it: by the way the argument turned, the way every paragraph gave you the first point, freely, as a gift, and then took the whole field with it. He had heard that turn every Thursday for two years. He would have known it through a wall.

The interviewer asked Professor Park whether the talks could succeed. She said that she believed they could. Then she said that of course there were a great many difficulties, and that perhaps success might mean something different from what either side expected, and that perhaps, in the end, it was not for her to say. The interviewer asked whether Chronos's position on the Gap was reasonable. She said that it was very understandable. She said that one could see why they felt as they did. She said that perhaps Veridica's position was understandable too, in its way, and that perhaps one ought to allow for the possibility that both were partly right.

She agreed. Then she qualified. Then she agreed a little more, and qualified that.

She did not turn it. She never once turned it.

He listened to the end of the interview with his eyes shut. Then he opened them.

"No," he said.

Everyone in the kitchen looked up. Alexei, halfway through a sentence to Dmitri, stopped. Kira's pencil stopped. It was the first word the old man had said all day, and in this house that was noticed.

He looked at the screen, where the small white-haired woman was smiling at the interviewer and saying *perhaps* again.

"Elise never said *perhaps* in her life."

---

Nobody asked him anything. They had learned that when he said a thing, you let it stand, and if there was more it would come in a tune. He went back to the stove and let the kitchen talk itself back up around him, and did not hear it.

He heard, instead, a reading room in 2063. The dust and the floor polish. The green-shaded lamp at his own desk by the periodicals trolley. Thursday afternoon, and the door, and a young woman from the systems department coming in with ink on her fingers and her coat buttoned wrong because she had been thinking about something else on the stairs. She read the gray journal the day it came in, closely, slowly, making notes, and then she put it down and argued with him.

She never said *perhaps*. She never said *it depends*, or *one might*, or *in a sense*. She said *yes*. That was how she won. He would say that rhyme was a kind of prayer, and she would say, *Yes. It is. And a prayer is a compression algorithm*, and he would open his mouth to answer and find that she had taken his own ground from under him and was standing on it. She gave you the first point every time. She gave it so completely, so generously, that you did not notice until too late that she had given you the one point she did not need, and kept the rest. He had lost to her across that table every Thursday for two years, in whispers, while Kerr at his desk by the window pretended not to hear, and at eleven took his gloves off and peeled his apple in one long curl with his pocketknife and ate it slice by slice, and put the gloves back on.

A woman who said *perhaps* was a woman who did not want to win. Elise had always wanted to win. It was the most honest thing about her.

The professor on the screen was a kind, clever, courteous woman who had read in an Archive once. She was not Elise. He had known it from the first *perhaps*, if he was honest, and had not let himself hear it, because for three weeks it had been pleasant to think that the voice in the blue book belonged to someone he could see on a screen, in a knitted jacket, with tea. Someone alive, and well, and nearby.

He let it go. It went easily, the way a thing goes that you were only holding in one hand.

It left a space behind it. He did not look at the space.

---

Later, when the pot was off the heat and the others had gone through to the other room with the papers and the lamp, he stood at the stove alone and began to hum something he had never hummed before.

He did not decide to. It came the way the tunes had always come to him, under his breath, out of his hands as much as his head, while they were doing something else. It was short. Four phrases. It began low and went up by steps, and at the end it lifted, as all his tunes lifted, like a question left open for someone else.

He stopped.

It was not right. He knew at once that it was not right, and not how. He stood and listened to the kitchen, to the stove ticking and the lamp hissing faintly and the voices through the wall, and hummed it again from the beginning. Low, and up by steps, and up, and at the third phrase, where the tune turned towards its end, it went thin. It went thin the way a rope goes thin when there is no weight on the other end of it. The phrase was reaching for something, and there was nothing there to take it.

He stopped again.

It needed another voice. That was what was wrong. The tune was not finished because it could not be finished by one mouth. At the third phrase a second voice had to come in under it, starting the tune again from the beginning while the first went on, so that the two held each other up; and a third after that; and then the question at the end would not be left open, because someone would already be singing the answer.

He had spent forty years making tunes that one man could carry alone, through a meal slot, where there was nobody to sing with. It was the only kind he knew how to make.

He started it a third time. Low, and up by steps, and up, and at the third phrase he stopped, and stood with his head a little on one side, listening to the place where the second voice should have been. There was nothing in it but the stove.

Behind him, in the doorway to the other room, someone had been standing since the first time. He knew without turning that it was Kira; she had come back for a pencil, and had not picked it up. She stood very still, the way she had once stood at the glass of 7-K, counting. She had heard him stop in the same place three times. She did not come in, and she did not go away.

---

It was nearly midnight when Marcus came back into the kitchen.

The others had gone to bed, or to the barn, or to wherever Dmitri went at night to stand with his back to a wall. The lamp was low. The strip lay on the table on its clean sheet beside the photograph of page thirty, the numbers on one and the sentence on the other, and Marcus sat down in front of them in his coat and looked at them for a long time without touching either.

The old man was at the stove, banking it for the night. He had his back to the room.

"We were always going to be the restorers," said Marcus.

He said it quietly, not to anyone, the way a man reads out an entry he has just written and does not like. The old man heard in it the whole of the last two years: the steps of the Justice Center, the oath, the Commission, the dead brought back to life by testimony, a nation grateful to them for mending the thing that had been broken. All of it true. All of it anticipated, on page twenty-nine or thirty, in a typed voice, before most of them had been born.

He closed the damper. The crack in the stove door went dark.

"Then stop restoring," said the old man, without turning round. "Start copying."
