# Chapter 11: Seventeen Minutes Asleep

*Friday, November 30, 2108. The Temporal Archive, the Administration of Temporal Defence, and a flat in the Archive district, the Chronos lakeshore city*

---

Julian Kovač woke at 06:10:04 and knew it to the second, because he always did.

He lay still for a moment with his eyes closed, the way he had lain every morning for fifteen years, and let the night come up behind them. It came as it always came, quietly, in the corner of his sight where the log sat when he wanted it: the whole of last night, from the moment he had put out the lamp at 23:41:17 to the moment, just now, when his eyes had opened on the ceiling.

He read it the way other people looked out of the window to see what the day would be.

*23:41:17. Lamp off. 23:52:40. Asleep.* Then the long quiet column of the night, line after line, breath after breath: every one of them there, logged, timed, the slow in and the slower out. The small hitch at 01:14 where he had turned onto his left side. Another turn at 02:36. His heart rate below it all like a tide, drawing back to fifty-one in the deep hours and coming in again towards morning. At 03:47:22 he had woken. The log said so. He had sat up, apparently, for nineteen seconds, and said a word. The word was *no*. Then he had lain down again and gone back to sleep, and the breaths had resumed, one by one, in order.

He did not remember it at all. He never did. He scrolled back and listened to the nineteen seconds, his own voice thick in the dark saying *no* to nobody, and felt the small, familiar embarrassment of hearing himself from outside. Then he let it go. It was only a night. In Chronos nobody needed to remember their nights. The nights were kept.

He opened his eyes.

*06:10:04. Waking.*

---

The flat was small, and orderly, and quiet. Two rooms and a narrow kitchen on the fourth floor of a building in the Archive district, on its eastern edge, where the streets ran down to the water.

He had arranged it, fifteen years ago, so that he never faced that window. The desk stood side-on to it. The chair at the table had its back to it. The bed was on the inner wall. It had not been a decision, exactly, or not one he had ever said aloud; it had simply been how the furniture went, the first week, when he was nineteen and the city was new and he had not wanted to look east. If anyone had ever cared to count, they would have found that in fifteen years of log, every second he had lived here recorded, there were almost no frames of the far horizon. The far shore was too far to see, even on the clearest day. He kept it out of the frame anyway.

He made tea. He drank it standing at the counter and read the morning's annotations.

Every Chronos citizen had an annotation layer beneath the log, where a person could add a note to their own record, a word on a moment, a mark for later. Most people used it for appointments. Julian used it for lists. He had kept lists since he was nine years old, in a little book with a red cover that his grandmother gave him, and when he came to Chronos he had moved them, one by one, into the layer, where they could not be lost. There were two thousand three hundred and six of them now. The oldest was the red book's first page, copied faithfully, in a child's spelling.

*List 1. Things the grown-ups said that do not match.*

He had never kept anything anywhere else. He had never needed to. Anyone with access to his log could read the layer, and he had always known it, and it had never once occurred to him to mind. That was what it meant to live somewhere that forgot nothing. You did not hide. You did not need to.

He looked, as he did most mornings, at the list he had never finished.

*List 47. Things to tell Adrian.*

It had been begun in 2098, the last year his brother had answered a message. It had thirty-one items. The first was *I'm sorry about the station*, which referred to an argument on a railway platform that neither of them had ever mentioned again. The most recent, added in October, was *I read about the storms on your side. Are you all right?* Nothing on it had ever been sent. He had a younger brother somewhere in the claimed strip or beyond it, an outlaw, a man with their mother's eyes, and he had not spoken to him in ten years and seven months, and he could have given the figure to the day.

He closed the list and washed the cup and went to work.

---

The Temporal Archive stood at the heart of its district, white stone and clear glass, with its clocks set into the façade in a long row, each showing the time to a finer division than the one before, until the last showed nothing a human eye could read at all. Julian had stood on the pavement at nineteen with his bag at his feet and the whole of Veridica behind him, watching the last clock blur. He still liked them.

His office was on the third floor. On the shelf, between a study of provincial birth registers and a history of the nanosecond standard, was his own monograph: *The Lost Hour Testimonies: A Chronos Reading*. It had taken him fourteen months, and come out last year. It cited a certain Veridican historian eleven times. He did not take it down. He had not taken it down since the talks began.

The night's reconciliation was waiting for him, as it was every morning. The Archive ran its backups against each other in the small hours, set against set, and in the morning the senior historian countersigned the result. It was the most important thing he did, and it took less time than the tea. He read the summary, found it clean, and countersigned it under the senior key without looking up, the way a clerk initials a ledger, and the key did what it always did, in the time it always took, and the reconciliation was closed.

*08:02:31. Reconciliation, night of 29–30 November. Countersigned.*

He had held the key alone for four years. People on both shores of the lake seemed to think it was a great deal more interesting than it was.

On the second floor, as he passed, two junior archivists were laying out display cases for the observance. It was thirteen days away: the first anniversary of the Interval, the seventeen minutes of the thirteenth of December last year, when the logs of the lakeshore province had lost their content and Chronos had learned what sacrilege felt like. The Archive was to mount an exhibit. There would be a silence at 03:12, city-wide, for seventeen minutes, and the Archive's great clocks would stop for its duration, which they had never done before. One of the juniors looked up and said, "Historian," and he said, "Good morning. That case is crooked," and she laughed and straightened it.

---

The Administration of Temporal Defence occupied a long low building by the harbour with no clocks on it at all. He was called there at 14:00, and he arrived at 13:58:40, and was shown in at 14:00:00 precisely, because that was how Administrator Aubry kept time.

Her office was very clean. It had a desk and two chairs and a screen on the wall, and nothing else: no plants, no pictures, no shelf. Constance Aubry sat behind the desk with her hands folded. She was perhaps fifty, fair, with gray eyes and a courteous voice that never rose, and she had the air, which Julian had noticed at their first meeting, on the ninth of November, of a woman who saw the world as a set of overlapping curves and was always quietly working out where they crossed.

"Historian," she said. "Thank you for coming. Please sit."

He sat.

"I've read the twenty-second," said Aubry.

He had known she would have. The garden was in his log, every second of it: the fog, the bench, the pear tree, Marcus's voice going on in order, *on the first of November, at twenty-one hundred hours*, and then his own helpless laughter, and the paper coming out of his jacket, and the long silence, and *Good. Neither can I.* Temporal Defence had operational access to his log for the duration of the directive. That had been in the directive too. It had not seemed, three weeks ago, like a thing that would ever matter.

"It's an extraordinary record," said Aubry. "I've read it four times."

He felt his face go hot, and said nothing.

"You were distressed. That's in the record too. I'm sorry for it." She said it as if she meant it, and he thought perhaps she did. "But I'd like you to look at it the way I have to look at it, for a moment, as a matter of what's likely." She turned slightly towards the screen on the wall, though she did not put anything on it. "Commissioner Chen has told you his orders. He has told you them in the one place he knew you would record them. He has, in effect, made you the keeper of his refusal. That is a very great intimacy, Julian. People don't do that with strangers."

He said nothing.

"A man who has confessed to you has chosen you," said Aubry.

It was said gently. It was a statement of fact, the kind of thing she said in the voice other people used for the weather. He found that he could not look at her for a moment, and looked at the clean desk instead.

"MERIDIAN continues," she said. "It continues better than before. The shared confession is a foundation neither of you could have built on purpose. You both know now what the other was sent to do, and you both declined. That leaves you, as far as he's concerned, the only person in either government he can trust." She paused. "They want your key, Julian. We knew they would. What we didn't know was whether they'd send someone who would actually refuse to take it. Now we do. That makes him more valuable, not less."

"He meant it," said Julian. "In the garden."

"Probably." Aubry considered him. "I'd put it higher than even. But you're a historian. You'd want the other number too." She said it without any change of tone. "The probability that Commissioner Chen's confession was itself a move? Near a third."

He made himself think about it honestly, the way he would have thought about a disputed entry. A third. It was not a number he could argue with, because there was nothing on the other side of it but the memory of a thin man's face on a wet bench, looking at a pear tree, and a memory was not a record.

"What would you like me to do, Administrator?"

"I don't like people to do things," said Aubry. "I like to know what is likely to happen." She unfolded her hands and folded them again the other way. "If you go on meeting him in the garden, the likeliest outcome, by some distance, is a joint verification under your key by the spring, with his name on it. The war would end. Chronos would be vindicated. It would be a very good outcome for a great many people." She stood, which meant that the meeting was over. "Round three opens on the tenth. I expect you'll go."

"Yes," said Julian. "I expect I will."

At the door she said, "Historian." He turned. "You're a gentle man. It's why we sent you. I'd be sorry to see it used against you." And she smiled, a small cool courteous smile, and he could not tell, and he had spent fifteen years among people who could tell everything, whether she meant it as a kindness or a warning, or whether in her distributions those were the same curve.

---

He walked home along the harbour. He did not take the tram. He wanted the time.

*List 2307,* he began, in the layer, as he walked. *Things Administrator Aubry said that she did not need to say.* And then he stopped, and left it with only its heading, because he found he did not want to make it yet.

She was not wrong. That was what he kept coming back to, past the fish market and the ferry sheds. Marcus had chosen him. MERIDIAN could go on. The key was what both sides of the lake wanted; he had watched Marcus look at his hands whenever it was mentioned, and he had looked at his own. He was a thing two governments wanted to hold, and every word he said to Marcus from now on would be a word in a directive, whatever he meant by it.

Unless nobody could use it.

He stopped by a mooring post and stood there in the cold with his hands in his pockets.

The right of self-disclosure. Every child in Chronos learned it the year the interface went in. A citizen's log was the citizen's own, and any citizen might publish any part of it, at any time, to anyone, and no Administration could forbid it. It was the oldest right Chronos had. It was the foundation of everything: that the record belonged to the people it recorded. Erasing a log was the worst thing Chronos knew. Publishing one was the most ordinary.

If he published the garden, and the directive, and his own orders, then the whole of it would be out in the open, and it could not be taken back, and it could not be used. Not by Aubry, as a foundation. Not by Veridica, as a lever. A confession that everybody had read was not an intimacy any more. It was a fact.

He would not do it alone. He knew that at once, standing by the post, and was surprised by how certain he was. He would do it with Marcus, on the same day, each from his own side. Marcus's orders on his shore and Julian's on this one. Two refusals, published at once, so that neither was the other's instrument.

He found he was smiling. He was standing in the cold on the harbour front at 15:47 on a Friday with the ferry sheds dripping, and he was smiling, and the log had it, and he did not mind.

---

At home he did what a historian does before he publishes anything. He checked his own record.

He did not check all of it. That would have taken fifteen years. He checked the directive, and the garden, and every meeting in the talks, second by second, to be sure there was nothing in them he would be ashamed to have read. There was a great deal he was embarrassed by. There was nothing he was ashamed of. He noted that in the layer, and then, because the observance was thirteen days away and it was on his mind, and because he was a historian and could not help himself, he scrolled back further.

A year. Almost exactly.

*Tuesday, 13 December 2107.*

Everyone in the province had done it, that week. He remembered the week very clearly, because it was logged, and because it had been the strangest week of his life in Chronos: four million people scrolling back through the same night, all at once, to find the place where their record had gone quiet. People had done it on trams. People had done it at their desks and in the queues at the fish market. He had done it himself at this table, on the evening of the thirteenth, with his tea going cold, and had found what everyone found, and had closed it, and had not opened it again.

He opened it again now.

The evening. His lamp going out at 23:09. The long quiet column of the night beginning, as it always began. And then, in the small hours:

*03:12:00 – 03:29:00. Asleep. Heart rate steady.*

That was all. Seventeen minutes, and nothing else in them. The same trace everyone had. The same four words, more or less, in four million logs across the lakeshore province: the last state carried over, and held, and let go again at 03:29, when the record picked up and went on as if nothing had happened. Most people had been asleep. Most people's logs said so. His did.

He had slept through it. That was what he had felt, the first time, a year ago: a kind of shame, almost, that on the one night in Chronos's history when the record had been violated he had been lying in this bed with his eyes shut, breathing, while someone somewhere took seventeen minutes out of the world. He had not woken. His log said he had not. He had slept through the sacrilege like everyone else.

He looked at it for a while, as he had looked at it before. He had never thought about it much. He did not think about it much now. It was an old wound in the record, a year old, and there was an observance for it, and an exhibit, and a silence, and his job was to see that the cases were straight.

He closed it.

---

He made more tea and sat at the table with his back to the window, and thought about Marcus.

He had found, since the talks began, that he did it anyway, at odd moments, and that it was better to do it on purpose.

The fog. The bench under the bare pear tree. The thin man in glasses beside him, telling it all in order with dates, like a man reading an entry aloud, and never once looking at him until the end. The long silence afterwards, which had been the most restful silence of Julian's life, and he did not understand why. And then his own voice saying *I can't take it back*, and Marcus, very quietly, not triumphant at all: *Good. Neither can I.*

He had lived in Chronos for fifteen years. In fifteen years no one had ever said anything to him that could not be looked up. That was the nature of the place. Every word anyone had ever said to him was in his log and theirs, and could be read back, and was. There was no such thing, here, as a sentence meant only for the person who heard it. He had come here at nineteen because he wanted to live in a country that forgot nothing, and he had got his wish, and it had turned out that a country that forgot nothing was a country where nothing was ever only yours.

And then a man from the other shore had sat down beside him and said the most private thing he had, on purpose, in the one place where it could not be private, so that it would be kept.

He did not know what to call what he felt about that. He did not try very hard. He sat with it, with his tea, the way one sits with something warm.

---

He wrote to Marcus at nine.

The Synthesis secretariat at Saltmarsh Crossing carried documents between the delegations under Professor Park's seal, and would carry a private letter addressed care of the talks. It was the only post that crossed the lake in wartime. He did not suppose it was private. He did not suppose anything was.

Before he wrote it, he stood up, and went to the window, and for the first time in a long while he stood facing it, with the dark water beyond the glass and the far shore somewhere out there, too far to see. And he said the letter aloud, slowly, word by word, so that it would be in his log first. So that it existed, whatever happened to the paper.

*Marcus,*

*I have been thinking about what we can't take back, and I think we should make sure nobody else can either. I would like to publish it. The directive, and the garden, from my own log. It is my right here, and nobody can stop it. I would like to do it on the same day as you do yours, if you will, each from our own side, so that neither of us is anyone's instrument. I'll be at the talks on the tenth. We can decide the day there, where it will be recorded.*

He stopped. Outside, a ferry light moved slowly across the black water and went out behind the harbour wall.

There was one more thing. Nobody had asked for it. Aubry had not asked, and Marcus had not, and nobody on either shore had ever asked him for it straight out, in all the weeks of wanting it. He thought of Marcus looking at his hands every time the key was mentioned. He thought that a thing two governments wanted so much must look, from the outside, like a locked door with something terrible behind it, and he thought that was a sad way for a friend to have to look at it.

He said it aloud, into the log, to the window, before he could decide not to.

*If it helps, I can show you the reconciliation key. So you can see there is nothing behind it.*
