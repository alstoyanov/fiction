# Chapter 9: The True Source

*Monday, November 26, 2108. The Commission's public testimony intake, and the flat by the old river, Veridica Capital*

---

*Incident: none yet. Time: 08:52. Location: commissioners' room, fourth floor. Reporting party: Kovač, H., by internal line.*

"Commissioner Osman. There is a deposit I would like you to see."

That was all Kovač said. She did not say what kind of deposit, or why it could not wait for the ten o'clock circulation, and Kira did not ask. She had known Helena Kovač for two years and five months, and in that time the woman had never once used a telephone to say something she could have written down.

Kira put her pen in the gutter of the file she had been reading and stood up.

It was the Monday after the second round. Four days since Marcus had come in at eleven on Thursday night with the fog still on his coat and sat down at the kitchen table without taking it off. He had told her in order, in the voice he used for things he did not trust himself to feel yet: the bench, the orders, the key, the laugh, the folded paper in the other man's jacket. MERIDIAN. *C. Aubry, Administrator.* A list of questions nearly the same as his own.

She had listened to all of it. At the end she had broken the silence she had kept since the Day of Testimony, because it had stopped being useful. "Then it's in their record," she had said. "Good." And then, because she was an engineer and could not leave a system half understood: "Write to Wei. One question. In writing. About the annex."

He had written it on Friday morning and sent it to the Technical Director's office under his own name, and she had read it over his shoulder before he sealed it. *How does a restoration route reach Chronos's true source from outside Chronos?* A courteous acknowledgement had come back within the hour. The Technical Director would be glad to reply in full. He hoped Monday would be convenient.

It was Monday. She went down the stairs.

---

The public intake was on the ground floor, at the front of the building, where anyone could come in off the street. Kira counted as she walked in, because she always counted, and because counting kept the rest of her quiet.

One counter, long, pale wood, with a clerk's station at either end. Six writing desks along the left wall, each with a lamp, a pen on a chain and a tray of blank testimony forms. Two cameras: one high in the corner above the door, covering the hall, and one on the far wall, angled down at the slot. The slot itself was set into the right-hand wall at chest height, a steel mouth the width of a hand, with a small steel plate beneath it that read, in plain capitals, *TESTIMONY MAY BE DEPOSITED HERE AT ANY HOUR*. Behind it, she knew, was a chute and a receiving tray on the other side of the wall.

The doors opened to the public at nine. It was nine minutes to. Nobody was in the hall but a clerk at the right-hand station, who looked up, and a woman standing by the inner door with her hands in the pockets of her dark coat.

"Ms. Osman," said Kovač.

"Director."

Kovač did not correct her. She never had.

Kovač opened the inner door and stood back to let her through.

Her office was behind the counter: small, a desk, two chairs, a filing cabinet, a window onto a light well, and the plant, which had come with her from Ashford and was, Kira thought, overwatered. On the desk was a terminal and, beside it, a gray card folder, closed.

Kovač did not touch the folder. She sat down at the terminal and turned the screen a few degrees towards the other chair, enough for Kira to read one line and no more.

*26 NOV 2108. 06:58:14. PUBLIC DEPOSIT (STANDARD). ITEMS: 1. SHEETS: 5. SOURCE: SLOT. DEPOSITOR: ANONYMOUS.*

"The clerk found it in the receiving tray at eight forty," said Kovač, "when she came on. It had been logged at six fifty-eight. The slot's scanner stamps every deposit as it passes the chute, and the stamp is here." She touched the line without touching the screen. "The intake doesn't open until nine. People use the slot overnight. It's what it's for. There's nothing unusual in an early deposit." She paused. "I then did what I do with every anonymous deposit, which is to look at the camera."

She changed the screen. The hall came up, gray and still, from the camera on the far wall: the slot in the right of the frame, the desks, the long counter, the door. A timestamp ran in the corner. *06:00:00.*

Kovač ran it forward.

Kira watched. She counted the minutes as they went by in the corner of the frame, and watched the slot, and watched the door. The light in the hall changed a little as the street outside went from dark to gray. A shadow moved across the floor once, at 07:31, and she leaned forward, and it was the shadow of a tram passing the front windows. At 08:38 the clerk came in through the inner door, hung up her coat, and went to her station. At 08:40 she went through to the receiving side. Nobody else came into the hall. Nobody went near the slot. The steel plate over the chute did not move at all.

At 06:58:14 there was nothing in the frame but an empty room.

Kovač stopped the playback.

"I have looked at the other camera," she said. "It shows the same hall from the door. Nobody." She took her glasses off, rubbed her eyes once, and put them back. "Either the camera is wrong or the log is. Neither has been wrong before."

Kira looked at the frozen frame for a while.

*Deposit: logged 06:58:14 via slot. Camera: slot undisturbed, 06:00 to 08:40. Second camera: concurs. Visitor: none.*

She did not have a status for it. She put it where things went when she had no status for them, and said, "May I see the pages?"

---

Kovač opened the folder.

Five sheets, typed, lying square. The paper was old and good, gone soft and faintly gray at the edges, and the type was the type of a machine rather than a printer: each letter pressed into the paper, not laid on it. Kira did not pick them up. She looked first: the object before the words.

Page numbers at the foot of each sheet, centred. *13. 14. 15. 16. 17.*

In the lower right corner of every sheet, small, typed: *X*.

And the *e*.

She had seen it on Singh's photograph of page 30 a hundred times, and on the three sheets from the blue songbook since the night the old man had given them up: a small bite out of the upper curve of every lowercase *e*, the same place, the same shape, over and over, like a limp. It was here. She found it in the first line of the first page and then could not stop finding it. Every *e*.

At the foot of the fourteenth page, and the sixteenth, and again below the last line on the seventeenth, a little apart from the text, there was a short typed mark: two capitals, a raised dot, a number.

*YP·02.*

Page 30 had one. *CZ·11.* The pattern was the same: two capitals, a dot set high, a small two-digit number. She did not know what they meant, and she knew that not knowing was different from there being nothing to know. She filed the pattern and did not try to force it. A key you tried to force told you only what you already believed.

The heading was at the top of page thirteen, in capitals.

*PRINCIPLE THREE: STRATEGIC AMBIGUITY THROUGH BACKUP CONTROL*

She read the first paragraph, and then she read it again, and then she stopped reading and sat quite still in Kovač's second chair with her hands flat on her knees.

> *"Maintain three backup systems that appear independent and are not. Should outsiders corrupt the record, it may be restored from the true source. Should insiders reform it, it may be restored from the true source. Ambiguity, controlled, is both defence and instrument."*

---

She read her own case.

It came to her in the only form she had ever been able to think about it without her hands shaking, which was an incident report.

*Incident 1. Date: November 18, 2105. 22:00. Location: Factorepo Spire, sixtieth floor. Event: Security Protocol Update 7.3 entered by Osman, K. Verified green. Three weeks later: absent from all systems. No record of implementation. No contradiction. Mechanism: unknown.*

*Mechanism: restored from the true source.*

*Incident 2. Date: October 2105. Location: Correction Registry. Event: The Judge's finding on Chen, M., 0.58. Entry on file: 0.73. No trace of alteration. Mechanism: unknown.*

*Mechanism: restored from the true source.*

*Incident 3. Date: November 15, 2106. 14:00:00 to 14:59:59. Location: primary repository and all three backups. Event: one hour absent. Every backup empty at once. Three independent systems, failing identically in the same second.*

That was the one that had never made sense. She had said it aloud in a cellar two years ago, to Nikolai's feed, to nobody: you could not erase an hour from three independent backups at once, because that was the whole point of having three. You could only do it if the three were not independent. If they only *appeared* to be. If behind them there was one source they all answered to, and whoever held it could put a thing back, or take it out, and every backup would agree.

*Three backup systems that appear independent and are not.*

It had been typed in 2066. It had been waiting on a shelf somewhere for forty-two years to tell her how her fact had been stolen.

And the other two words. She had heard them three weeks ago, not in her own ears but in Marcus's, at the kitchen table, on the night of the first. The technical annex to KINDRED. Wei's annex, elegant, unhurried. A reconciliation run under Julian Kovač's key would let Veridica's technicians re-verify Chronos's account of the December interval *from the true source*. Marcus had repeated the phrase as jargon, the way the annex had used it. She had heard it as jargon too.

It was not jargon. It was a term of art. It was the name for the thing behind the backups, and whoever had written the annex knew it had one, in Chronos as well as here, and knew how to reach it.

*Finding: Veridica knows how to reach Chronos's true source. From outside. With one man's key.*

She made herself stop there. It was the finding the evidence supported, and no further. She did not let herself ask who else might know the way, or from which side, because that was a question with no data under it yet, and she had watched too many people build a model before they had the measurements.

But the third sentence would not stay still. *Should insiders reform it, it may be restored from the true source.* She read it once more, slowly, as a specification. It did not say *if*. It said *should*, the way a manual says *should the pressure fall*: an anticipated condition, with its procedure already written. Someone in 2066 had sat at a machine with a chipped letter and planned, calmly, for reformers. For people inside the system who would try to fix it. The procedure for them was the same as the procedure for enemies.

She sat on the Historical Integrity Commission. For two years her whole work had been to fix the record from inside. She was an insider, reforming.

She put that where she put things that frightened her, which was in the same column as everything else.

Her question had been answered. Wei's office owed her a reply this morning, and the reply was lying on Kovač's desk, and Wei had not written it.

She noted, in the same flat column, that the pages had arrived on the morning the answer was due. She noted it and did not do anything with it. A coincidence was a data point until there were two.

---

It started while she was still looking at the page. It always started when she was looking at something.

*Layer one: key exchange. Asymmetric, lattice-based. Rotation interval—*

She felt it come up under her breastbone the way it had come up in the white chair and on the river walk and a hundred times since: the specification, word for word, in her own voice, wanting to be said aloud. Two years of it. It had faded. It had not gone. It came back when something reached the place where the fact had been.

She did not let it reach her mouth. She put one finger on the corner of the top sheet, beside the *X*, and counted.

*Thirteen. Fourteen. Fifteen. Sixteen. Seventeen.*

Five.

She counted them again. Five. The words went back down.

When she looked up, Kovač was looking at the plant.

---

"Procedure," said Kira.

"Anonymous deposits are held unpublished pending verification," said Kovač. It was the voice she used for rules, flat and exact, a woman reading a form she had written herself. "The intake does not verify. It receives, logs and keeps. A Commissioner may take an item out for the purpose of verification, against signature, and must return it in the condition it left. Nothing goes into the public archive until it is verified or until I enter it as testimony received. Which I will not do on my own authority for this." She pushed her glasses up her nose. "I would rather it went out of this office in your hands than sat in my cabinet with nobody knowing what it is."

She took a release slip from the drawer. Paper, printed, with carbon behind it. Kira read it through before she signed, because she read everything through. *Item: 1. Sheets: 5. Released to: OSMAN, K., Commissioner. Purpose: verification.* She signed. Kovač signed under her, in small upright capitals, the way Kira had seen her sign a work order in a basement once, long ago.

*AUTHORISED: H. KOVAČ.*

"And the log?" said Kira.

"The log stays here." Kovač tore off the carbon and put it in the folder with the pages, and put the top copy in her own drawer, and locked it. "I've shown you the entry. That is the entry. Nobody else has been in the log since I opened it this morning, and I would like it to stay the one thing in this building nobody has touched." She said it without emphasis, as an item on an agenda. "If I start letting people in to look, I won't know afterwards what they looked at."

Kira thought of a hash chain re-sealed backwards from the far edge. She did not argue.

At the door, with the folder under her arm, she stopped.

"He wrote to you," she said.

Kovač had her hands in her coat pockets again. She did not pretend not to understand.

"One line," she said. "Mr. Chen has it."

"I've read it."

"Yes. I thought you might have."

Neither of them said the other name. It sat between them in the small office, with the plant and the locked drawer and the light well, a man Kovač had not seen since he was nineteen and Kira had never met, on the far side of a lake, with a key.

"He used to take apart my kitchen clock," said Kovač, after a moment. "The other one. Adrian. I don't know if I ever told you that."

"You did," said Kira.

"So I did." Something moved at the corner of Kovač's mouth. "I'll expect those back, Ms. Osman."

---

She did not go back to the fourth floor. She took the folder home.

Wei's office sent nothing all afternoon. At four a Commission messenger brought the day's circulation and there was nothing in it from the Technical Director. At six, when Marcus came in, there was still nothing. He looked at her face and then at the folder on the kitchen table and took off his coat without asking, and sat down, and waited.

She did not show him the pages at once. She drew the first journal towards her from where it lay open at the end of the table, turned to the next clean page, uncapped her pen, and began to copy.

She copied Principle Three by hand, all five pages, in her own writing, line by line, with the page numbers in the margin where they fell. She copied the heading and the text and the numeral in each corner, and the mark at the foot of the fourteenth, sixteenth and seventeenth pages, drawing the raised dot carefully above the line. She did not copy the chipped *e*, because it was not a thing you could copy. It took her an hour and forty minutes. Marcus made coffee twice and did not speak.

When she had finished she dated the entry, timed it, signed it, and turned the book round on the table, and Marcus read it in her hand before he read it in the type. That was how she wanted it. It existed twice now. Whatever happened to the five sheets in the gray folder, there was another copy in a house nobody had any reason to search, in the hand of a woman the record had once declared dead.

He read the four sentences three times. She watched him reach the second sentence and the third and understand them, one after the other, the way she had. The 0.58. The 0.73. The hour.

"The true source," he said at last. "The annex."

"Yes."

"He didn't answer."

"He didn't need to."

She ruled a line under the copy and wrote her columns below it, the way he had taught her, which was the way she had taught him.

*CHECKED: five sheets, pp. 13–17. Typed. The chipped e, as the Introduction and p. 30. Copy numeral X, all pages. Mark YP·02, pp. 14, 16, 17.*

*UNCHECKED: 06:58. No visitor.*

She looked at that for a while. Then she wrote the other thing underneath it, the thing that had been sitting in her since she first saw the corner of the top sheet.

*UNCHECKED: The Introduction: no numeral. These pages: X. Page 30: XI. One sender, or two?*

---

It was nearly nine when she got up and took the envelope from the drawer under the window: Singh's photograph of page 30, the one they had all worked from before Kovač brought the original out of a dead woman's safe, the one they had all looked at so many times the print had gone soft at the corners.

She laid it on the table beside the seventeenth page of Principle Three. The photograph's lower right corner, and the typed sheet's. She put one finger on each.

*XI.*

*X.*

The same machine. The same chipped letter. The same small numeral in the same place, typed by the same hand, on paper of the same age. Two copies of one document, from two different copies.

Marcus leaned over, glasses pushed up on his forehead, and looked at her two fingers.

"XI came out of the Ministry's own correspondence," he said slowly.

"X, not XI," said Kira.

She did not take her fingers off the corners. Outside, a tram went down the street, and the window shook very slightly in its frame, and stopped.

"Someone else has a copy."
