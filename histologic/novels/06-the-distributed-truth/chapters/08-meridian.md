# Chapter 8: MERIDIAN

*Sunday, November 18 – Thursday, November 22, 2108. The resistance farmhouse, the border zone; the Old Customs House at Saltmarsh Crossing, the neutral zone*

---

*November seventeenth, 2108. Twenty-three ten hours. The flat, kitchen table. Involved parties: Chen, M.; Osman, K. Subject under discussion: none.*

Kira had not spoken about KINDRED since the Thursday of the Day of Testimony.

She spoke about everything else: the listening sessions in the lakeshore towns, the tram timetable, the Chronos broadcast figures, which she read standing at the counter with her coat still on. Her hand found his under the table as it always had. But when he came near the word, she simply stopped, the way a person stops at the edge of a kerb, and waited for him to go back.

At night she wrote in the first journal at the kitchen table, late, after he had gone to bed, and in the morning the book was still there, open, the pen laid across the gutter. For two years she had shut it every night and put it on the shelf above the stove, as if it were a thing that could be taken.

He did not read it. That was the point of leaving it open. She had nothing in it to hide from him, and nothing to say to him about what he was doing, and she wanted him to know both.

He understood the other thing later than he should have. It was not only KINDRED. It was the surname on the last page of the file.

On Saturday night, after Kovač had gone, he had put Adrian's letter on the table beside the open journal and left it there without a word. In the morning the envelope was where he had left it, but turned over, the flap tucked in, the way you close something you have read.

She was standing at the window with her coffee when he came in. She did not turn round.

"I'm going out to the farmhouse," he said.

"Yes," said Kira.

---

Elena was counting trucks.

There were four of them, gray, mud to the doors, coming back up the track from the lakeshore road in a line with their lights off in the middle of the day. She stood by the barn with her hands in the pockets of her heavy jacket and watched them go in one after another, and her eyes moved left to right across each cab as it passed, the way they moved across every group of people she had ever been responsible for. Marcus had seen her do it on a dark road once, at the end of a night he still could not remember properly. *Eleven. I was told eight.*

He waited until the fourth truck was in. From the back room of the farmhouse, through a window propped open with a book, Old Songs was humming the Lakeshore Air, slow, each phrase climbing at the end and hanging there. The Introduction had gone to Kira a week ago, after the old man's one night with it.

Elena pulled the barn door across and turned.

He told her, not well, standing in a muddy yard to a woman who was looking at the gate. The office on the top floor. The cover sheet with no signature line. The key. The seven questions. The week that had become a delegation, and the delegation that had become two rounds, and a bench in a garden where everything Julian Kovač heard was written down.

She heard him out without once looking at him. When he had finished, a dog barked somewhere down towards the lake. The humming in the back room went on, lifting, hanging.

"It always becomes real on one side," said Elena.

That was all. After a moment she went into the barn to see about the trucks.

He knew which side she meant, and which autumn: a café, seventeen forty-three, a fountain he could no longer walk past without something in the light going through him. He knew which of them it had become real for.

He drove back to the capital with the window down, in the cold, so that he would have something else to feel.

---

*November nineteenth, 2108. Oh-nine-thirty hours. Old Customs House, Saltmarsh Crossing. Lakes Talks, round two, first session. Involved parties: the Veridican delegation; the Chronos delegation; Park, J. (Synthesis). Status: continuing.*

Round two was round one again, with the fog a week colder.

The Old Customs House stood on its spit of hard ground in the marsh, stone the colour of wet slate, the Synthesis flag hanging straight down for want of wind. Inside, the Chronos delegates still paused very slightly at every shielded door, as a person pauses on a stair that is not where he expected it. Behind, through a low arched door, the garden waited with its wall and its gravel and its single bare pear tree, trained flat against the stone in the shape Marcus had lost the word for a week ago and been given back.

Professor Park opened each morning with tea and an anecdote: a winter in the old capital Archive, perhaps in the sixties, when the heating failed and the readers worked in their coats. She said *perhaps* the way other people breathed. Marcus looked forward to it. It was the only thing in the building that did not want anything from him.

The positions did not move. Chronos wanted a joint verification of the December interval under the Temporal Archive's senior key, with Veridican witnesses, so that Veridica could watch its guilt confirmed. Veridica said the interval had been fabricated. Each side said it in numbered paragraphs, and Park thanked them both.

Every time the key was mentioned, Julian looked at his hands.

At every break the aide came, young and polite, with a tablet held against her chest: "Historian, the protocol room is ready." And Julian said, "In a moment," and then, to Marcus, as if it had only just occurred to him, "Shall we get some air? I can't think in a room that doesn't remember." And they went out into the fog and sat on the bench under the pear tree, and the fog stood a few metres off on every side like a patient audience.

Julian talked about the Archive's clocks, which were accurate to a degree that made him, he said, feel personally responsible for every second he wasted, and about a professor at the Archive school who had failed his first paper for writing *approximately* in a footnote. He asked about the fourteenth floor, and when Marcus reached for the end of a story about a burst pipe and found nothing there, Julian waited and then said, "It's all right. I'd rather have the half that's true."

On Tuesday afternoon, on the bench, Julian said, "I have a brother. Younger. I haven't spoken to him in—" and stopped, and did the arithmetic visibly, a Chronos historian unable to leave a number unfinished. "Ten years. More. He's on your side of the water, I think. Somewhere." Then he looked at the pear tree and said, in quite a different voice, "Do they prune it, do you know? Or does it just grow like that, against the wall, because it has nowhere else to go?"

Marcus said that he thought someone must prune it.

The letter was in his inside pocket. It had been there since Saturday. He could feel the corner of the envelope against his ribs when he leaned back, and he did not lean back again that afternoon.

---

The Synthesis guesthouse was a converted granary on the landward side of the marsh, with narrow rooms and good lamps and a view of nothing. On Wednesday night Marcus sat on the bed with his lists spread on the blanket and did what he had done every night for two weeks, which was to read them through from the top and pretend it was study.

He had ruled a new sheet for Julian in round one. The left column was headed CHECKED. It was long now.

*Kovač, J. Senior Historian, Chronos Temporal Archive. Confirmed: public register.*

*Monograph on the Lost Hour testimonies. Cites Chen, M. eleven times. Confirmed: counted.*

*Left Veridica at nineteen for the Archive school. Confirmed: Kovač, H.*

*Has a younger brother, not spoken to in more than ten years. Confirmed: Kovač, H. (the brother's name is Adrian).*

*Knew "14-08". Confirmed: Commission public archive.*

Everything Julian had told him that could be checked, he had checked, and all of it was true. Marcus had never met a person whose account of himself held so completely. He had begun to find it unbearable.

The other list was on the last page of his notebook, where he had begun it in round one. KINDRED. He had not ruled it into columns. It was only a list, in the format of the approach sequence, as if he were a technician logging the progress of an installation.

*Subject asks questions: yes.*

*Subject lonely: yes.*

*Subject trusts me:*

The line stopped where it had stopped a week ago. He looked at it for a long time.

He understood, sitting there, why he could not finish it. It was not uncertainty. The answer was yes. Julian had turned to him on the bench on Tuesday and said *I have a brother*, which was not a thing a man in his position said to a delegate of a hostile nation, and he had said it because he trusted Marcus. And Marcus had come back to this room and written it down in the CHECKED column, with its source.

If he wrote *yes* in the KINDRED column, the entry was complete, and a completed entry was a thing you could hand to somebody. Maitland was waiting to be handed it. So was Wei, pleasantly, with his tablet in his right hand.

And if he did not write it, it would not stay unwritten. The entry did not need his pencil. It was already true, and it would go on becoming more true every day he sat on that bench and asked the next question on the sheet, until it finished itself without him, and the only choice left would be what to do with it.

He had been the subject of an entry like that once. Someone had sat opposite him in a café for eight weeks and let it complete itself.

*It always becomes real on one side.*

He put the lists together, squared the edges, folded them once, and put them in his coat. Then he sat on the edge of the bed with his hands between his knees and thought, carefully and in order, about where.

---

*November twenty-second, 2108. Oh-seven-fifteen hours. Synthesis guesthouse. Involved parties: Chen, M. Decision: the garden.*

Not a shielded room. That was the first thing, and he was surprised how quickly it came.

In a shielded room Julian's interface recorded nothing. A refusal spoken there was a thing two men had said to each other in private, and could be denied by either of them, and by both their governments, for the rest of their lives. Marcus himself, some grayer year, under some pressure he could not yet imagine, could deny it.

The garden was unshielded. Everything Julian heard there went into his log, second by second, into the permanent record of a nation that regarded forgetting as a sin. A refusal spoken on that bench would be in Chronos's record the moment it was spoken, timestamped to the nanosecond, and no one in either capital could ever make it not have been said.

It was the one place in the neutral zone where a sentence could not be taken back.

That was why he chose it.

---

The morning session was on the broadcast overlap and ran long. At eleven forty the aide came to the table with her tablet and said, "Historian, the protocol room is ready," and Julian said, "In a moment," and looked at Marcus.

Marcus stood up first.

The fog was the thickest of the week. From the bench the far wall was a gray idea, and the pear tree's branches went up the stone and vanished before they reached the top. Everything dripped. Julian sat down at his end of the bench, in his archivist's jacket with the gray cardigan under it, and put his hands in his pockets, and said, "Eleven forty-one and some seconds. I won't tell you how many. You'd think less of me."

"Julian," said Marcus.

Julian looked at him. Something in Marcus's voice must have reached him before the words did, because the easy look went out of his face, quite slowly, and what was underneath was very attentive and very still.

Marcus told him.

He told it in order, with dates, the way he would have entered it at desk 14-08.

"On the first of November, at twenty-one hundred hours, I was called to the First Minister's office. The First Minister was there, and Minister Chen Zhao, and our Technical Director. They offered me the delegation to these talks, and a file called KINDRED. It had no signature line." He kept his eyes on the pear tree. "It said that you hold the senior reconciliation key to your Archive's backups. That a reconciliation run under your key would let our technicians re-verify your government's account of the December interval from inside your own backups. From the true source. That your Judge would then read a record that no longer supported its finding, and the war would end within a month."

He heard Julian breathe in.

"My task was to befriend you. To win your trust, and then to use it to get the key." The next part was harder. He said it anyway. "There was another sheet. An approach sequence. Seven questions, to ask you over the first meetings. What did you want to be before you were what you are. Who taught you to keep records. Who still writes to you. What do you do on the nights you can't sleep. There are three more. I've asked you some of them. You answered all of them." He stopped, and made himself go on. "They gave me a week to decide. On the eighth I accepted the delegation, and said I would study the rest. I've been studying it for two weeks. On this bench."

The fog dripped. Somewhere beyond the wall a bird made one sound and stopped.

"I'm not going to do it," said Marcus. "I should have said so on the first day. I'm saying it here, because you're recording, and I want it to be somewhere I can't take it back."

He made himself turn his head.

Julian had gone white, all the colour gone out of his face at once, so that his very blue eyes looked almost black in the fog. For a long moment he did not move at all, and Marcus thought, with a lurch, *I have done it after all. I have done the thing on the sheet. I have found the place where he was open and put my hand in it.*

Then Julian laughed.

It came out of him in a helpless rush, like the breath of someone who has been held under water. He put his hands over his face and laughed into them, bent forward on the wet bench, his shoulders shaking, until the sound changed and caught and Marcus could not tell any more whether it was laughing. Then it stopped. Julian sat with his hands over his face and breathed, carefully, like a man counting.

"I'm sorry," he said through his fingers. "I'm so sorry. It isn't— I'm not laughing at you." He took his hands away. His eyes were wet. "I'm really not. Wait."

He reached inside his jacket and took out a paper folded small and carried a long time, the creases gone soft. He unfolded it on his knee, smoothed it once with the side of his hand, and held it out.

Marcus took it.

It was printed, not written: a clean Chronos document face with a header band and a reference line and a time of issue accurate to a fraction of a second.

*ADMINISTRATION OF TEMPORAL DEFENCE. DIRECTIVE: MERIDIAN.*

*Authorised: C. Aubry, Administrator.*

*Objective: to win the trust of the Veridican restorer (CHEN, MARCUS, Commissioner, Historical Integrity Commission) and to turn him. Measure of completion: a joint verification of the December interval, conducted under the Temporal Archive's senior key, bearing the restorer's name as Veridican witness.*

Below it was a second section, headed *APPROACH SEQUENCE*, and a numbered list.

*1. What did you want to be, before you became what you are?*

*2. Who taught you to keep a record?*

*3. Who still writes to you?*

*4. What do you do on the nights when you cannot sleep?*

*5. What is the first thing you remember that no one else does?*

*6. Is there anything you have never told the record?*

*7. Who would you trust to remember you?*

Marcus read it twice. Six of the seven he knew, a little differently worded, in nearly the same order. Only the last was not on his own sheet.

He did not say anything about that. Neither did Julian. They both looked at the list for a while, and then Marcus folded the paper along its soft creases and gave it back, and Julian put it on his knee under his hand, and neither of them said what it meant that two governments on opposite shores of a lake had sat down separately and written nearly the same questions for the same two men.

---

It fell into place the way things fell into place for him now, one entry at a time, each one taking its status as he reached it.

*"14-08, I think? I read your testimony." November twelfth, first break. Status: CHECKED against the Commission archive. Revised: the number was in his briefing.*

"It was on page two of my briefing," said Julian quietly, as if he had followed him there. "Your desk. Your index, the real one and the typed one. I did read your testimony too, years ago, for the book. I'd have known the number anyway. But they gave it to me, and I used it on the first day, because it said to use it on the first day."

*The questions. November twelfth to fifteenth. Asked in order, nearly. Status: explained.*

*The garden.* Marcus looked up at the wall, the tree, the fog.

"Every break," he said. "The aide took us in and you took us out."

"Administrator Aubry wanted every word in my log," said Julian. "She was very clear. Nothing that mattered was to be said in a room that didn't remember." A small, exhausted movement of his mouth. "And it was true, what I told you. I really can't think in them. That was the terrible part. Every true thing I said was also on the sheet."

*Kovač, H. November seventeenth. "Chronos never lets Julian out of the Archive… So why send him to talks now?" Status: UNCHECKED.*

Marcus moved it across in his head, and felt it go.

*Status: answered. MERIDIAN.*

A man who had not travelled since he was given the key, sent to sit on a bench in the neutral zone and ask a stranger who still wrote to him. Kovač had seen it in one frame of a broadcast and asked the only question that mattered, and the answer had been sitting beside him.

---

They sat side by side on the wet bench, each with a paper in his keeping that ordered him to seduce the other, and for a long time neither of them said anything at all.

Marcus thought about Wing C. A cell across a corridor, and a woman placed in it, *line of sight*, so that something would grow in a mind that had been emptied, and a man in a white coat watching to see what did. Protocol R-2. He had spent two years learning to say *they arranged the room, they did not arrange what we did in it*, and meaning it.

Now two states had arranged the same room. Each had sent a lonely man with a list of questions to sit in front of another lonely man, and each had waited to see what would grow.

He sat and looked at the pear tree, trained flat against the stone so long ago that it no longer knew any other shape.

"What was real?" he said at last. He had not meant to say it aloud.

Julian did not answer at once. When he did, his voice was very quiet and very exact, the voice of a man giving evidence who knows the value of every word.

"The answers," he said. "All of them. I wanted to be a lock-keeper until I was eleven, and then they automated the locks on our canal, and I cried for a day, and my father laughed at me. My grandmother taught me to keep a record, with a pencil, in a little book with a red cover, because she said my mother never remembered anything properly. Nobody writes to me. On the nights I can't sleep I read my own log back from the morning, to see if I said anything stupid, and I usually have." He turned his head. "All of it. It was on the sheet to ask. It wasn't on the sheet to answer."

"Mine too," said Marcus. "The answers."

"I know," said Julian.

They looked at each other. It was true, and neither of them could prove it. There was no record of what a man meant when he answered a question. There was only the question, and the order to ask it, and the log. The Judge could read the log to the nanosecond and never know which of them had been lying, because neither of them had, and there was no field in any record for that.

Julian looked down at the folded directive on his knee. He smoothed it once more with the side of his hand. Then he looked up at the fog, at the wall he could not see. Every second of the last quarter of an hour had already gone from behind his eyes across the water.

"It's in my log now," he said. "I can't take it back."

Marcus thought of the file with no signature line, and the office on the top floor, and a Technical Director who had wished him good luck in a corridor. He thought of a KINDRED entry that would never now be finished, by him or by anyone.

"Good," he said. "Neither can I."
