# Chapter 12: The Third Fragment

*Monday, December 3, 2108. The Sovereignty of Free Will*

---

The link came in at eleven minutes past eleven, which in the capital was eleven minutes past four in the morning, and Isaiah supposed that Kira had not been to bed.

It came the long way round, as everything from Veridica came now: through three borrowed nodes, across the lake to a Synthesis relay, and then south and east across half the world to a desk in a building that had no neural interfaces in it at all. The voice channel crackled. There was a delay of a little over two seconds on every sentence, so that each of them arrived like a letter, already finished, with nothing to be done about it.

"Isaiah." Two seconds. "I'm sending three images. They'll be slow."

"Good morning, Commissioner," he said, and waited for his own courtesy to reach her.

She did not return it. She never did at this hour. He heard, instead, very faintly, paper moving, and then nothing but the crackle, while the first image assembled itself on his screen in bands from the top down, like a blind being lowered.

It took four minutes. The third took nearly five, because the relay dropped and had to begin again, and he sat through that too with his hands folded on one knee, because there was no hurrying a thing that had come so far.

Three typed pages, photographed flat under a lamp. A clean white margin round each. Page numbers at the foot, in the centre: *2*, *3*, *4*.

Then a single line of text, in the plain field below the images, typed in the capital while the city slept.

*The old man knows the voice. There is more. Not yet.*

He read it, and read it again. There was no name in it. She had written *the old man*, which could only mean one person, and *the voice*, which could have meant a great many things and meant, he was certain, only one. She had written *knows*, not *thinks he knows*. Kira did not use verbs she had not tested.

"Received," he said. "All three."

Two seconds.

"I'm going to sleep," said Kira, as though announcing a decision reached after long argument, and the link closed.

---

He read the pages the way he had read editorials for twenty years: first for what they said, and then for what they had been built to do. Three pages, double-spaced, with a wide left margin. The type was an old machine's type, slightly uneven in its pressure, and in every lowercase *e* there was a small bite out of the upper curve, as if the letter had once been dropped. He had seen that bite before. He had sat on the cellar stairs in his head for most of a night, two winters ago, looking at it in a photograph of a torn page.

There was no heading beyond a single word, centred at the top of page two: *INTRODUCTION*. There was no numeral in the lower corner, where page thirty had carried one. There was no mark at the end. The last paragraph simply stopped, and below it was white paper.

He read the opening paragraph three times before he let himself go on to the rest.

*This document assembles principles from seventeen contributors across political theory, systems architecture, social psychology, historical methodology and information security. Our goal is a post-democratic order that prevents the chaos of the Fracture Years while preserving the appearance of participation. Truth, we have learned, cannot be decided by a public. It must be decided for one, and the public must be allowed to believe it decided.*

He sat back.

Outside the window the courtyard lay in pale winter sun, with a bare fig tree in it and a man from the print room smoking on the step. None of it was recorded. Nothing in this country was recorded that a person had not chosen to write down, and after two years he still noticed it, the way a man who has been ill for a long time goes on noticing that he can breathe.

He read the rest. The pages went on in the same voice: calm, orderly, sure of itself, granting each objection before it could be raised and then turning it, gently, into support. It did not rant. It did not flatter. It explained. It sounded like a very good teacher who had decided, with regret and at length, that the class could not be trusted with the syllabus.

Then he went back to the first paragraph, because that was where the story was, and took it apart.

*Assembles.* Not *proposes*. Not *argues*. Someone had gathered these principles from other people and put them in order, and was telling you so.

*Seventeen contributors.* A number. Nobody writing a manifesto gave you a number. People gave numbers when they were keeping accounts.

*Political theory, systems architecture, social psychology, historical methodology and information security.* A list of disciplines. It was the list you would draw up if you were building a state that governed through its record, and needed someone for the machine, someone for the people, someone for the past and someone for the locks.

*Preserving the appearance of participation.* He read those five words for some time.

*The public must be allowed to believe it decided.*

He had spent twenty years listening to how people said words. He listened now to how this one had been written, and what he heard in it was not cruelty. It was relief. The relief of someone who had found an answer to a thing that frightened her, and had written the answer down cleanly, and did not yet know what it would cost.

He did not know why he had thought *her*. He noticed that he had, and filed it, and did not trust it.

---

He walked down to the cellar.

The house on Carver Street had been pulled down years ago, and survived only in his head; but in his head it was perfectly sound. The narrow stairs from the kitchen. The door at the bottom, which had hinges and a handle and a colour he could no longer name. The shelves. The front of the newest one was kept clear, the way an editor keeps one tray on his desk empty for the thing that matters, and on it, side by side, lay two things.

*One.* A photograph of a torn page, number thirty: *When the record fails, the people will believe whoever restores it. Arrange to be the one who restores it.*

*Two.* An old man's song, sent to him in Kira's handwriting with a note that the words were near but not exact: *A people that has lost part of its past will accept any past it is given, provided it is given gently, and by someone it trusts.*

He put the third beside them.

*Three. 3 December 2108. The Introduction, pp. 2–4. Typed, the chipped* e. *No numeral. No mark. Source: the old man, through K.O. Carrier: not stated.*

*One fragment is a curiosity. Two is a pattern. I'll wait for three.* He had written that to Kira two years ago, in the resistance cipher, and meant it exactly. Two made a pattern, and a pattern could always be called coincidence, or forgery, or the fantasy of an outlaw with a grudge and a foreign passport. A third would be something else.

It was something else. The torn page said what to do. The sung essay said what people would accept. Neither of them said who *we* were. The third did. It said *assembles*. It said *seventeen*. It said *our goal*. It said, in the first sentence of the first page, that the thing he and six other people had been put into glass boxes to test had not grown up out of the Fracture Years like a weed out of rubble. Somebody had drawn it. Somebody had sat at a desk with a list of disciplines and a machine with a chipped letter, and designed it.

*The system was built.*

He had known it, he thought, the way a reporter knows a thing before he can print it: on page thirty-one of a procurement leak, at three in the morning, looking at a number. But knowing it and holding the first page of the plan were different, and he let himself feel the difference for exactly as long as it took, and then went back upstairs, because it was not yet noon.

---

The managing editor came in at half past twelve, without knocking, which was a thing he did to indicate that they were friends.

He was a broad, cheerful man of about sixty with reading glasses pushed into his white hair and a habit of standing in other people's doorways with both hands in his pockets, jingling something. Two winters ago, from this building, he had spiked Isaiah's column on the Lost Hour Doctrine, and had the envoy at the mission in Chronos deliver the news; when Isaiah came south he had explained it again in person, warmly: the Sovereignty's readers already knew the Histologic world's reforms were dangerous, and wanted only to be told how. Isaiah had not argued. He had sent the column to a Commissioner in Veridica instead, and it had done what it was for.

He had not expected to like the managing editor, and he did, which he regarded as one of the small professional failures of his exile.

"They told me downstairs," said the managing editor. "Something came in from the lake." He came in and looked over Isaiah's shoulder at the screen without asking, as he looked at everything. He read the first paragraph. His lips moved very slightly on *seventeen*. When he reached the last sentence he took his hands out of his pockets.

"Well," he said. "Well, well."

"It needs to be verified," said Isaiah.

"Of course it needs to be verified." The managing editor straightened up. He was smiling, and the smile was entirely genuine; that was the trouble with him. "Isaiah. Do you see what this is? Forty years they've been telling the world they found the record the way you find a spring in a hillside. Natural. Inevitable. The only sane answer to the Fracture Years. And here it is in their own typing." He tapped the screen, once, over *allowed to believe*. "The Histologic world confesses."

Isaiah looked at him.

He had spent a career listening to how people said words, and he listened to this one. When the managing editor said *confesses*, he did not say it the way people say *admits*, or *tells the truth*. He said it the way people say *surrenders*. There was a war in the word. There was a flag coming down over a building somewhere, and a crowd in a square, and it had nothing to do with three pages typed on a machine with a chipped letter.

"It isn't a confession," said Isaiah. "It's an introduction."

"Same thing, in the right hands."

"That," said Isaiah, "is what worries me."

The managing editor laughed, and clapped him on the shoulder, which Isaiah permitted, and went out saying that they would talk about space on the front page. He left the door open behind him.

Isaiah sat for a moment with his hands folded, looking at the open door.

He was still glad of the Sovereignty. It had taken him in when he had no state, and given him a room with a door that opened from the inside, and his byline back. What it had not given him, and what he had understood from the first day it would not, was indifference. The Sovereignty had been founded to say no to the Histologic world. It needed the Histologic world to deserve the no. A man who arrived with evidence was welcome in exact proportion to what the evidence proved, and the managing editor had just read three pages that proved more than anything Isaiah had brought them in two years.

He was delighted. The delight was the problem.

---

The attaché was in the corridor when Isaiah went down to the archive at two. He was there by coincidence. He said so himself, before Isaiah had asked, which was how Isaiah knew that it was not.

He was a slim man in his forties in a civilian suit that had been cut for a uniform's posture, and he stood as military men stand when they have been told to look as if they were not: a little too easily, with the weight a little too even. He had a courteous, careful voice, and he spoke in paragraphs, each of them complete, as though he had drafted them in advance and was reading them from inside his own head. Isaiah caught himself glancing behind the man's ears, the old Histologic reflex, for the fine white scars of an installation. There were none, of course. Nobody in this building had an interface.

"Mr. Okonkwo. I was visiting your managing editor on another matter. I understand you have received material from the Veridican shore this morning, of some historical interest. My office has a standing interest in such material. We would be grateful for the full set, for assessment."

"There is no set," said Isaiah. "There are three fragments."

The attaché inclined his head, as if Isaiah had corrected his grammar.

"Then the three."

"You may read them," said Isaiah, "when everyone does."

There was a short pause. The attaché did not look offended. He looked like a man making a note in a file, and Isaiah, who had kept files himself for a very long time, recognised the expression and respected it.

"Your position in the Sovereignty," said the attaché pleasantly, "remains conditional, I think."

"It does."

"Conditional," the attaché repeated. He seemed to taste the word. "It's a good word. Very exact."

"It is," said Isaiah, and meant it. It told you precisely what you had been given, and on what terms, and that the terms were not yours. There were not many words left so honest about themselves.

"Then I'll wait," said the attaché. "With everyone."

He smiled, and went down the corridor towards the stairs, and did not go into the managing editor's office on the way, though he had said that was where he was going.

Isaiah stood in the corridor for a moment and filed him. Not in the bedrooms upstairs, where he kept the people he had interviewed, with their words laid out on the pillows. In the study, under the current year, in a drawer he had labelled some months ago without quite admitting to himself why. *Hosts.*

---

In the afternoon he wrote to nobody and made no calls. He sat at the desk with the three pages on the screen and the door closed, and did the arithmetic.

Kira had told him on Saturday, in outline, the way she told things she did not want to say at length. Marcus had been sent to befriend a man in Chronos. The man in Chronos had been sent to befriend Marcus. They had told each other so, in a garden, where the man's interface would keep every word. And now they meant to go public together, the two of them, once they knew what they held: Marcus's orders from his side of the lake, the other man's orders from his, and whatever else could be proved.

Isaiah had asked her one question. *Who else knows?* And she had said, after two seconds that seemed longer, *You.*

He laid it out as he would have laid out the structure of a long piece, in order, striking out every step that was not necessary.

If Marcus published in Veridica alone, Veridica would own it: the work of a damaged man who had been dead once already. If the man in Chronos published alone, Chronos would own it, a grievance useful in a war. If Isaiah published here first, the Sovereignty would own it, and there would be a front page, and a square, and a flag, and three pages written by someone who had been frightened would become a reason for something much worse than fear. The managing editor's face had been a preview. The attaché had been a second one.

He did not want the Sovereignty to own this. He did not want anybody to own it. The only way he could see to prevent that was the oldest trick in his trade, the one every good editor knew and very few ever had the chance to use at this scale: you gave the story to all of them at once. You took away the first hour.

He would publish. He had waited two years for a third fragment, and he would not wait for a fourth. But he would publish in the same hour as the two men on the lake, to the minute if it could be managed, so that when the Sovereignty woke to the story, Veridica and Chronos would already have it in their own words, and when Veridica woke to it, so would everyone else. It would go out here, under his name, and at the same moment through the Synthesis Institute for Historical Methods, whose open channel had carried his account from Wing C in the summer of 2106 and which belonged to no one in particular.

The hour was not set. That was not his to set. It was theirs.

He opened the link at half past five and typed one line, and sent it into the long way round.

*Your hour is mine. Not before.*

He did not sign it. Then he put on his coat, the old one, with its lining full of newsprint, and went home.

---

His rooms were two streets from the paper, up three flights, over a shop that sold notebooks, which he had never once bought. He made himself tea and did not drink it, and sat down in the good chair by the window with his hands folded, and went into the house.

He wrote first sentences in the parlour now.

He had not always. In Wing C the parlour had been a decoy, the room he fed to the machine: warm, true, harmless things laid out for a probe to find and skim, while everything that mattered waited below in the cellar. In the summer of 2106 he had given it away altogether and left it bare.

He had furnished it again over his second winter in the Sovereignty, one thing at a time, the way you make a room habitable after a death in the house. The fire. The long table with its lace runner. The pepper soup, which in the palace was always hot and always just about to be served. His first byline, framed above the fireplace, a small piece about harbour dues that he had been absurdly proud of at eighteen. And he had found, once the room was whole again, that it was the best place in the house to begin anything. A first sentence ought to be written somewhere warm, among true things. It ought to be able to sit at a table with them without embarrassment.

He sat at the table.

He had known for most of the afternoon what the headline would be. He had spent the hours since trying other headlines on it like coats, to be sure. Every one of them had been a statement. Every one of them told the reader what to think, which was precisely what the three pages did, and his pieces did not end on answers. They ended on the question he could not answer.

So the headline was a question too.

He wrote it on a clean sheet in his head, in the plain upright hand he had used since school, and set it on the lace runner in front of him.

***Decided For Us?***

And below it, smaller, the standfirst, which he wrote and struck and wrote again until there was not a word in it he could remove:

*Pages written before the Founding, describing a nation that would be governed through its record, and allowed to believe it chose.*

He read it through. *Before the Founding* was a claim he could stand behind: the pages spoke of the Fracture Years as a thing to be prevented, not a thing survived, and an old man had sung the same voice to Kira from a journal he catalogued in 2063. *Allowed to believe it chose* was the page's own verb, turned by one degree. He had struck out nine versions to find that degree.

It would do. It was not finished, but it would do, and it was honest, and it ended on a question.

He looked up.

At the head of the table, in the chair with its back to the fire, sat his Aunt Sarah.

She had always sat there. She had sat there when he was six and the table had seemed as long as a street, and when he was sixteen and she had talked all through dinner about the diplomatic service and the languages it would need of him, and when he was eighteen and brought home his first byline and she had read it at the table, in silence, and handed it back with one word underlined in pencil. She sat there now, sharp and elegant, in dark silk, with her hands folded on the cloth in front of her in exactly the way he folded his own. The firelight was on one side of her face.

In all the years he had kept the palace, in all the rooms he had built and emptied and filled again, she had never said a word.

She was looking at the headline on the table.

Then she was looking at him.
