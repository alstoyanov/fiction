Prologue: The Doubled Pier

Tuesday, October 30, 2108. Kestrel Point, the claimed strip

Ruth Calloway locked the ferry office at six, as she had every working evening for nineteen years, and then unlocked it again, because she had forgotten the timetable.

It was not the timetable anyone needed. The last sailing of the season was tomorrow, and after that there would be none until April. The one she had forgotten was the summer sheet, which she had taken down that afternoon to make room for the notice about the last sailing. She went back in without putting the light on and pinned it up again in the corner of the window, where it had hung since May. 07:40. 12:10. 17:40. 21:40. It was a stupid thing to do. The window looked wrong without it, that was all, and she had enough in her life that looked wrong.

She locked the door for the second time and tried the handle.

The fog was coming in off the lake, low and gray and smelling of weed and cold iron. It took the far end of Harbour Street first, the quay and the bollards and the black water, then the chandlery, then the church. It was only weather. There had been a time, before the spring, when it had not occurred to her to say so.

She put her hand to the inside pocket of her coat and felt the book there, flat against her ribs, and went up the hill to get the supper on.

The siren went at twenty to ten.

It was the third since Sunday. Ruth was at the sink with her sleeves pushed up and her hands in the water, and when the note began on the harbourmaster's roof, rising and falling, she did the things you did. She turned off the tap. She dried her hands. She looked at the clock on the cooker, which said 21:40, and at the hook by the back door, where Wren's coat was not.

Wren was at her friend's, near the bottom of Harbour Street. She had been told half past nine. She had said half past nine in the voice that meant she had heard the words and had no intention of being governed by them, and Ruth had let it go, because the girl was fourteen and there had been no siren on a Tuesday yet.

Ruth put her coat on over her apron. She did not run; you did not run on the hill in the fog. She went down fast with one hand on the wall of the houses, telling herself, in the voice she used on passengers who had missed the last boat, that the girl would have stayed where she was, as she had been told a hundred times, and would be sitting in somebody's kitchen being given a biscuit.

At the corner by the chandlery the pressure came.

It started behind her eyes, the way it always did, like the moment before a sneeze that does not arrive. Then it spread, and settled, and became something she had no good word for, though she had tried to find one since May. Not pain. A sort of fullness. As though her head had been asked to hold two things in the space meant for one, and was doing it, and would go on doing it whether she liked it or not.

And then she knew about the pier.

She knew that the old pier ran out from the end of Harbour Street into the lake, a hundred and forty metres of tarred boards on iron piles, with lamp posts every twenty paces and a rail you could lean on to watch the boats. She had always known it. She had sat on it as a girl with her feet hanging over and a paper of chips. It was simply there, the way the church was there.

And she knew, with exactly the same certainty, that the pier had been pulled down in the summer of 2089. She had been twenty-seven. She had stood with half the town behind a rope at the end of the quay and watched the cranes lift the boards off in sections, and somebody's child had cried because the lamps were going. She remembered the smell of the tar when they burned the boards on the shingle.

Both were memories. Both were hers. Neither of them would give an inch. She had learned that much since May. They did not give. You did not win. You kept walking.

She saw Wren at the bottom of the street.

She knew her by the walk: hands deep in her pockets, shoulders up round her ears, the unhurried step of a girl who is exactly where she means to be. She was past the last house, on the quay, where the stone ran on for another thirty metres between the bollards and then stopped, square and sudden, with a drop of two metres into black water. There had been a chain across it once. Nobody had replaced it, because nobody had needed to be told where the end of the quay was.

Wren was walking towards it. Not fast. The way you walk along a pier.

Ruth ran.

The street would not keep still. Ahead of her, beyond Wren, the fog lay flat on the water, and there was nothing in it; and then there were lamp posts in it, going out in a line into the gray, their light soft and yellow and very kind; and then there was nothing again. There was no moment of change. It was simply one and then the other, the way a word can look like a word and then like a set of marks, and back. Her feet did not know which harbour they were running down. Her ankle turned on a cobble, and she did not fall.

Something boring, she told herself. Think of something boring.

She had got that from a leaflet the Commission sent round in June, and had thought it the silliest thing she had ever read, and it worked.

Closing time eighteen hundred, she said to herself, not aloud, with her breath going ragged. Till, three hundred and twelve and forty. Seventeen foot passengers on the 12:10. Two cars. One bicycle, not paid for, the Nilsen boy again. Float carried forward. Timetable in the window. The lamps went out along the pier and came back. Receipts in the tin. Tin in the drawer. Drawer locked. Key on the hook. The quay was stone. The quay was stone, and it stopped, and her daughter was four steps from where it stopped. Key on the hook, she said. Key on the hook.

Wren's front foot went out over the edge as if onto a board.

Ruth got her by the back of the coat.

She did not remember deciding to. Her fist was simply in the thick cloth between the girl's shoulders, and she had pulled, hard, backwards, with all her weight, and then they were both sitting down on the wet stone with a force that jarred Ruth's teeth. Wren's heel came down on the edge of the quay and scraped. Below them, close enough to touch with a boot, the water moved against the stone with a small sucking noise, and the fog lay on it, and there was nothing else.

Ruth held on to the coat. She could not have let go if she had tried.

Wren turned her head and looked at her, puzzled, a little put out, the way she looked when her mother came into her room without knocking.

"I was nearly at the lamp," she said.

Her voice was perfectly calm. Ruth looked past her, out over the edge, into the gray.

There was no lamp.

The nearest lit window belonged to the retired engineer and his wife, in the last house before the quay. He had kept the ferry engines running for thirty-one years and had never, in Ruth's hearing, said more than was needed, and he was standing at his open door with a torch when she got Wren up off the stone. His wife was behind him in her dressing gown. She said, "Bring her in, the kettle's on," as though a kettle being on settled everything, and in Ruth's experience it very nearly did.

Their kitchen was small and hot and smelled of toast. There was an oilcloth on the table with a pattern of lemons, faded almost white where the elbows went. The engineer's wife put a blanket round Wren and a mug in her hands, and Wren said "I'm fine," twice, and then began to shake.

It came up through her all at once, now that it was over. The tea slopped in the mug. She looked at it with surprise, as if it belonged to somebody else, and Ruth took it away and held her daughter's hands between her own, which were not much steadier, and nobody said anything for a while.

The storm went the way they did, slowly, like cold going out of a room. It took about twenty minutes. The fullness behind Ruth's eyes thinned, and was a headache, and was only tiredness. When she closed her eyes she could still see the pier, the rail and the tar and the yellow light, and she could still see the cranes lifting it away in the summer she was twenty-seven. Both were still there. She had spoken to enough people since May to know that they would stay. The storm went, and what it had put in you stayed, and you learned to live in a head with two harbours in it.

She would know both piers for the rest of her life. She found she minded less than she would have expected, sitting in a hot kitchen with her daughter's hands going still in hers.

"Well," said the engineer, at last, from the doorway. It was the first thing he had said.

"Yes," said Ruth.

She let go of Wren's hands. She took her coat off the back of the chair, and took the book out of the inside pocket, and laid it on the oilcloth among the lemons.

It did not look like much: a stout notebook with a black cloth cover, the kind the chandlery used to sell for logbooks, a little bigger than a hand and swollen to nearly twice its proper thickness with use. There was a rubber band round it to keep the loose sheets in.

Inside the front cover were four pages that did not belong to the book at all. They had been folded in quarters once, and somebody had pasted them in with flour glue at the start. They were written in a small hand that nobody in Kestrel Point knew, in blue ink, and they were headed, in capitals, HOW TO KEEP A JOURNAL THAT CANNOT BE CHANGED, and under that, smaller, after K. Osman.

A courier had brought them in April, a young man with a rucksack whom nobody knew. He had come in on the 12:10 and sat at the café window for an hour and left the folded pages under his saucer when he went back for the 17:40. By the end of that week nine people had written in a new book, in their own hands, the things the town remembered about itself. Now there were seventeen. Ruth had been the fourth. She had become its keeper in June, when the first keeper's knees went, and she carried it home every night because a book in a coat was harder to take than a book on a shelf.

She turned past the entries of the summer, past other people's storms, to the first clean page. She uncapped her pen. She wrote the way the method said: date first, then time, then place, then what happened, then who saw it. Plain words. No feelings, unless the feeling was a fact.

Tuesday 30 October 2108. Storm siren 21:40. Third since Sunday.

Harbour Street, quay end, at the old pier steps.

W. Calloway (14) walked along the quay in the fog towards where the old pier used to be. She said afterwards she could see the boards and a lamp. R. Calloway came down the hill and caught hold of her coat at the edge of the quay. Her front foot was over the water. We sat down. Nobody hurt except W.'s heel, grazed.

Storm eased off over about twenty minutes. Over by about 22:05.

The pier: I know it is there and I know it was pulled down in 2089. I still know both.

Seen by: R. Calloway.

She stopped, and read it over, and added, Time she got to the edge: 21:50.

She turned the book round on the oilcloth and pushed it across to the engineer. He put on his reading glasses, which lived in his shirt pocket, and read it through slowly, moving his lips a little at the long words, and then took the pen and wrote under it in square capitals, the way engineers write, that he had come to his door at the sound of the siren and seen the two of them sitting on the quay stone, and brought them in. He signed it. He pushed the book to his wife.

His wife read it and frowned.

"It wasn't ten to," she said.

"It was about ten to."

"It was eight minutes to. I looked at the clock when I heard you shout." She pointed at it, over the stove: an old round clock with a second hand that jumped. "I always look at the clock. It said eight minutes to ten."

"I looked at my watch when the siren went," said Ruth. "And I counted. Ten minutes, near enough, to get down the hill."

"Near enough," said the engineer's wife. She did not say it unkindly. She said it the way you might point out a stain on someone's collar.

They looked at each other across the lemons. Not long ago this was the sort of thing that went on for a week, because a time was a fact, and a fact was either right or wrong, and if it was wrong somebody had to be. Ruth could feel the old habit of it in her chest, and could see that the other woman was as tired as she was.

"Put it in," said Ruth. "Your time. In the margin."

The engineer's wife took the pen. In the margin, beside 21:50, in a round hand that sloped backwards, she wrote 21:52 by my kitchen clock, and her initials, and the date. Then she wrote her own short account underneath her husband's, which agreed with his and with Ruth's in everything except the eight minutes, and signed it.

Nobody crossed anything out. You did not cross things out in the book. That was the second rule, after the one about your own hand. You wrote what you saw, and the others wrote what they saw, and where it didn't match you left both, side by side, with a name against each.

Wren had stopped shaking. She had come to stand at Ruth's elbow with the blanket round her shoulders, and was reading the entry upside down, the way she read everything that wasn't meant for her.

"Why did you write the argument in?" she said.

Ruth looked at the page. At her own 21:50, and the other woman's 21:52 beside it, and the two sets of initials.

"Because we both saw it," she said.

Wren considered that. It was the look she gave to a sum she did not think was finished.

"But one of you's wrong."

"Maybe. Maybe we both are. Maybe the clock's slow." The engineer's wife made a small noise at that, and Ruth nearly smiled. "It doesn't matter. What matters is that anybody who picks this up in ten years knows two people saw it and didn't quite agree. That's truer than one of us being sure."

"The broadcasts are sure."

"Yes," said Ruth. "They are."

She did not say the rest of it; the girl had lived through the same summer. Since May the broadcasts had been sure of everything. They were sure the school on Chapel Row had been a school for fifty years and sure it had been a customs shed until the year before last. Each was sure on its own account, and sure the other was lying, and between them they had put four hundred people in hospital along the lakeshore and more than thirty in the ground, on both shores. Everything the town was told contradicted the last thing it had been told. Everything in its own head had a second version standing behind it, just as solid.

The only thing in Kestrel Point that agreed with itself was this book.

And it agreed with itself, Ruth thought, looking at the two times in the margin, precisely because it had never pretended to agree. It was the only record in the town that kept its arguments. Everything else had tidied them away, and been wrong.

She closed the book, and put the rubber band back round it, and put it in her coat.

At the door the engineer's wife pressed a paper of biscuits on Wren, who took it and said thank you in a small voice. The engineer said, "Mind how you go," and then, after a moment, as though it had cost him something, "She'd have been all right. The water's not deep off the end there. Not now."

"No," said Ruth. It was deep enough. They both knew it.

Wren's heel hurt her, so they did not go back up the hill but the long way, along the lakeshore road, which ran flat round the bay to the bottom of their own lane. The lamps along it were the council's, tall and white and a long way apart, each standing alone in a room of fog. Out on the left, past the sea wall, the lake breathed against the stones.

Wren walked close. After a while she put her arm through Ruth's, which she had not done in public since she was eleven. Ruth did not remark on it.

At the far end of the road, where it met the lane, there was a van.

It was parked on the verge, facing out towards the bay, with its engine running and its lights off. That was what she noticed first: the engine, a low even note under the sound of the water, and the dark. Nobody left an engine running in the fog with the lights off. Then she saw its shape. A long gray van with no windows in the back and a short stubby bonnet, the kind the Border Security crews drove, and on its roof, folded flat and strapped down along its length, a mast. A long pale mast, lying down, with something at its tip like a closed hand.

The window of the cab was open a little. There was a man in it, in a dark jacket, drinking something from the lid of a flask. The steam came off it. He did not look at them. He looked out at the bay, at nothing, at the fog, the way a man looks at the view on his break.

Ruth kept walking. She kept her pace exactly as it had been. She felt Wren's arm tighten in hers and she pressed it with her elbow, keep walking, and they went past the van close enough to hear the engine tick, and the man in the cab tipped his flask and drank, and did not look.

She knew what a Border Security van looked like. She had sold its crews ferry tickets every summer for nineteen years. They came in pairs in their gray jackets for the island run, and they were polite, and they paid, and one of them always asked her how the fishing was, as if she would know. She had never seen one of their vans with its mast up. She had never thought about what the mast was for.

At the corner of the lane she looked back, once. The van was only a gray shape now, and a low sound under the water.

She thought about the fullness coming behind her eyes at the chandlery corner, all at once, not creeping the way the old storms had crept when she was a girl, when you felt them coming for half an hour and had time to get indoors. She thought about the man drinking his tea in the dark with his engine running, parked on this side, on the Veridican shore, facing the town.

She did not want to understand it. She understood it anyway, walking up the lane with her daughter's arm through hers and the book against her ribs.

The storm had not come across the lake.

It had come from ours.
