Chapter 16: Fourteen Hundred

Monday, December 17, 2108. Kestrel Point, the claimed strip

The fog had come in before light and stayed. By nine it lay over Harbour Street the way it did in December, low and white and wet, so that the lamp outside the ferry office was a yellow smear and the end of the street was nothing at all.

It was real fog. Ruth had lived forty-six winters beside the lake, and she knew the difference now in her body, the way she knew the weight of a full till drawer. Fog was cold on the backs of her hands. It had a smell, iron and weed and somebody's chimney. The other thing had no smell. The other thing started behind the eyes.

She unlocked the office at half past eight, on winter hours, and put the kettle on the ring in the back room. The summer timetable was still in the window, faded to the colour of weak tea: 07:40, 12:10, 17:40, 21:40. Beside it, since November, when she had moved it up from the noticeboard, hung the winter board, which was one line long.

FREIGHT. Mon–Fri 15:00. Sat 10:40.

She took the Kestrel Book out of her coat and opened it at the back. The back pages were hers: onsets, in three columns ruled with the edge of a ticket, time, street and duration. For five months the first column had matched the summer board to the minute, even after the last sailing on October thirty-first. Then, in the last week of November, the summer times had stopped, and the storms had started keeping the freight hours instead. Fifteen hundred, weekdays. Ten-forty, Saturdays. She had written the change down with the date she noticed it, and had made herself not think about what it meant that the storms could read her window.

Today's line was already ruled in pencil. Mon 17 Dec. 15:00. Space left for the street and the duration.

At a quarter to ten she took Wren's coat down from the hook and hung it over the chair by the stove to warm. She had done it every weekday since the school shut in October. At three she would walk home with it over her arm and sit Wren at the kitchen table with her back to the wall, away from the window, where there was nothing to walk towards. It wasn't a plan. It was a habit. In Ruth's experience habits got you through the parts of life that plans didn't reach.

At twenty past one somebody knocked on the glass, although the sign said open.

A young woman, twenty perhaps, in a knitted hat and a gray scarf wound three times round her neck, with frost on the wool where her breath had caught it. Her boots were wet to the ankle, the way you got them walking the shore path from the south. She had a canvas bag across her body and held it against her hip as if it were heavier than it looked.

"Mrs. Calloway? The keeper?"

"Yes."

The young woman took out a stiff envelope, the big kind the Ministry used for tax returns, sealed along every edge with tape and heavy with paper. Across the front, in black capitals:

NOT BEFORE 14:00, MONDAY 17 DECEMBER. FOR THE KESTREL BOOK.

No name. No return address.

"What is it?"

"I don't know. Truly. I was given six and told the towns." She held out a folded slip and a pencil stub. "Would you sign? They said you'd want to."

It was a hand-ruled receipt, with the date and the town already written in. Ruth looked at the clock and wrote 13:22 and her name. Then she turned the slip round and pushed it back with the pencil on top.

"You too. Under mine."

The young woman looked at her for a moment, and smiled, and signed in a quick round schoolroom hand. Ruth read the name and didn't keep it. She had the feeling it wasn't the girl's own, and that it didn't matter.

"There are four more towns on the shore," the courier said, winding her scarf. "I'd better not stop."

"The path's bad past the breakwater. Keep up by the fence."

The girl nodded as if she had been told that in every town so far, and went out into the white. The bell rang once behind her.

Ruth turned the envelope over. The tape on the back had been pressed down with a thumb all along its length, by someone who wanted to know if it had been lifted. She put it inside her coat, against the book, and locked the ferry office at half past one on a Monday for the first time in nineteen years.

Word got down Harbour Street before her. She never learned how. By the time she had walked up the hill with the envelope and the book in her coat and put the kettle on at home, there were people in her kitchen.

The engineer and his wife from the end of the street, who had given them tea on the night of the pier. The rope-seller from the chandlery, a big man with a cough. The two sisters from the bakery, still in their flour. The schoolmaster, who had no school. Old Mrs. Aske from the church, with her stick. Emmet Varley, pink with cold and pleasant, handing round a tin of biscuits before anyone had asked. Others. Ruth counted without meaning to, the way she counted passengers up a gangway. At 13:55 there were fourteen people in a kitchen built for four, one of them on a chair borrowed from next door, and the windows had begun to run.

Wren sat on the counter by the sink with her knees up, which she was too old for, and watched them with the flat, interested look she had worn since October. She had nearly walked off the end of the world that night and said afterwards, perfectly calmly, I was nearly at the lamp. Ruth thought the look was the look of someone who had stopped expecting things to be only one way.

The kitchen screen was on, the sound low. The state channel's midday bulletin.

"It'll be from her," said one of the bakery sisters. "Osman. The one who came to the church hall."

"She had the look of someone who'd send you nothing," said the rope-seller, "and mean it kindly."

Ruth opened the book to a clean page and wrote Mon 17 Dec 2108, 13:58, the Calloway kitchen. Received by hand of a courier, 13:22, a sealed envelope marked Not before 14:00. Opened before witnesses. Then she wrote every name in the room in a column, and the room watched her do it and didn't say anything, because that was how the book worked.

At 13:59 the screen changed. Nobody had touched it. The bulletin cut away mid-sentence to a gray view of broad pale steps and a crowd at their foot. Everyone in the country knew the steps. The Justice Center. A thin man in a dark coat and glasses stood at the top, a little to one side, as if he didn't want to take up more of the steps than he needed. A man with a very still face stood a pace behind his shoulder and watched the crowd.

"That's Chen," said Varley. "The commissioner."

The clock in the screen's corner went to 14:00.

Ruth tore the tape.

Afterwards she couldn't put it all in order, which bothered her more than she expected. The pages went round the table hand to hand while the voice from the screen went on over them, and what she remembered was not a sequence but a pressure in the room that had nothing to do with the other kind.

They heard the orders before they read them. The thin man read them aloud in a level clerk's voice, the voice of a man reading a manifest, and the copy went round the kitchen a line behind him, so that people's eyes kept lifting from the paper to the screen and back, checking. OPERATION KINDRED. A cover sheet with no signature line. A historian of the Chronos Temporal Archive, thirty-four, who lived alone. A key. A list of questions to ask him, to make him trust you. Who still writes to you?

"They wanted him to make a friend of a man," said Mrs. Aske, "so as to rob him."

"To end the war," said Varley, and, when people looked at him, "I'm only saying what it says. Within a month."

"I refused it," said the man on the steps, "in a place where my refusal was recorded by the other side. The man I was sent to befriend was sent to befriend me. He is publishing his orders this hour, from his own record. I am reading mine."

And nobody cut him off.

Ruth half noticed it, the way you notice a sound that has stopped. This was the state channel, a commissioner reading the government's secret orders on the Justice Center steps, and nobody in any control room had pulled the picture back to the man at the desk. Someone has decided to let this run, she thought, and then the next sheets came into her hands and she stopped thinking it.

Three typed pages, numbered 2 to 4, photographed and printed. Every e had a small nick out of its top, the same nick over and over, like a chipped tooth. The schoolmaster read a sentence aloud, then read it again more slowly, like an examination answer he wasn't sure how to mark.

"…Truth, we have learned, cannot be decided by a public. It must be decided for one, and the public must be allowed to believe it decided."

"Decided for one," said a bakery sister, as if tasting it.

Then PRINCIPLE THREE, five pages with an X in every corner, read out by the engineer in the flat voice he had spent thirty-one years explaining pumps in. "…Should insiders reform it, it may be restored from the true source."

Someone laughed. It was the rope-seller: one bark, very loud in the small room, the most horrible sound Ruth had heard since the night of the pier. He put his big hand over his mouth afterwards, as if it had got out without him. Nobody asked why. Everyone in that kitchen had at some time gone to the factorepo about something it said had never happened, and been told kindly that the record was the record.

A strip of paper, photographed against dark cloth to show how narrow it was: three torn lines, only their right-hand ends. IV …… 18. V …… 23. VI …… 29. Wren leaned down off the counter and said, "It's a contents page." The schoolmaster asked how she knew. "It's the shape of one."

A torn typed sheet numbered 30, with a line in the middle about whoever restores the record.

And the handwritten page. You could see the hand through the photograph: small, slanting, very precise, a young woman's hand that had been taught to write on lines and stopped needing them. 40 in the corner. March 2066 at the foot. It went round the table in silence, which nothing else had done all afternoon.

Distribute truth. Distribute verification. Accept uncertainty. Build by many hands.

"That's us," said the engineer's wife. "Isn't it? That's what we do."

The last six sheets had reached Wren.

PRINCIPLE FIVE, and a long heading, and an X in every corner. Wren read the heading to herself, then slid off the counter and stood the way they had made her stand in school, feet together, pages held up at the level of her chin.

Nobody had asked her to. The room went quiet anyway.

She read in her school voice, clear and rather too loud, with the stress in the wrong places, the way children read things nobody has told them the meaning of.

"External threats unify more reliably than internal messaging. Fact warfare between Histologic nations is not failure but function, so long as it remains a war of facts and not of blood. Border regions of broadcast overlap should be preserved, not resolved: they are where a population's tolerance for contradiction may be measured, and raised."

Nobody said anything.

Ruth thought of the pier. Not the telling of it in the church hall in November. The pier itself, both of it: the one with the lamp posts and the tarred boards that had always stood there, and the one the cranes pulled down when she was twenty-seven while she watched from the crowd. Wren's shoes on the wet stone, and her front foot already over the water, stepping onto boards her mind could see.

She thought of the back pages. 07:40, 12:10, 17:40, 21:40. The summer board, and then the winter board, and the storms keeping both as neatly as she kept them herself.

Measured.

Someone had stood at the end of the lakeshore road with a van and a flask and a timetable, and written down how much a town could take.

"Who's the population?" said the rope-seller.

Wren lowered the pages and looked round the kitchen at all of them. She said it as if it were obvious, which it was.

"Us."

At a quarter past two Ruth went out without her coat.

She didn't decide to. She was in the street, walking fast in the white, the cold going straight through her cardigan. Down Harbour Street past the shuttered chandlery and the locked ferry office, to where the street ended and the lakeshore road ran off along the top of the shingle, and the fog stood on the water like a wall.

The van was there.

It had stood there, or at the far end of the road by the bottom of her own lane, each time since October that she had gone out before a storm to look: gray, unmarked except for the small Border Security crest on the driver's door, two wheels on the verge, engine running, lights off. Ruth had sold its crews ferry tickets every summer for the island run. In October she had understood which side the storms came from. Some of them. Enough.

The engine was running now. There was somebody in the cab. And the mast on the roof was up, standing straight into the white, taller than she had ever seen it, because she had only ever seen it folded.

She stood on the shingle and watched, and didn't know what she meant to do.

Then the mast came down. It came down slowly, in sections, with a small whine of motors, and folded itself flat along the roof. The man in the cab lifted a handset and spoke into it; she saw his lips move and heard nothing. The lights came on, two yellow smears. The van pulled off the verge, its wheels grinding shingle, and went away inland up the lakeshore road.

Ruth listened to it go. She listened until she couldn't tell whether she was hearing it or remembering it, and then a while longer, until she was sure, and her arms had gone numb.

At three o'clock the kitchen waited.

Nobody had gone home. At ten to three the talk stopped without anyone stopping it, and people began to look at the clock. The engineer's wife held her husband's hand on the table. Mrs. Aske closed her eyes. Wren sat at the table with her back to the wall, where Ruth had put her, in her own coat, zipped to the chin, because Ruth had come in blue with cold and put it on her without a word, and Wren had let her.

15:00.

Ruth sat with her hands flat on the book and waited for it. She knew how it came. A pressure behind the eyes, as if someone were leaning gently on the inside of her skull. Then the doubling: not a picture but a certainty, two certainties, each as solid as her name. It never came slowly. It came like a switch.

15:01. The clock on the wall. The tap dripping into the sink. The small catch in the engineer's breathing. Behind her eyes only the dull ordinary ache of a long day and too much tea.

15:04. Somebody let out a breath. The rope-seller began very quietly to cry, wiped his face with his palm and said, "It's the tea. It's hot." Nobody contradicted him.

At 15:20 Ruth turned to the back pages, found the pencilled line Mon 17 Dec. 15:00, and wrote none in the column for the street and none in the column for the duration. Then she sat looking at the two words for a long time. It was the first line in five months she hadn't known how to fill.

The six o'clock bulletin read it without expression. Border Security, lakeshore command. All transmitter units stood down, 14:05 today. Statement attributed to Captain Y. Sokolov. One line. Then the price of fuel.

"Who's Sokolov?" said a bakery sister. Nobody knew.

14:05, Ruth thought. Five minutes after the steps. Somebody had heard the first page and made up her mind in five minutes. Or had made it up before, and waited for the hour.

There was more, in pieces. In Chronos, a historian of their Temporal Archive had published orders of his own, from his own life-log, under some Chronos right the bulletin gave a long name and didn't explain. In the Sovereignty, over the sea, a newspaper had printed the same pages, and the bulletin put its headline on the screen, white on black, like an exhibit: DECIDED FOR US?

Then the kitchen argued.

It was full of people who had been told for five months that the storms were the other side's attacks, and had half known otherwise, and had been told this afternoon, in their own government's typeface, that they were the measurement. They argued the way people argue in a kitchen: loudly, over each other, with their mouths full. The schoolmaster thought the pages were forged. The engineer said you didn't stand down a whole command on a forgery. Varley said reasonably that perhaps the government had known all along and this was its way of telling people gently, and the rope-seller said something that made Mrs. Aske say language. Somebody said it had always been the Chronos people's doing, and somebody else asked why our van had gone home, then.

Ruth wrote it down.

The entry itself was hers, and plain: the courier, the envelope, the steps, the pages, the van, the storm that didn't come. She read it out and the room countersigned it, one name under another. But all down the margins she held the book out to whoever was talking and let them write their disagreement beside it in their own hand, as the book always had. Forged, in my opinion, in the schoolmaster's neat slope. Not forged. You don't stand down for a forgery, in the engineer's square capitals. I think they knew and are telling us kindly, in Varley's round, eager hand, and he countersigned the engineer's line too, before anyone asked him. They weren't telling us anything, the van was, from the rope-seller, pressing so hard the nib went through.

Nothing crossed out. By nine o'clock the margins were fuller than the page, and the page was still true.

At 23:40 the other channel came on.

It came across the water, and the town wasn't meant to watch it, and did. Most people had gone. The engineer and his wife were still there, and Varley asleep upright in the borrowed chair, and Wren asleep across two kitchen chairs pushed together, with Ruth's coat on top of her own.

A woman's pale, composed face, speaking the Chronos way, every sentence evenly timed. The Judge, she said, had read the collective will in the logs of millions. The collective will had changed. The Judge had determined, at zero point eight eight, that corrective broadcasting no longer served the collective interest.

Then the channel went quiet. Not dark: a still frame of the Chronos crest on gray, and no sound at all.

"What does that mean?" said the engineer's wife.

"It means they've stopped too."

"Both sides."

"Both sides."

At the door the engineer's wife turned back. "Ruth. You'll write it." Ruth said she would.

It was the first day since May that a storm had not come when it was due.

She sat at the kitchen table with the book under the one lamp. The house was quiet except for Varley's breathing from the chair, and Wren's from the two chairs, slow and deep, the breathing of a child making up for a long time without proper sleep. Outside the window the fog was only fog. She could see the lamp at the corner through it, one lamp, where it had always been.

She wrote the last line of the day under the countersignatures.

Today the war stopped. Witnesses: seventeen.

Seventeen was the book: the seventeen who had written in it since April. It wasn't the fourteen in the kitchen and the borrowed chair. It wasn't the crowd at the foot of the steps, or the four more towns down the shore where a girl in a gray scarf had knocked, or the millions whose logs the Chronos Judge had read.

She crossed out seventeen and wrote everyone.

Then she looked at that for a long time, and it was worse. It was the kind of word the pages had been full of. A public. A population. Everyone was nobody in particular. Everyone hadn't signed. You couldn't ask everyone whether they recognised their own hand.

The book didn't cross things out. That was the rule that made it work. She had already crossed out once, in her own entry, on the night the war stopped.

She drew a single line through everyone, so that it could still be read underneath, beside the other, because a book that hid its crossings-out could hide anything.

Then she wrote, in her ordinary hand, seventeen, again.
