Chapter 30: Two Hands

Thursday, February 14, 2109. Nikolai and Maya's flat, and Marcus's flat, Veridica Capital

At 04:50 the comparison finished, and the screen said what it said.

Nikolai did not move. He sat at the kitchen table with his hands flat on either side of the keyboard, the way he had sat at a desk on the fourteenth floor with a copy of his own brother's pinpricks in front of him, and read the result once, and then read the method it had run, line by line, from the top, because a result is only as good as the thing that produced it and he had built the thing himself.

The flat was dark except for the screen and the small lamp he had clipped to the shelf above the table. The radiator under the window knocked twice and went quiet. In the bedroom Maya was asleep. He could hear her breathing through the half-open door, slow and even, a sound no log in this country recorded.

He read the method to the end. It was sound. He had known it would be. He had checked it on Tuesday and again on Wednesday night, before he let it run, because he had known since Tuesday what it was going to say, and he had wanted, very badly, to find that he had built it wrong.

Match, said the screen. And under that, the numbers.

He looked at his hands.

Fifteen months.

It had started in November 2107 with a booking on another nation's maintenance tables: a window on a primary core, some weeks ahead, and an owner field left blank. He had shown it to the seven in a blind spot in the plaza colonnade on the Day of Testimony, and Kira had looked at the structure of it and said, It's the same hand, and none of them had said the name. Four weeks later, at twelve minutes past three in the morning on the thirteenth of December, the window had opened, and four million people on the far shore of the lake had lost seventeen minutes.

That was the first hand. It had never been in doubt.

In January 2108 Chronos had published its own analysis of the Gap, in full, to show the world what Veridica had done to it. Chronos believed in publishing things. The analysis laid the seam out in forensic detail: the chain re-sealed backwards from the far edge, the tombstones in the order of removal. Kira had read it at the kitchen table and recognised it within the hour. The same backward stitch that had taken her fact, typed a seventy-three onto Marcus's file, and erased a Veridican afternoon. She had written a technical note that said resembles, and filed it, and none of them had said the name then either.

It had never been enough.

Nikolai had known that from the first week. The seam was Wei's, but the Gap was not on a Veridican core. It was inside Chronos's primary, the master record of a nation, and nothing was sealed inside Chronos's primary on one signature. Every sealed block there carried a countersignature from the Chronos side, an authorisation made inside the country, by a key the country trusted. The Gap had one. It was there in Chronos's own analysis, printed in full, and described by Chronos's analysts as an intrusion.

He had had its shape for fifteen months. He had never had its grammar. A countersignature is a set of habits, and habits cannot be read without something to read them against. He had nothing. Chronos did not publish its keys.

In December he had been given one.

The slip was in the back pocket of the gray folder, the one labelled NOT OURS that lived in the bottom drawer under the spare cables. He took it out now and laid it beside the keyboard, under the lamp.

A narrow printed slip: a single line of text, and under it a countersignature block.

Saltmarsh Crossing, garden, 13 Dec 2108.

Marcus had brought it to him on Friday the fourteenth of December, the day after the talks ended, in an envelope, and told it in order, the way Marcus told things. The bench under the pear tree, in fog. Julian taking out the senior reconciliation key of the Chronos Temporal Archive, unasked, a small flat gray token, and opening a session on his tablet in the garden where his log recorded every second. So you can see there is nothing behind it. One harmless test entry, one line, countersigned under the key, and the slip out of the little printer in his bag, handed across. Marcus had held it by its edges. He had never touched the token. He had laughed, because Julian had made a joke about the date: it was a year to the day since the Gap, and his log for those seventeen minutes showed him asleep, like everyone. I've never slept so well.

"It was the most honest thing anyone did at the talks," Marcus had said. "You keep copies."

Nikolai had kept copies. He had put the slip in the back pocket of the folder, behind fourteen pages of notes on the countersignature he could not read, and he had taken it out once, in Christmas week, only to check a date, and put it back.

He had not compared them. Not because he did not think of it. Because one signature was a sample, not a profile, and a single sample matched against anything gave you nothing but the pleasure of seeing what you had hoped to see. He did not trust that pleasure. He had lived inside what if all his life.

But the slip had taught him what to look for.

The key had habits. They were not in the cryptography, which was clean and standard and could have been anyone's. They were in the way the key did its work. It closed every sealed block with a short empty field after the last entry, two bytes where the Chronos standard asked for none. And it waited before it signed. Between the moment the block was sealed and the moment the countersignature was laid on it, there was an interval, and the interval was never less than four hundred milliseconds and never more than four hundred and nine. Some particular piece of hardware, reading some particular key, at some particular speed, every time. It was as individual as a slant in a hand.

One slip was one sample. He needed hundreds.

The Archive gave them to him. Every night, for years, it had published a public notice of its backup reconciliation, primary and backups concordant, countersigned under the senior key, so that four million people could see for themselves that their past had been kept. It was the most reassuring document in Chronos. Nobody read it. Nikolai had spent January reading every one he could reach. Four hundred and twelve notices, going back years. Every one closed its blocks with the same two empty bytes. Every one waited between four hundred and four hundred and nine milliseconds before it signed.

By the first week of February he had a profile.

On Tuesday he had set the profile against the countersignature in Chronos's analysis of the Gap. He had not run it. He had only looked, the way you look at the two halves of a torn page before you let yourself fit them together. And he had known.

Tonight he had let it run.

Match.

The countersignature on the Chronos Gap had been made by the senior reconciliation key of the Chronos Temporal Archive.

There was one key. One man held it. Chronos trusted him with four million people's past.

Nikolai made himself say the rest of it in his head, in order, plainly, the way he would have had to say it to Dr. Tanaka, who would want to know exactly.

The key was used. That is what this shows. It does not show whose hand held it. A key can be lent. A key can be taken. A key can be used by someone standing behind the man who holds it. I do not know which. I want to say that clearly, to myself, first.

He said it. It did not help as much as it should have.

He opened the other file.

It was the sample trace from Chronos's own analysis, the one they had printed to show what the Gap looked like from inside a single life: an anonymous sleeper in the lakeshore province, the night of the thirteenth of December 2107, from 03:05 to 03:36. He had looked at it a hundred times. He looked at it now with the slip beside it.

From 03:05 to 03:11, the log recorded a night. Breath by breath, each one a small rise and fall on the trace. A turn at 03:08. The heart rate going up and down like a tide. A Chronos sleep log was the most intimate document in the world. It kept every breath you had ever taken in the dark.

At 03:12 the trace went flat.

Not dead. Not the flatline Morrison pushed through a relay when a living person needed to become a dead one. Flat. The last state carried over, minute after minute, unchanging. Asleep, heart rate steady. No breaths. No turns. No night at all. Seventeen minutes of a number held still, and at 03:29 the breathing started again as if nothing had happened.

Four million logs said that. Julian's was one of them. He had said so himself, in the garden, as a joke about the date.

Nikolai set the flat stretch beside the minutes on either side of it and looked at the difference until his eyes ached.

The breaths were a record. The flat stretch was not. It did not record sleep. It recorded nothing and labelled it sleep. It was filler, and nobody could check filler, because there was nothing in it to check. A man whose log said asleep for those seventeen minutes had no more proof of where he had been than a man whose log said nothing at all.

And a Chronos log could not lie. That was the country's faith. It could lie only if someone held the true source and could reach inside the primary and seal what they wished there. A key like this was how you held it.

The key that could make his log lie was his.

Nikolai sat back from the table.

If Julian had kept the key in his pocket in the garden, there would have been no slip. Without the slip, no habits. Without the habits, no profile, and the countersignature would still be a shape without a grammar, and Nikolai would still be looking at it once a month and putting it back. Julian had taken the key out because he wanted Marcus to see there was nothing behind it. He had signed a line about a garden because he was proud of being open. Every step that led here was a step he had taken to be trusted.

His openness was what implicated him. There was no way to say that which did not make it worse.

Nikolai looked at his hands for a long time.

He made copies. He always had.

Three: the result and the method and the slip and the four hundred and twelve notices. One on the drive he kept zipped in his wallet, beside the folded primer page and the copy of the primer page that Wei had once put on his desk. One in the flat, in the place under the kitchen floor that Maya knew about and did not mention. One in a third place, which he did not let himself picture, in case picturing it was a kind of saying.

For fifteen years it had been the one thing that felt like safety: a copy nobody knew about, a way for the truth to survive whatever happened to him. It was the whole method now, three books checking each other in three towns.

Tonight it did not feel like safety. It felt like building something in his own kitchen, piece by piece, very carefully, that could be pointed at a man.

He sat with his hand on the zipped wallet.

"Many hands," he said aloud, to the dark kitchen. "Very good. Now there are three copies of the worst thing I know."

The radiator knocked. Nobody laughed. He had not expected anyone to.

Maya got up at half past six.

She came into the kitchen in his old jumper with her hair flattened on one side, and stopped in the doorway, and looked at him, and at the lamp, and at the screen, and at the slip under the lamp, and her face went through three expressions in about a second and settled on none of them.

"You've been up all night."

"Since two." He did not look up. "It finished at ten to five."

She did not ask what had finished. She had watched him with the gray folder for fifteen months. She came and stood beside him and did not touch the keyboard.

"I need you to check the timings," he said. "All of them. The notices, the slip, the countersignature in the analysis. The four hundred to four hundred and nine. The two bytes." He looked at his hands. "And I need you not to tell me what you think until you're finished. Please. I don't trust myself on this one. I want to say that clearly."

Maya looked at him for a moment longer. Then she went and put the kettle on, and came back, and sat down in the chair he got up from, and pulled her knees up under the jumper, and began.

She worked the way she always worked, fast, prickly, with times in every sentence, and she worked in silence, which she never did. At 07:12 she asked for the raw notices, not his extracts. At 08:40 she found one notice from the autumn of 2105 where the interval was four hundred and ten milliseconds, and he felt his whole body lift, and at 08:43 she found that the notice had been republished after a correction and the original was four hundred and six, and he sat down again. At 09:30 she made toast and did not eat it. At 10:15 she took the countersignature from the Gap analysis apart byte by byte and rebuilt it by hand on a sheet of paper, with a pencil, because she wanted to see it without a machine between her and it.

At 11:20 she put the pencil down.

"It holds," said Maya.

He had known. He found that knowing had not prepared him at all.

She sat with her hands flat on the sheet of paper, in the same position he had sat in at five that morning, and he saw her notice it and not move them.

"I hate that it holds," she said. She said it furiously, and very gently, both at once, the way she said the worst things. "I hate it. I wanted you to have built it wrong."

"So did I."

"I know." She wiped her eyes with the heel of her hand, angrily, as if they had done it without permission. "I timed it. It holds."

He sat with it all day.

He did not go to Kira. Kira was at the farmhouse with the second round of copies of the Reply and would not be back until Saturday, and he could have reached her by the line in half an hour. He did not. Julian was Adrian's brother. Kira had looked at Julian's handwriting under a lamp two weeks ago and seen, for a beat, another hand; Nikolai had watched her do it. He was not going to be the one who put this in front of her on a crackling line in a farmhouse kitchen with Elise Vance at the stove.

And it was Marcus's. Marcus had sat on the bench. Marcus had been handed the slip, and laughed at the joke, and brought it to Nikolai because Nikolai kept copies. On Tuesday, over coffee, Marcus had said of Saltmarsh Crossing only, He thanked me, and then had not been able to say anything for a while.

Nikolai wanted Marcus to hear it first. In person. From someone who would not pretend it was simpler than it was.

Through the afternoon he felt his brothers the way he always did, the way you know where your own hand is in the dark. Dmitri, a steady cold weight at the edge of him, on duty somewhere by the lake. Alexei, far off and restless, a faint pressure behind the eyes. Neither of them knew. The bond did not carry facts. It only carried where they were. For once he was glad.

At a quarter to nine he put on his coat and went out.

Marcus opened the door in his shirtsleeves, with his glasses pushed up on his forehead and a pencil behind his ear, and looked at Nikolai's face and stood back to let him in without saying anything.

The flat was quiet. Kira's coat was not on its hook. The lists were on the table, unfolded, with the pencil's twin beside them, and the window over the table was dark.

Nikolai did not take his coat off. He sat at the table and took out the wallet and did not open it, and laid it out in order, plainly, the way he would have written it.

The first hand. The seam, Wei's, the same as the Lost Hour, recognised by Kira within an hour in January last year, never enough on its own. The countersignature inside Chronos's primary, which nothing could be sealed without, and which nobody could read. The slip. The habits: two empty bytes, four hundred to four hundred and nine milliseconds. Four hundred and twelve public notices. The profile. The match, at ten to five that morning. Maya's check, finished at twenty past eleven.

He told him about the sample trace, the breaths either side, and the flat stretch between, which recorded no night at all. He told him what that meant about any log that said asleep. He told him what a key like this could do to a log.

Then he said the thing he had been saying to himself since five.

"I want to say clearly that I don't know if he did it." He looked at his hands. "I know his key did."

Marcus said nothing.

He sat very still across the table, with the lists in front of him and the pencil in his fingers, and he did not say anything for a long time. Nikolai waited. He had learned, in Wing C and after, that Marcus needed silence the way other people needed air, and that it was not the same thing as not listening.

When Marcus spoke at last it was very quietly, and not really to Nikolai. It came out in pieces, like entries being read back.

"He took it out himself. I didn't ask."

A pause.

"He said there was nothing behind it."

Another.

"He said he'd never slept so well." Marcus looked down at the lists. "I laughed."

And then, lower still, so that Nikolai almost did not hear it: "He thanked me."

That was all. He did not say it can't be. He did not say I knew. He did not defend Julian and he did not accuse him. He sat with the four facts laid out between them on the table, and looked at them, and did not put anything else beside them, and Nikolai, who had been braced all day for one or the other, found that this was worse and also that it was right.

At twenty past nine Nikolai stood up.

"I have three copies," he said. "You know where one is if you need it."

Marcus nodded. He did not get up. Nikolai went to the door and put his hand on it, and then turned, because something in the room had changed.

Marcus had risen and gone to the window.

He stood with his back to the room, looking out over the dark capital towards the lake, which could not be seen from here at night or at any other time, and towards the other shore beyond it, which could not be seen from anywhere in Veridica, and which was there all the same.

"Two hands," he said. "One on each side."

Nikolai waited for the rest of it.

It did not come. Marcus stood at the window and did not say it, and Nikolai did not say it either, though he had been carrying it since ten to five that morning, and he knew it was the same sentence, because there was only one sentence it could be.

Then the war was never between them.

Nobody could prove that. Nikolai could not even prove the half of it he held. It was a guess, and he knew what guesses did to evidence.

Marcus turned from the window. He sat down at the table again and pulled the lists towards him and took the pencil, and he did not go to CHECKED. He went to the middle column, UNCHECKED, and wrote in his small precise historian's hand, and when he had finished he turned the sheet round, and Nikolai came back from the door to read it, the way he offered every entry to a witness if there was one.

Feb 14, 2109. Chronos Gap, Dec 13, 2107, 03:12–03:29. Two hands: Wei's seam; the Archive's key. Holder of the key: Kovač, J.

He looked at the line for a long time. Then he turned the sheet back and added one more word, with a question mark, and Nikolai watched the pencil make it.

Julian?

"Unchecked," said Marcus.

It was 21:30. Nikolai opened the door. Behind him, very quietly, as if to the paper, Marcus said one more word.

"Yet."
