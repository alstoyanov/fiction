Chapter 29: Thank You

Monday, February 11, 2109. The Old Customs House, Saltmarsh Crossing, the neutral zone; the Justice Center, Veridica Capital

February eleventh, 2109. Ten hundred hours. Old Customs House, Saltmarsh Crossing, east conference room. Involved parties: Chen, M.; Kovač, J.; Park, J.; Aubry, C.; delegations. Purpose: closing session, Lakes Talks. Status of the war: ended, by determination, December seventeenth.

There was nothing left to negotiate, and so the last session of the Lakes Talks took exactly as long as the first.

Marcus had expected that. He had been in enough rooms by now to know that the less a meeting had to decide, the more carefully it was conducted. The long table in the east room had been laid as it was laid in November: the Synthesis flag at the head, water jugs, notepads nobody would write on, the two delegations facing each other across polished oak under a ceiling that had been built to shelter customs officers from the marsh wind two hundred years before anyone had thought of recording a life. The walls were shielded. The Chronos delegates had come through the door that morning with the small involuntary pause Marcus had learned to watch for, the half-step of people walking into a deafness, and had sat down with the faces of people who could not remember the next hour and were being polite about it.

Professor Park Ji-won presided. She was small and white-haired and warm, and she had had a pot of tea brought to the table before anything else was said, as she had every day since November.

"I think," she said, "that we may call this a successful winter." She smiled round the table. "Not because of anything that happened in this room. Perhaps in spite of it." The Veridican delegation laughed. The Chronos delegation smiled, which was their laughing.

She read the closing statement. It noted that the corrective broadcasting of both nations had ceased on the seventeenth of December, and that the Chronos determination of that night and the Veridican stand-down of that afternoon had together brought the fact war to an end, and that the Talks had "provided a venue". It noted nothing else. It had been drafted by three secretariats over two weeks, and it had the smoothness of a stone that has been in a river.

Administrator Constance Aubry spoke for Chronos, coolly and courteously, with an expression of attention that did not change when she spoke. She thanked the Synthesis for its hospitality. She thanked the secretariats. Then she turned her head a precise number of degrees and looked down the table at Marcus.

"And the Veridican delegation," she said, "for a very informative winter."

Marcus inclined his head. He did not look at Julian, three places down from her, and Julian did not look at him. Aubry's eyes rested on him a moment longer, not unkindly, the way a statistician looks at a result that came in outside the expected range. Then she moved on.

On the panel in the Justice Center his index stood at 0.44. Everyone at the table knew it. It had been in every Veridican paper on Tuesday and every Chronos digest by Wednesday. Nobody mentioned it. He found that he was grateful, and then that he was a little ashamed of being grateful, and filed both.

Professor Park closed the session at eleven forty. "Perhaps," she said, and stopped, and laughed at herself, and did not finish the sentence.

Julian was waiting in the garden.

He had not arranged it. There had been nothing to arrange. At every break since November the aide had steered them towards the shielded rooms and Julian had steered them out, and this last time there was no aide, and no break, only the end of a thing, and Marcus walked out through the side door into the cold and found him on the bench under the pear tree, as if he had been there all winter.

The garden was a walled square behind the Customs House, with gravel paths and brick walls gone soft with lichen. In November it had been full of fog. Today the fog had lifted, and a thin sun lay across the gravel, and the frost was going off the grass in patches where the light reached. The pear tree stood against the south wall with its branches trained flat along wires, bare, every twig in the same plane.

He reached for the word for it. It had been there in December. It was gone now, a smooth place.

"Espalier," said Julian, without looking up. "You're doing the face."

"I'm doing the face," Marcus agreed, and sat down.

Everything Julian heard here went into his log. Marcus had known that since the first morning, and in November it had been the whole point: the garden was where Aubry wanted every word, and where, in the end, Marcus had put the one word he could not take back. He did not mind it any more. He had come to think of the garden as the only room at Saltmarsh Crossing where nobody could lie about what had been said.

December thirteenth, 2108. Saltmarsh Crossing, garden, this bench. Involved parties: Chen, M.; Kovač, J. Event: senior key shown, unasked. One test signature, one line. Slip to Volkov, N., December fourteenth. Status: the most honest thing anyone did at the talks.

He noted it and moved on. It did not need anything added.

"They've given me a job," said Julian.

He said it the way he said most things, lightly, with the dry edge that Marcus had learned in November to listen for. He was wearing the gray cardigan under his archivist's jacket, and a scarf that somebody had knitted, and his hands were bare and red with the cold.

"Liaison to the witness networks. That's the title. There are nine towns on our shore now keeping books by hand, and two more asking how, and the Administration has noticed that it doesn't know what to call them. So they've called me." He smiled at the gravel. "I think the reasoning was that I'd already embarrassed them as much as a man can, so I might as well be useful."

"Are you?"

"I don't know yet. I've been to four of them. I sit in kitchens and people read me their entries and I countersign the ones I saw on the broadcasts." He paused. "They're very kind to me. I think they feel sorry for me. I've never been felt sorry for. It's a strange sensation. Rather like being warm."

Then he reached inside his jacket and took out a small notebook with a cloth cover, the kind sold in Synthesis stationers, and held it on his knee without opening it.

"And I've started one."

Marcus looked at it.

"In handwriting," said Julian. "My colleagues at the Archive find it deeply strange. Nobody writes by hand in Chronos except the very old and the very ill. One of them asked me quite seriously if it was a medical condition." He opened it at the first page and turned it so that Marcus could see. "I told her I was being treated for it."

The first page was dated in ink.

18 December 2108. 06:10:04. The seconds had been struck through with a single neat line. Woke. Did not read the log. Wrote this instead. It is already less accurate than the log would have been. That is not the point.

The hand was careful and slightly stiff, a schoolboy's hand grown up without practice. And the capitals sloped. The W and the D and the T all leaned forward together, eager, as if each one wanted to be first to the next word.

"You kept the seconds," said Marcus.

"I crossed them out."

"You wrote them first."

"Old habits," said Julian. "I'm told they're the last to go." He closed the book and held it in both hands. "It's the first thing I've ever made that isn't in the log. Well. That's not true. It's in the log, because I saw myself write it. But the log only knows that I wrote something. It doesn't know what. You have to read the page." He looked at the book. "I find I like that more than I can explain."

They sat for a while without speaking. Beyond the wall a gull went over, crying, and somewhere on the marsh road a truck changed gear. The sun moved a hand's width along the gravel.

Julian looked out over the wall, towards the lake, which could not be seen from the bench but which was there, the way it was always there on this coast, in the light and the smell of the air.

"Thank you for not weaponising my loneliness," he said.

Marcus could not answer.

He knew loneliness. He had known it before any of this, before Wing C, before the hood and the holes. There had been a man at desk 14-08 who had colleagues but few friends and had not thought it worth mentioning, who went home at seventeen hundred hours to a flat with nothing on the walls and entered the day's facts in his head on the tram because there was nobody to tell them to. He had not called it loneliness then. He had not had the word. Continuity had found it anyway, and built a room around it, and put Kira in the room, and waited to see what grew.

And in November a folder had asked him to build the same room around this man.

He sat with that for a long moment. Julian did not hurry him. Julian, of all people, knew how to wait for a sentence.

"It's in your log now," Marcus said at last.

"Yes." Julian did not look away from the wall. "I wanted it to be."

In November, on this bench, in fog, Julian had said It's in my log now. I can't take it back, and it had been a man discovering what he had done. This was a man doing it on purpose. Marcus heard the difference, and knew that Julian meant him to.

They walked to the gate together.

The Chronos cars were waiting on the gravel sweep outside the Customs House, three of them, pale and silent. Aubry was already in the first. The Veridican car was on the other side of the sweep, and between the two, at the gate in the old wall, the Synthesis flag moved a little in a wind off the marsh.

Julian stopped at the gate and held out his hand.

Marcus took it. Julian's hand was cold and his grip was firm and he did not let go at once. He looked at Marcus with those very blue eyes, the Kovač eyes, the eyes Marcus had first seen in a photograph at the back of a folder in a lift, and there was nothing in his face but what was there: gratitude, plain and whole, with nothing held behind it.

"I'll write," said Julian. "In handwriting. You'll have to read it."

"I'll read it."

"Badly spelled, probably. Chronos doesn't spell. It autocorrects."

"I'll check it," said Marcus, and Julian laughed, the helpless laugh from November with the fear gone out of it, and let go of his hand and walked across the gravel to the second car.

Marcus stood at the gate and watched it go. He found that he was carrying something he had not been carrying that morning. It had weight. He examined it, the way he examined everything, and found that it was the weight of being trusted by someone who had every reason not to, and that he was glad to carry it. He did not know when he had last been glad of a weight.

The train back to the capital took three hours and ran along the lakeshore for the first of them.

He had a compartment to himself. He took out his lists and unfolded them on the table under the window: CHECKED, UNCHECKED, HOPES, in pencil, the paper soft at the folds. He had copied them fresh in January, after the fourteenth, when he had noticed that his hand shook very slightly on the old sheets and had not wanted the shake to be part of the record.

At the bottom of HOPES, where he had written it on the night of November twenty-first, in the guesthouse at Saltmarsh Crossing, with the fog against the window and his lists spread on the blanket, was a line he had not looked at in weeks.

The talks end with nobody used.

He looked at it for a long time. The lake went past the window, gray and flat, and then a stretch of marsh, and then the lake again.

He did not rub it out. He never rubbed out a hope. He drew a single line through it, neatly, so that it could still be read, and wrote it again at the bottom of CHECKED, in the past tense, because it had happened now and could be checked.

Feb 11, 2109. Saltmarsh Crossing. The talks ended with nobody used. Verification: Kovač, J. (garden, in his log).

He looked at the name. Then he folded the lists and put them away, and watched the lake until the line turned inland.

It was dark when he reached the Justice Center.

He had meant to go home, to the flat, where Kira would be back from the farmhouse by eight. But the Commission had sat in the great hall that afternoon without him, and a clerk had left word that the session papers were waiting for him there, and he had learned that the things he left overnight were the things he lost.

The corridor behind the great hall ran the length of the building. Tall windows along one side looked out over the plaza, and the plaza lamps laid long panels of white light across the floor, bright and dark and bright, the way the winter sun had laid them on the fourteenth of January two years ago. It was very quiet. The committee rooms on the other side were dark. At the far end, by the stairs, one window bay was lit from inside.

Thomas Wei was standing in it.

He had his coat over his arm. He was alone, which was rare, and he was looking out at the plaza, where the Lost Hour stone stood pale by the fountain and a few people were crossing late with their collars up. He turned as Marcus came along the corridor, and his face did what it always did, which was to be pleased to see him.

"Commissioner."

"Director."

They stood a metre apart in a panel of light. Wei looked well. He always looked well. Tall, graying at the temples, a quiet voice, the thin pale line across the back of his left hand faded now almost to nothing.

He took something from the inside pocket of his jacket and held it out.

It was a page. A single leaf, cut cleanly along the gutter from a small book, and folded once. The paper was cream and good and slightly soft, the paper of a pocket book carried for a long time. Marcus had not seen the book. Kira had. She had seen it a week ago, at the technical bench, and filed it without knowing where it went.

"For your records," said Wei.

Marcus took it and unfolded it.

The hand was not one he knew. Small, upright, economical, the letters joined without flourish, the hand of a man who had been taught to write properly as a boy and had never since had any reason to write badly. Two entries. Dated. Short declarative sentences.

Nov 15, 2107. The Doctrine is law. The reformers are grateful, and trusted. Chen knows, and says nothing; he has begun keeping records of his own, with many hands, all checking each other. That was not in the design. Neither was the old man. I find I want to see what they build.

And under it, in the same ink, the same hand, a line's space below:

Feb 4, 2109. They refused the index. That was not in the design either.

He read it twice. The second time he read it as he would have read an entry at desk 14-08, for its structure rather than its content, and saw the thing the structure said.

There was nothing between them.

The first entry was fifteen months old. The second was a week. Between them, on this page, there was one line of white space and nothing else. No entry for the seventeenth of December, when the pages had gone out in three nations at once and the war had stopped. No entry for the eighteenth, and a speech written in about a day. No entry for the Night of Copies, or the confession read in the squares, or the fourteenth of January, when a panel had climbed to 0.58 while he watched it with polite interest. Fifteen months, and among them the most remarkable winter in the history of two nations, and this man had not written a word of it down.

Frequency of entries: two in fifteen months. Interval: irregular. Condition for entry:

He knew the condition. It was on the page twice. That was not in the design.

This was a man who wrote only when he was surprised.

Marcus looked up. Wei was watching him read, with the particular attentive pleasure he brought to anyone doing careful work, the look of a man who has set a problem and is watching it being solved.

"You sent the X pages," said Marcus.

Wei considered him for a moment. His face did not change at all.

"Did I?" he said.

Then he inclined his head, very slightly, the way a man acknowledges a good move across a board, and shifted his coat on his arm, and walked on past him down the corridor, unhurried, light on his feet, through the panels of white and dark, until he reached the stairs and was gone.

Marcus stood in the panel of light where he had been left.

He read the page a third time. It did not say that Wei had sent anything. It did not say that Wei had drafted anything, or fed anything, or had anyone fed. It said that a man had watched a design he admired meet something it had not expected, twice, and had written it down, twice, and had then cut the page out of his book and put it in Marcus's hand in an empty corridor, with nobody to see him do it.

It proved nothing. The Judge would not have entered it. It was a page in a stranger's hand, which Marcus would never be able to show was Wei's, which Wei would say, pleasantly, if anyone asked, was a page of notes about a book he was reading.

But he had wanted Marcus to see it. That much was on the page, because the page was in his hand.

He took out his lists and unfolded them against the cold glass of the window. The plaza lay below, the stone by the fountain, the last people crossing. He went to the bottom of CHECKED, below the line from the train, and wrote, in his small historian's hand, in the format of desk 14-08:

Feb 11, 2109. Justice Center, east corridor. Involved parties: Chen, M.; Wei, T. Event: he wanted us to see it. Verification: the page. Witnesses: none.

He looked at the last word for a long time.

The public testimony intake, across the plaza on the ground floor of the Commission's building, had closed at five. But Helena Kovač signed for releases on paper, herself, and she did not trust anyone else to lock the log away at night, and on Mondays she was always there until seven.

Marcus folded the page inside the lists, and put them in his inside pocket, and went to find one.
