Chapter 22: Two Old People

Friday, January 4 – Saturday, January 5, 2109. Ward 9S, the teaching hospital, the Chronos lakeshore city; the Drowned Causeway

He had hummed since the causeway.

He had hummed on the marsh road, and past the porter, and in the linen room among the folded sheets, through the night and the day and the night again, while the boys slept in turns against the shelves. He had stopped once, on the first evening, in the chair by her bed, and then begun again. He had hummed the way he had hummed in Wing C and in other places for forty years before that: low, steady, a sound for the machines to pass over. He had not decided to. It was what his throat did, in a country that was listening.

At noon on Friday the doctor came and stood in front of him.

"Mikhail," she said. "I will tell you once more, and then I will not tell you again. Nothing here is recorded. Not in this room, not on this ward. I would not say it if it were not true. I have never said anything to you that was not true."

He looked at her: small and very straight, with the thin pale scar on her temple. He had held her head in his lap in the back of a car on the night of the storm.

He had watched the ward for a day and a half. The sisters said what they liked to each other at the station. And yesterday, behind the drawn curtain by the east window, he had heard a woman laugh, short and dry, a sound he had last heard across a reading-room table in 2066.

He stopped humming.

The doctor nodded, once, as if a pulse had come back within range, and went away.

Elise was sitting upright by the east window, the curtains drawn round the bed, a cup in both hands. The right one trembled until it held something, and then it was steady. Behind her the lake lay flat and white under a white sky.

He took the visitor's chair. The other patients were asleep. For a while neither of them said anything. In the Archive they had sometimes sat for an hour on a Thursday without a word, and then she would look up and say rhyme is a compression algorithm, Mikhail, as if they had been arguing the whole time.

He found that he wanted to speak, and that he could. In Wing C he had said perhaps a dozen sentences in a year. Here, in the one room in Chronos that did not remember, the words were simply there, where they had always been, under the tune.

"We met again on a Thursday," he said. "I noticed. You'd have noticed too."

"I did," said Elise.

His voice was rough. He let it be.

"You came in on Thursdays for two years, from the systems department, with ink on your fingers. You read the gray journal the moment it came in, slowly, making notes, the way you read something you mean to build on. I never once asked what you wanted with it. I was twenty-five. I thought wanting to understand a thing was the same as being on its side."

He looked at his hands.

"You sat at the far end of the long table, because the lamp there was better, and because it was the furthest from Anselm's desk. We argued in whispers. He pretended not to hear. You said rhyme was a compression algorithm. I said it was a kind of prayer. You gave me the prayer and won anyway. You always gave the first point. In November I read three typed pages that gave the first point in every paragraph, and I knew you before the end of the first one."

She didn't move. Her cup was steady.

"I was at a kitchen table with a cracked stove door. I stopped humming, and a boy who learned my tunes through a meal slot looked up, because in the place where he learned them, when I stopped, it meant somebody was watching." A breath. "Somebody was. You were. Forty-five years late, through a songbook, with your chipped e." He very nearly smiled. "I wrote your name on a piece of paper that night. I'd never once written down a name I cared about. Things that are written down get found. I sat and looked at it for an hour, like a fool."

"Not like a fool," said Elise. "Like an archivist."

"There isn't always a difference." He let that go. It was not what he had come to say.

"Anselm ate his apple at eleven. Gloves off, pocketknife, one long curl. He asked where the box had gone. Politely. In writing. Twice. When they gave him back he still peeled it in one curl, and I said the shelf mark to him, Periodicals, Governance, one hundred and fourteen, and he asked me if I was sure I had the number right."

He heard his voice go at the edge, like an old record, and went on over it.

"The numbers run one hundred and thirteen, one hundred and fifteen now. A clever girl looked, two winters ago, on a machine in a farmhouse. There isn't a hole. There isn't even a seam. You'd stopped coming in by then. I used to look at the end of the table on Thursdays."

A trolley went past beyond the curtain. Neither of them looked.

"So I sang it. Forty years of it: correction rooms, meal slots, the trolley, the hood humming behind my ears. You were right about compression. That's why it lived. The machine skims a tune like furniture. I kept those essays alive with your argument." He turned his hands over on his knees and looked at the palms. "I used to think that was the joke. That I'd won the Thursday argument after all, just by being too stubborn to die. It isn't a joke. It's only true."

He was quiet for a moment.

"I didn't cross a marsh to forgive you. I don't know how. I'm too old to learn a thing that size, and I'm not sure it's mine to give; there were a great many people in those rooms besides me. And I didn't come to condemn you. The record does that for a living, and I've never once agreed with the record about anything." A pause. "I came to ask you something."

She waited. She had always known how to wait for the end of a sentence.

"Since sixty-six I've carried one question for you, and it was the wrong one. I wanted to know where you'd gone. Forty years I wanted to know where you'd gone. Whether they'd taken you too. Whether you were in one of those places, humming something, and I'd walked past your door with the trolley and not known. I used to listen at the slots for a woman's voice." He stopped. "Since November I've had the right one."

He looked at her.

"Did you know what it was, when you were writing it?"

She did not look away. He had not thought she would. She had never once looked away from a hard sentence, even her own.

"Yes," said Elise Vance. "That is what I have to live with. Not that I didn't know. That I did, and was frightened, and thought frightened was the same as wise."

The ward was very quiet.

He sat with it. He had expected to feel something large, and he did not. He felt what he felt when a tune he had been trying to recall for years came back whole: not joy, only that it had been there the whole time, and was worse and simpler than he had let it be.

"Thank you," he said, because it was the truth, and because she had not tidied it.

She set the cup down on the locker. Her right hand began at once to tremble, and she folded it inside her left and held it there.

"You'll want to know why here," she said.

He waited.

"The one room in Chronos that does not remember. In Veridica they could have corrected me. In Chronos they would have logged me. Here there is nothing to correct and nothing to log. A record can be restored. Nobody can restore what was never written." She looked at the curtain, and through it, at the ward. "And Soren was here."

"And before?"

"Later. That's a long list, and you'd want it checked."

He very nearly laughed. It came out as a breath.

"And his name," he said. "Why his?"

For the first time she paused before she answered.

"Because he asked where the box went," she said, "and I didn't."

She said nothing more about Kerr, and he did not ask her to. There was a place where he kept Anselm, with the apple and the courtesy you could have set a clock by, and the green-shaded lamp on his own desk across the room, and he did not open it now. She had made a door out of the name and lived behind it for eleven years. That was hers to have done. It was not his to walk through.

"The page at the end," he said instead. "The one in your hand."

"March 2066." She did not need to be told which page. "The document was typed by then. The copies weren't mine any more. I wrote it in my own, at night, at the kitchen table, in pencil first and then in ink. I told no one."

"No one."

"Who would I have told?" Very level. Very dry. The Warning voice. "Anyone who could have acted on it was in the document. Anyone who couldn't would have been helped, like Anselm."

He looked at her for a long time. She let him.

"It's the best thing you ever wrote," he said at last. "And you gave it to nobody."

"I gave it to whoever found it." The corner of her mouth went, very slightly. "That isn't the same as nobody. It only felt like it for forty years."

In the afternoon the others came.

Alexei laid out Kira's photographs on the blanket over Elise's knees, in the order they had gone out on the seventeenth: the Introduction, Three, Five, the torn strip, the handwritten page. Dmitri stood at the gap in the curtain. The doctor stood at the foot of the bed with a chart she did not look at.

Elise read them the way he read things; they had learned it at the same table. The object first, the paper, the edges, the corners. Then the words. She did not read the Introduction at all, only looked at it, as a woman looks at a photograph of a house she used to live in.

At Three she stopped. She put out her left forefinger and touched the lower right-hand corner of the page in the photograph, where the small typed numeral was. Then she found Five, and touched the same corner.

"Copy X isn't mine," she said.

Nobody spoke.

Dmitri, at the curtain, had built cases for most of his life. Mikhail watched him set this one out in his head before he spoke.

"Dr. Vance," he said. "If it is not yours, then it is someone's. Whose is it?"

"Names would make you a court," said Elise, "and a court is one record."

"With respect. There were seventeen."

"Yes."

"Then one name. The one who holds that copy."

"No." She did not raise her voice. "Not that one. Not any of them. I'm sorry, Mr. Volkov. You'd do something very sensible with it, and it would be the wrong thing."

Dmitri looked at her for a long moment, and his face did nothing at all, which Mikhail had learned long ago meant a great deal. Then he inclined his head and said nothing further.

The doctor spoke then, quietly, in the voice she used to ask a patient's permission.

"Is there anything else in your copy," she said, "that ought to cross the lake?"

Elise turned her head and looked at her.

"There is one page I will not give anyone yet."

That was all. The doctor wrote it down.

Mikhail noticed two things, and kept them where he kept things. She had said page, not pages, and not part, and not anything. And her right hand, while she said it, was lying on the blanket holding nothing at all, and it was perfectly steady.

At twenty past four Vale came through the ward door and drew the curtain back.

He was as courteous and as unhurried as ever. Only his hands gave him away: folded in front of him, one holding the other.

"There are two officers of the border directorate in the lobby," he said. "They've asked for Mrs. Kerr. By name."

Mikhail felt the room change.

"Not for the patient in 9S. Not for intruders. For Mrs. Kerr." He looked at his mother. "The ward has listed initials for eleven years. That name is in one file in my office and nowhere else anyone has opened since 2098."

Somebody knew.

Nobody said it. Nobody said who could have. The doctor stood very still with her chart. Dmitri at the curtain was already counting something, doors, minutes, the distance to the lift. Alexei, by the window, glanced once towards the ward door and the corridor beyond it, where the service lift stood that they had come up in the night before last on Julian Kovač's card, and then he looked away and didn't look back.

"I've told them she is gravely ill, and that the ward is under seal, and that they will need an Administrator's order to come through that door. They've sent for one. The Administration is very thorough." A pause. "It will not take them all night."

Elise looked out of the window. At the far edge of the lake, where the Veridican shore should have been, there was nothing, and the nothing was coming closer: a soft wall moving in over the water, eating the lake a hundred metres at a time.

The fog came in at dusk.

Outside 9S, her interface would log her: every step, her face, her voice, her heart, to the nanosecond. There was only one way out of a record like that, and Mikhail had taken it himself, from Ashford, at night.

She would have to leave as a dead woman.

The doctor sent for Morrison from the fourth floor. He came up the back stairs in his shirtsleeves with a ward tablet under his arm, and stopped in the linen-room doorway, and saw the old man on the step stool, and went white. "Sorry," he said, to nobody, and came in.

Mikhail knew him. He had pushed the trolley past the console room in Wing C a thousand times and seen those pale eyes on the screen and never on the patient. The hands had shaken then. They were steady now.

He did what he had done at the fence. He plugged the tablet into the ward's telemetry relay and opened a signature from the hospital's archive: a woman who had died on the fourth floor in the autumn, whose family had signed the research release. He pushed it through the relay for the patient in the east-window bed, a small green trace that rose, and faltered, and ran out into a straight line.

Then he set the relay to report zero.

"Done," he said. "She's—" He stopped. "The relay's reading zero. It'll go on reading zero." He didn't look at the patient. He hadn't once. "I'm sorry," he said, and it wasn't clear to whom.

Vale sat down at the nurses' station and filled in a form, in his own hand, while they watched. Mikhail read it upside down across the counter, as he had read documents all his life. KERR, Margit. Ward 9S. Date of death: 4 January 2109. Time: 17:52. The cause, in the language of her chart. The signature at the bottom was the same signature that had blocked two orders from the Administration and stood surety for an émigré physician in 2106. He blotted it, and folded it once.

In Chronos's record, from that minute, Mrs. Margit Kerr was dead in 9S.

"The van," said Vale, and took a set of keys from his pocket and held them out to Dmitri. "The mortuary van. It's in the bay." He put the keys in Dmitri's hand. "Nobody in Chronos stops the dead."

The doctor had gone to the locker by the east window. She came back with a folded sheaf of paper and gave it to Dmitri, too.

"My case notes," she said. "From the third to this afternoon. For Commissioner Osman. Everything the patient said to me, as she said it." She held on to them a second longer than she needed to. "She will know what to do with them. I don't."

"You're not coming," said Alexei.

"I am the attending physician on a sealed ward that has just lost a patient," said the doctor. "There will be forms. If I am not here to fill them in, someone will ask why." She looked at the bed, and then away. "A sealed ward that stops running gets noticed. I shall keep it running."

Morrison had already gone, back down the stairs to the fourth floor, without a word to anyone.

They laid Elise on a mortuary trolley in the linen room. She lay down on it herself, without help, very straight, and folded her hands on her breast, the right inside the left. Her son stood over her. Before the sheet went over her face she looked up at him.

"Go up and be charming to them," she said.

They were the last words she said in the room that did not remember.

Outside the ward door, Mikhail began to hum.

He hummed in the service lift, going down, with the trolley between them and the sheet very white under the light. He hummed past the booth at the bottom, where a different porter looked at the director and the trolley and three men in dark coats, and lowered his eyes, as people do. Nobody spoke. Outside 9S, everything Soren Vale saw and heard was being written down, and he knew it better than anyone, and he said nothing at all.

The mortuary was at the end of the basement corridor, cold and white and very clean, and at this hour empty of the living. Steel drawers in a wall. A double door to the van bay, where a white van stood with the hospital's name on it in small gray letters.

Vale stopped the trolley by the doors.

He folded back the top of the sheet, a little way, and looked at his mother's face. Her eyes were shut. He reached down and straightened the collar of her cardigan where it had turned under, carefully, with both hands, the way you straighten something for a person who can't see to do it. He did not speak. Mikhail, humming, watched his hands and thought that he had never in his life seen anything so plainly recorded, or so private.

Then Soren Vale drew the sheet back up, and took the folded certificate from his pocket, and went up to the lobby to be charming to them.

Dmitri drove. Alexei sat in the back with the trolley, one hand flat on the sheet. Mikhail sat beside him and hummed.

At a junction a directorate car passed them going the other way, towards the hospital, fast and without a siren. It did not slow. Nobody in Chronos stops the dead.

They left the van on the marsh road with the keys in it. Then they waited in the reeds, because there were lamps moving on the shore road, slow and methodical, and Dmitri counted them, and the minutes between them, and said at last, very quietly, "Now." By then it was long after midnight.

Elise walked the first stretch herself on Alexei's arm, in a coat the doctor had found her, placing each foot where he told her. At the first drowned stretch, where the sleepers lay awash, Dmitri stepped past his brother without a word and picked her up. He carried her easily and carefully, as if she weighed nothing, which was nearly true. She did not protest. She put one arm round his neck and held his collar with her right hand, and Mikhail saw that the hand was steady.

At every gap Dmitri carried her, and between them she walked. He said nothing the whole way. The fog was only fog, cold and wet and ordinary.

Mikhail hummed all the way across, every phrase lifting at the end, because behind him for a long time there were the lamps of a country that was listening, and he did not yet believe they could not hear.

The fog thinned towards dawn.

They came off the last gap as the light began, gray and colourless, and ahead of them the concrete buffer stop stood up out of the reeds like a milestone. Somewhere beyond it, on the dyke, an engine was idling: Elena, counting the minutes.

Dmitri set Elise down on her feet. She stood, a little unsteadily, holding his sleeve.

Mikhail stopped humming.

He stood on the sleepers with his hands in his coat pockets and looked out over the marsh, where the fog was going off the water in long slow threads. It was Saturday, the fifth of January, and there was no one on this shore listening but the people he had chosen.

He drew a breath, and he sang, with words.

"Down by the water the ferry is late, lanterns are low on the pier; who will remember the name of the boat when there is no one left here?"

His voice was cracked and very quiet, an old man's voice in the open air at first light. He did not try to make it anything else.

Beside him, Elise Vance had turned her head. She had heard that tune across a reading-room table for two years, hummed under a young man's breath while he catalogued. She had heard it through two doors on a sealed ward. She had never once, in her life, heard the words.

She stood very still in the gray light and listened to them, holding Dmitri's sleeve, and her face was the face of someone reading something for the first time that she had been told about for years.

The last line lifted at the end, like a question.

when there is no one left here?
