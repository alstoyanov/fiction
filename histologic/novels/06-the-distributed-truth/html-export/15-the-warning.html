Chapter 15: The Warning

Sunday, December 9, 2108. The resistance farmhouse, the border zone; the Commission building, Veridica Capital

December ninth, 2108. Ten-forty hours. Farmhouse kitchen, border zone. Involved parties: Petrov, M.; Osman, K.; Volkov, E.; Volkov, A.; Volkov, D.; Chen, M. Object: one songbook, pre-Histologic, received from a Chronos physician at the lakeshore checkpoint, Saturday. Purpose: the last binding.

Marcus had written the entry in his head on the drive out, and corrected it twice, and he ran it again now at the kitchen table because it was the only thing in the room that was entirely under his control.

The book lay in the middle of the table on a clean sheet of paper, where Old Songs had put it. It was the smallest of the four: faded cloth the colour of wet sand, no bigger than a hand, with a spine that had been mended so neatly that Marcus could not see where. Alexei had brought it in last night out of the fog, with the cold on him. "The last one," he had said, putting it down. "That's what she told the doctor to tell me. The last one, I think." He had not grinned, which for Alexei was a kind of speech.

Nobody had opened it. There was a way things were done now at this table: the books were his, and he opened them, and the rest of them watched.

Elena stood at the end of the table with her arms folded. Dmitri was by the door, his back to the wall. Alexei sat on the bench under the window with his elbows on his knees. Kira sat beside Marcus with her hands flat on the table in front of her, not touching anything, and he could feel her counting.

Old Songs took the small kitchen knife from the drawer and sat down.

He slid the point in under the cloth at the head of the spine and drew it down, a finger's width at a time. Halfway down he stopped, and put two fingers inside, and drew out a sheet of paper folded twice.

It was not typed.

Old Songs unfolded it on the clean sheet and smoothed it once with the side of his palm.

One page. Handwritten, in ink that had gone brown at the edges of the strokes. A woman's hand, and a young one, he thought: precise, small, leaning forward as if into a wind, each letter made completely and then left alone. At the top right corner, in the same hand, a number: 40. At the foot, below the last line, a date: March 2066.

Nobody moved.

Old Songs did not read it. He looked not at the words but at the ink: the slope, the pressure, the way the tails of the letters went. Then he laid two fingers on the paper beside the first line, not touching the ink, a centimetre away from it, the way you might lay your hand on a table beside someone else's hand without taking it.

"Her hand," he said.

He pushed the page towards Marcus.

Marcus looked at him. Old Songs looked back, and nodded, very slightly, and folded his own hands in his lap. You read it, the nod said. You're the one it's for.

He picked the page up by its edges, and read it aloud.

"To whoever finds this: I assembled these principles because I was afraid. I was wrong. The system contains the seeds of its own corruption: Three enables the war of facts and Five encourages it. Reformers will try to fix it. They will fail, because the design anticipates them: it will make whistleblowers into criminals and reformers into its own restorers. There is no reform path, only replacement. Distribute truth. Distribute verification. Accept uncertainty. Build by many hands."

He put it down.

The stove ticked. Outside, the yard lay under a thin fog.

Reformers into its own restorers.

He heard it the way you hear your own name said in a crowded room. Page thirty, in a Deputy Minister's photograph: arrange to be the one who restores it. The strip on Wednesday, three lines of numbers, telling him that page thirty was the sixth principle of something. And the Commission. Two years of the Commission. The steps of the Justice Center where he had taken the oath with Isaiah's column folded in his pocket, the dead declared living, the country grateful. It's us, he had said on Wednesday at this table, and late that night, we were always going to be the restorers, and the old man had said, without turning round from the stove, then stop restoring.

It had been written down in 2066. In ink. Before he was born, before Wing C, before Elena had stopped him by a fountain and asked him for directions. Someone had sat at a desk, forty-two years ago, frightened and exact, and described him.

And the last line.

Build by many hands.

He looked across the table at the old man. He remembered it, and for once the memory was whole: this kitchen, in January of last year, the old man's one speech, Kira's folder squared to the table's edge. Write it down. All of it. In your own hands. Many hands. Many copies. Old Songs had said that without ever having seen this page.

Two people, forty years apart, had come to the same four words. One of them had built the prison. The other had spent forty years in it.

Marcus found that he had taken out his pencil. He did not remember doing it. He wrote, in the margin of the clean sheet under the page, in his small historian's hand:

CHECKED: Three enables the war of facts. (Principle Three, pp. 13–17; the deposit of Nov 26.)

CHECKED: Five encourages it. (Principle Five, pp. 23–28; the deposit of Nov 28. Kestrel Point. The claimed strip. The vans.)

CHECKED: reformers into its own restorers. (Page 30. The Commission.)

He stopped, and looked at the next line, and wrote it in the other column.

UNCHECKED: There is no reform path.

It was the only line he could not check, because checking it would mean trying every path, and he had only one life, and part of that was missing.

"Not the Commission," said Marcus.

He said it to the table, and heard it land, and went on before he could stop.

"We can't take this to the Commission. We can't take it to one paper, or one broadcaster, or The Judge. Any of those is a centre. It goes in at one door, and whoever holds the door decides what it means." He put one finger on the Warning, beside the word restorers, not on it. "She's telling us that. Anything with a centre, the design already knows what to do with. It restores it."

"Then what?" said Elena. Commander's voice, flat.

"Everywhere at once."

"The same hour. Three nations. On paper first, so nobody can say it was adjusted." He counted it on the table, the way Kira counted things, one finger for each. "Your couriers, and the keepers. Sealed packets, paper, to every journal we know, this week, all of them marked not to be opened before the hour. At the hour, Kira enters the whole set into the Commission's public archive, where anyone can read it and nobody can say it came from nowhere. Kovač enters the two deposits from her intake as testimony received, which is her procedure, and her custody ends in her own archive entry. And I read my orders aloud on the Justice Center steps. The KINDRED orders. All of them. The annex." He looked at Dmitri. "With you beside me, if you'll stand there."

"I will stand there," said Dmitri.

"Chronos," said Kira.

"Julian publishes his orders from his own log. MERIDIAN, and the garden, the twenty-second, everything he recorded. It's his right. Self-disclosure. Nobody in Chronos can take it back once he's done it." Marcus paused. "I'll give him the hour tomorrow. In the garden. Where it's recorded."

Kira looked at him for a moment and did not say anything. Neither of them said that Julian was Adrian's brother. It sat on the table between them with the Warning and the knife, and they both left it there.

"And Isaiah," said Marcus. "In the Sovereignty, and through Synthesis. The same hour. He's already said so. Your hour is mine. Not before." He almost smiled. "It'll be evening where he is."

"What goes out," said Elena.

"The orders, and Wei's annex. The Introduction. Three. Five. The strip." He touched the edge of the page. "This. And Singh's photograph of page thirty, which we can now call what it is. The sixth principle." He thought of the original, the torn page with a dead woman's pencil in its margin, locked in Kovač's safe. "From the photograph. The original stays with Kovač. It's safer with her than with anyone."

"And the names," said Kira.

Everyone looked at the old man.

He had not moved. He sat with his hands folded in his lap and his eyes on the page, on the slant of the ink. He did not look up. He hummed one phrase, low, the Lakeshore Air's last phrase, the one that went down. Not up, like a question. Down. The phrase that in Wing C, through a meal slot, had meant no.

It was the whole answer, and everybody at the table had learned to read it through a wall.

"No names," said Marcus. "None. Not hers. Nothing about how any of it reached us. Not the books, not the doctor, not the checkpoint, not the patient. Not the crossings." He looked at Alexei. "Nothing that leads back across the lake."

"Good," said Alexei quietly.

"When?" said Elena.

Marcus had known that too, he found, since he read the last line.

"Monday week. The seventeenth." He heard the room hear it. "Fourteen hundred."

"They'll know it's on purpose," said Kira.

"Good," said Marcus. "It is."

Fourteen hundred. The hour the record had failed, two years ago, when for sixty minutes a nation's memory had been issued by the state. The hour of the Day of Testimony, when ordinary people stood up at the Lost Hour stone and read out ordinary days. The hour the country had been given back. By the Commission. By them.

It would be the hour they gave it something else.

Kira had already taken out her notebook. She was not looking at him. She was writing, very fast, in the tight small hand she used for lists, and he watched her lips move without sound: numbers. Kestrel Point. The four towns on the shore from Kestrel. The capital, east and west. The listening sessions, all eleven. She was counting couriers. She had not said she agreed with him. She had not been asked. She had simply begun, the way she began everything that mattered, with a list of what it would take.

He found he could not look at her for very long, and looked instead at the page.

Elena unfolded her arms.

"Seven days to move paper to every journal we know," she said. "Without a single packet opened early. Without a single courier stopped." She looked at the table, left to right, the way she looked at everything. "I'll need Alexei's routes and every keeper Kira trusts, and I'll need them to not know about each other."

"That's the point," said Kira, without looking up. "Nobody knows all of it."

"No," said Elena. "Nobody does."

She went out into the yard.

He found her by the barn twenty minutes later, counting the trucks back in, though none of them had gone out.

The yard was frozen mud and puddles skinned with ice. Elena had her back to him. She did not turn round when he came up, and he did not expect her to. In three years she had said perhaps a hundred sentences to him directly, and most of them had been about roads.

He stood a little way off and waited.

"You'll be arrested," she said to the barn. "On the steps. Or the day after."

"Perhaps."

"Perhaps." She almost laughed. It came out as breath in the cold. "You never used to say perhaps. You used to say according to the record."

He did not answer that. He was thinking, suddenly and with great clarity, of a fountain in the autumn sun and a woman in a blue dress who needed directions, and of a café on the far side of the plaza where she had bought him coffee, black, no sugar, because she had already known how he took it. He reached for the name of the café. It had had a name. It had been painted on the window, and he reached for it now and there was only a smooth place, like a wall skimmed and painted over.

He filed it. Café, north side of the plaza, autumn 2105. Name: not retained. It did not hurt. That was the part he had never got used to: that it did not hurt.

"I did this to you," said Elena.

She had turned round. She was looking at him directly, for once, with her dark eyes very steady in her bony, lined face, and her voice was low and hoarse and entirely controlled.

"In 2105. By the fountain. I was good at it, and I chose you, and I did it. Everything after that. The white room. The glass. What you haven't got any more." She did not look away. "I know what it costs. I know what it costs on both sides."

He knew what she meant by both sides. He thought he did. She had been the one who chose, and she had been, once, long before that, a girl whose brother had come home from correction with a smooth place where he used to be, and had died a year later on a border with a number instead of a name. She had been on both sides of it. She was the only person he knew who had.

There was a part of it he could answer. I know. I know you did. I'm here anyway. And there was a part he could not answer, because the thing that would have answered it had been taken out of him in a white room and was not coming back, and he would not pretend to her that it was.

He answered neither. He stood in the frozen yard and looked at her, and let her see that he had heard.

She nodded, once, as if he had said something. She did not ask him to say anything else. Then she turned back to the barn, and began, again, to count.

Dmitri drove them back to the capital in the dark.

He drove the way he did everything, exactly, at the limit and not a kilometre over. Kira slept in the back with her pencil still in her hand.

"You'll tell her," said Marcus. "Sokolov."

"I will tell her the hour." Dmitri did not take his eyes off the road. "Fourteen hundred, on the seventeenth. That there will be a publication. I will tell her nothing else, because she will not ask for anything else, and because if I told her more it would become something she was responsible for." A pause, measured, like everything he said. "She has enough of those."

"What will she do?"

Dmitri was quiet for nearly a kilometre.

"I do not know," he said. "I have never once known."

It was nearly ten when they reached the city. Kira went up to the flat. Marcus asked Dmitri to drop him at the Commission building, and Dmitri did, and did not ask why, and waited at the kerb with the engine running.

The building was dark, as it always was on a Sunday. The intake on the ground floor was shut and shuttered, the steel slot in the wall a narrow black line, the two cameras above it watching an empty hall. Marcus let himself in by the side door with his card and took the stairs.

On the fifth floor, at the end of the corridor, one light was on.

The Technical Director's office had a frosted glass panel in the door, lit from inside, as it was on most nights and most Sundays.

He did not have to do this. He had said so to Kira on the drive in, before she slept, and she had opened one eye and said, No. You don't. And then, after a moment: Tell him anyway. Then there's nothing he can say he wasn't told. The public archive was Wei's system. He had built it. He maintained it. At fourteen hundred on the seventeenth, Kira would enter a set of documents into it that would bring a great many people to it at once, and it was a courtesy, between colleagues, to tell the man who kept the doors that a crowd was coming.

It was a courtesy. That was all it was. He told himself so at the door.

He knocked.

"Come in."

Thomas Wei was at his desk with his tablet in his right hand and a cup of something at his elbow, in his shirtsleeves, with his jacket on the back of the chair. The office was very tidy. The screens behind him were dark. He looked up, and saw Marcus, and smiled, the way he always smiled: pleasantly, with his whole attention, as if Marcus were the one person he had hoped would come through the door on a Sunday night.

"Commissioner. You're working late."

"So are you."

"It's quieter." Wei set the tablet down, face down, with the careful habit of a man who was always tidy. The thin pale scar across the back of his left hand caught the light for a moment and then did not. "What can I do for you?"

Marcus stayed in the doorway. He found he did not want to come into the room.

"A courtesy," he said. "Monday week. The seventeenth. At fourteen hundred, Commissioner Osman will be entering a set of documents into the public archive. And I'll be reading my orders on the Justice Center steps. The orders I was given in November." He kept his voice level. "The orders, and some pages. I thought you should know, since it's your system."

Wei listened to all of it without moving.

Then he said, "Good."

He said it simply, the way a colleague says it when he has been told that a long-delayed project is finally on schedule. He did not ask what pages. He did not ask whose orders, though he had sat at the side table in the First Minister's office with a slim folder and a tablet on the night they were given, and had written the annex himself. He did not ask what the steps had to do with anything, or who else would be reading, or where. He said good, and he smiled.

The smile was pleasant. It was warm. It was exactly the smile of a man who has been told good news about a thing he admires.

It went on for one beat longer than it should have.

Then Wei picked the tablet up again. "Thank you for telling me," he said. "I'll make sure the archive can take the load."

Marcus walked home. It was a little under two kilometres, and he wanted the cold.

The flat was dark except for the lamp in the kitchen, which Kira had left on for him, and the door to the bedroom was ajar and the room behind it quiet. He sat down at the kitchen table in his coat, as he had sat at the farmhouse table in the morning, and took out his lists.

CHECKED. UNCHECKED. HOPES. He had kept them for three years, the way a man with holes in his life keeps track of where the floor is. He had never once written an entry in advance.

He took the pencil, and a clean sheet, and wrote the date at the top, and then stopped with the point of the pencil resting on the paper.

He thought of the format he had used at desk 14-08 on the fourteenth floor, every working day for three years: traffic incidents and births and property disputes, each small green tick a brick in a wall. Date. Time. Location. Involved parties. Event. Verification. He could feel the shape of having loved it, the way you can feel the shape of a tooth that has been taken out.

He wrote it in that format now. In pencil, so that it could be corrected.

Dec 17, 2108. 14:00. Justice Center steps; Commission archive; Chronos; the Sovereignty. Involved parties: many hands. Event: publication. Verification:

He sat and looked at the last word for a long time.

At desk 14-08 the verification line had been the one that mattered. It was where you entered who had confirmed the fact: the witnesses, the sensor logs, the cross-reference, the thing that made an event into a fact and a fact into the record. He had never once left it empty. An entry with an empty verification line was not an entry. It was a hope that had been written in the wrong column.

He did not know who would verify this. He did not know whether the packets would reach the journals, or the couriers would be stopped, or the archive would take the load, or whether he would be standing on the steps at fourteen hundred or sitting in a room with no windows. He did not know what Sokolov would do. He did not know what Julian's log would be worth once Chronos had read it. He did not know why Wei had said good.

He left the line blank.
