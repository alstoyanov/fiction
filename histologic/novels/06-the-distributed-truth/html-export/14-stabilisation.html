Chapter 14: Stabilisation

Friday, December 7, 2108. The teaching hospital, the Chronos lakeshore city

07:30. Order received, Administration of Temporal Defence, by priority channel. Subject: stabilisation of acute storm casualties. Effective: immediately. Grounds: "in view of wartime need". Authority: overrides the director's hold of November 5.

Presenting condition of the clinician: calm.

Dr. Yuki Tanaka read the order twice at her desk on the fourth floor, and then took her coat off and hung it on the door, because it was better to take bad news with one's coat off. It suggested one intended to stay.

The car park beyond the window was gray under a gray sky. Morrison's desk against the far wall was empty; he came in at eight. In her bottom drawer, where it had lived for a year, was a draft that began Twelfth memorandum on salience-reduction methodology and had stood at eight pages for most of that year. She had been told to finish it, a month ago, on the ninth floor. She had written a page and a half more and stopped again, because the director's hold had stood, and a memorandum against an order nobody was carrying out had seemed like a thing one wrote for oneself.

It was not for herself now.

She heard him before she saw him. The director did not take the lift. He took the stairs, all five flights, at the unhurried pace of a tall man who has learned to be careful with his height.

He knocked, although the door was open.

"Dr. Tanaka."

"Director."

Dr. Soren Vale came in and did not sit down. He was more than a head taller than she was and stood, as he always stood, with a slight stoop, as though the ceilings of his hospital had been built a little low for him and he did not want them to feel it. He had beautiful manners, the kind that are not a surface but a structure, and go all the way down.

"You've seen it," he said.

"I've seen it."

"I'm afraid I have been overruled." He looked at the window, not at her. "Temporal Defence has cited wartime need, and The Judge has upheld the citation. The hold was mine to place and it is not mine to keep. I wanted you to hear it from me."

"Thank you," said Tanaka. She meant it.

He stood for a moment longer, as if there were something else. She waited. In two years he had never told her why he had placed the hold, or the one before it, or why in August 2106 he had put his own signature under a Veridican émigré's registration. She had a clinician's hypothesis: a man who had read Isaiah Okonkwo's article about Wing C, and had a daughter, and had imagined her in a glass box. She had never tested it.

"The cradles are due on the storm ward at ten," he said. "Four units. I've asked for them to be held in their crates until the attending physician signs for them." A very slight pause. "The attending physician on the storm ward today is you."

"Yes."

"I thought it might be." He inclined his head, the small courteous movement that was, with him, nearly a bow, and went to the door. At the door he turned. "I'm sorry, Dr. Tanaka."

He did not say for what. He went back up the stairs, and she listened to him go until she could not hear him.

The crates came up at four minutes past ten, on two trolleys, pushed by porters who did not know what was in them and logged every step.

The storm ward was on the sixth floor, a long room with twelve beds and a window at the end that looked over the lake towards the Veridican shore, which today did not show at all. There had been a storm on Wednesday night over the harbour district, full strength at once. Eleven of the twelve beds were occupied, by Chronos citizens whose interfaces recorded everything they saw; so that when the porters set the crates down by the window, eleven people in eleven beds logged four long pale boxes and a small woman in a white coat going to meet them.

Tanaka did not sign for them. She read the lids.

Each crate carried a protocol summary under a clear plastic sleeve, with a timestamp at the foot. STABILISATION UNIT, CLINICAL. Chronos Neural Systems, lakeshore works. Hood, articulated arm, console. And at the top, where a clinician would read it first, a single line in larger type.

Aim: stable, self-originated certainty, experienced by the patient as their own.

She had a cup of tea in her hand, brought from the office without noticing. She found the window ledge and set the cup down there very carefully, with both hands, so that it made no sound at all.

She had set a cup down like that once before, in the summer of 2105, at her own kitchen table in Veridica, with a protocol in front of her that said to baseline, and the knowledge arriving all at once that she was going to refuse and that refusing was going to cost her everything. It had.

Experienced by the patient as their own. She had read its sister in Ashford, in Reeves's own hand. Stable, genuine belief, as distinct from compliance, experienced as authentic and self-originated.

Morrison had come up behind her. He was holding the calibration sheet from the side of the first crate, a single folded page, and he was reading it the way he read everything, all at once, with his head a little down.

She watched his face lose its colour. It went, not white, but gray, the colour of the sky over the car park, from the forehead downward, like a blind being drawn.

"James."

"It's mine," said Morrison. He did not look up. "The gain curve. The baseline threshold. The step intervals on the second pass." His finger moved on the page. "It's been adjusted for a Chronos interface. Somebody's changed the carrier. But it's mine. It's Wing C." He swallowed. "It's the one I built for the seven of you."

"Sorry," said Morrison.

He said it to nobody; not to her, not to the eleven people in the beds, who logged him saying it. It was the word he said at the end of every second sentence, and it had never before sounded to her as though he meant it for himself.

She did her round.

Whatever else was true, the patients on the ward had had a storm on Wednesday night and needed a doctor on Friday morning. She went down the row, bed by bed, and wrote as she went.

Bed 2. Male, 58. Ferryman, Chronos harbour service, lakeshore route. Admitted Thursday 01:10. Presenting: two concurrent autobiographical histories of residence, one on the Chronos shore (Chronos-consistent, logged), one on the Veridican shore (inconsistent with log; vivid; a wife, a house, the colour of its door). Both experienced as memory. Both experienced as his. He asks, courteously, which shore he lives on. He asks it every hour.

She told him what his log said. He thanked her, and she saw him not believe her, and saw him try.

Bed 7. Female, 9. Harbour district, Chronos shore. Admitted Thursday 02:40, with mother. Presenting: doubled perception of a landmark, the harbour pier, both present and absent; no confusion otherwise. She is not frightened. She is interested. She asks which pier is the real one. Mother asks the same question, less calmly.

The girl had been drawing it on the back of her menu card: two piers, side by side, one with lamps and one without. She held it up for Tanaka to see. "That one's in my log," she said, pointing, "and that one's in my head. Which one do I walk on?"

"Neither," said Tanaka, "until the doctor says so. Which is me. And I say not today."

The girl accepted this, provisionally, and went on drawing.

Course: expected resolution of acute doubling within ten to twenty days, per this ward's experience. Residual dual memory: probable, lifelong, tolerable. Recommended treatment: rest; reassurance; the patient's own log, reviewed with a clinician; time.

Proposed treatment, per order of 07:30: stabilisation.

Prognosis,

She stopped, and looked at the word, and did not finish the line.

The ninth floor smelt of itself.

The disinfectant they used on nine and nowhere else in the building was sharp and cold and faintly green, like cut stems. By evening it was on her cuffs.

Ward 9S was at the end of the corridor, next to the director's office, behind a door that looked like every other door and was not. Beyond it the walls were shielded and every patient's log was sealed by clinical order, so that anyone who walked through that door dropped out of the national record until they walked out again. It was the only place in Chronos where nothing was recorded. It ran on paper, and its patients were listed on the board by their initials only, which the senior nurse called the ward's one courtesy. Tanaka thought it was more than one.

In 9S, news travelled by nurse.

M.K. had already heard. Tanaka could see that she had heard from the door.

She was sitting up in the chair by the east window, where she sat every morning, dressed, very upright, in a plain dark cardigan buttoned to the neck. Her hair was white and cut short. Behind her the lake was fog, the colour of milk. She had a cup of tea in both hands, and she held it the way she held everything, firmly, a little in front of her, so that the tremor in the right hand had something to do and did not show.

Patient M.K., 72. Long-term, 9S. Lucid beyond her chart.

"They've arrived," said M.K.

"This morning."

"Four?"

"Four."

M.K. nodded, once, as if a figure in a column had been confirmed. "Sit down, Doctor. You look as though you've been standing since seven."

Tanaka had come to take a pulse. She sat in the visitor's chair, and took the thin wrist in her fingers, and counted. Seventy-two. Regular. She wrote it down.

"Your pulse is steadier than mine," she said.

"I'm not the one who has to sign for them." M.K. did not smile, but something in her face moved towards it and then away. "Have you read the lid?"

"Yes."

"Self-originated." M.K. said the word as though holding it up to the light, looking for a flaw in the glass. "Do you want to know where it comes from?"

Tanaka did not answer at once. She had a rule. It was the only rule she had built entirely for herself, in a country that recorded everything: she did not ask patients about themselves beyond what the chart required. What they told her, she kept. What they did not tell her, she did not seek. In Chronos it was the most valuable thing a doctor could give anyone, and she had given it to this patient for two years and four months.

She had not asked. She had been asked.

"Yes," she said.

M.K. set the cup down on the sill. Her right hand, freed, began at once to tremble, very finely, and she laid it flat on the arm of the chair and held it there with the left, and began.

She dictated. There was no other word for it. She spoke in short sentences, in order, each one complete, as though reading from a document Tanaka could not see, and Tanaka found that she had taken out her pen and was writing, fast and without looking up.

"The term salience reduction was first proposed in the capital. In a clinical working paper. Spring of 2066. It was proposed as a treatment for veterans of the wars in the fifties who could not stop reliving them. The idea was simple. If a memory governs a patient, reduce the memory's weight, and it will stop governing. The paper did not propose to change what the patient remembered. Only how much it mattered."

Tanaka wrote.

"It was trialled three times. Autumn of 2067. Spring of 2068. Winter of 2069. All three failed. The first cohort kept the terror and lost the memories it belonged to. That was worse. The second kept the memories and lost everything else of the same weight. Their children. Their wives. That was worse again. The third was stopped in its second month, and the report was not published."

"Why not?"

"Because the third trial did not fail at what it was trying to do." M.K. looked out at the fog. "It failed at what it said it was trying to do. Those are different trials. The report would have had to say which one it was."

Tanaka's pen had stopped. She made it go on.

"The cradles were built across the lake. They always have been. Chronos builds the best hoods in the world, and Veridica buys them, and when a Veridican unit is returned for servicing it comes back with its calibration profiles on board. For forty years. Nobody carried the method across. It rode in the hardware, and it was kept, because Chronos keeps everything. Your reference firmware is Veridica's clinical history, written in your own engineers' hand." A pause. "Your colleague will have seen that this morning. On the sheet."

"He did."

"Yes. He would."

"And the aim," said Tanaka. "On the lid."

M.K. was quiet for a moment. Her left hand lay over her right, very still.

"The aim is a paraphrase," she said. "Of something old. About compassion."

She said no more. Tanaka waited, and understood that there was not going to be more; that the sentence ended exactly where it was meant to end, and the rest of it was on the other side of a door the patient had not opened and would not.

She did not ask. It was her wall. She had built it.

Under the last line, in her own hand, she had put Chronos stabilisation: derived from Veridican salience methodology, 2066–2069, via servicing data.

"Not derived," said M.K., who could not possibly have read it upside down from where she sat, and had. "Descended."

Tanaka looked up.

"Derived means someone did the arithmetic," said M.K. "Nobody did the arithmetic. It was handed on, from one to the next, and nobody checked the sums." She picked up her cup again, in both hands, and the tremor went quiet. "Write descended."

Tanaka crossed out derived with a single clean line, and wrote descended above it.

She wrote the memorandum at her desk on the fourth floor between one o'clock and six, with the door shut and Morrison at the other desk with his screen turned so that she could see it.

He had gone back further than she had asked, all the way to the servicing records of the lakeshore works, which as a Chronos engineer he was entitled to read. He read them for hours with his head down, and every so often said a date aloud, and a unit number, and she wrote it down. At half past three he said, very quietly, "James Morrison, Continuity calibration, Ashford east wing, Wing C, June 2105," and then, "It's here. They serviced the Ashford hoods in March of 2106. It came back with them," and she wrote that down too, and did not look at him, because she knew what his face was doing and he would not want it seen.

The memorandum was plain. She had found, over eleven of them, that the plainer she wrote, the harder it was to refuse.

Twelfth memorandum on salience-reduction methodology. To: the Administration of Temporal Defence; the Administration of Health; the director, lakeshore teaching hospital. Filed publicly, under the clinical record provisions. 7 December 2108.

1. The "stabilisation" protocol delivered to this hospital today is not a new technique. It is the Veridican method known in Veridica as Project Continuity, adjusted for a Chronos carrier.

2. Its calibration descends, by documented servicing records of Chronos Neural Systems, from the calibration profile used on the seven Continuity subjects at Ashford in 2105–2106. I was one of the seven. The engineer who wrote that profile has verified the descent line by line and signs this memorandum with me.

3. Its stated aim ("stable, self-originated certainty, experienced by the patient as their own") restates Continuity's stated aim in all but carrier.

4. In August 2106, following the publication of the Wing C account, the Administration of Health of Chronos condemned Project Continuity, publicly and by name, as "a violation of the self-authored record, which is the foundation of the Chronos citizen". I attach the condemnation.

5. The technique Chronos condemned in 2106 has come back across the lake in Chronos's own crates. It is now proposed for use on Chronos citizens, in a Chronos hospital, in time of war, on the ground that the war requires it.

6. It is unrepeated here. It is not unprecedented. The precedent is on record. I was in it.

I recommend that the order be withdrawn.

She read it through twice, and struck out four words, and added none. Morrison read it and signed it under her name, in a hand that did not shake. She filed it at 18:10, through the public clinical channel, where anyone could read it, and where The Judge, in its continuous reading of the collective will, would read it too.

Before she went home she opened her case notes and wrote the entry she had not written on nine.

Patient M.K., 72. Recall of 2060s clinical literature: precise, dated, first-hand in tone. Knows the lineage of the stabilisation technique better than its own protocol does. Source of knowledge: not asked.

She read it once. Every clause in it was a thing she had observed, and none of it was a thing she had concluded, and that was how a case note ought to be written. A clinician did not speculate about where a woman of seventy-two on a sealed ward had learned the dates of three unpublished trials that had ended when the clinician was a child.

She did not examine it further. She filed it where she kept such things: in the place in her head, behind the checklist and the cello and the house in the hills, where she put what she could not explain and did not intend to. 7-K kept empty through an overflow. A kind woman's sincerity. A body nobody unseeded had seen. And now this.

It was her wall. She had built it herself, in a country that recorded everything, so that there would be one room where a person could say a thing and not have it taken down and used. She was not going to be the first to go through it.

She closed the file.

The notice came at 21:40.

She was at home, at her own kitchen table, with a cup she had not yet drunk from, when the terminal chimed. One paragraph, from the Administration of Temporal Defence, under the same priority channel as the morning's order.

The order of 07:30 this day concerning stabilisation of acute storm casualties at the lakeshore teaching hospital is suspended, pending review of the clinical record.

No reason, no name. Chronos did not give reasons for suspensions. Somewhere between 18:10 and 21:40 The Judge had read a public filing and weighed it against the will of a nation that had condemned Continuity in 2106 and had not forgotten, because Chronos did not forget anything.

The crates would stay sealed. The girl in bed seven would go home in ten or twenty days with both piers in her head and a note that said tolerable.

Tanaka sat and looked at the paragraph for a long while. Then she put her coat back on.

The hospital at night was quieter, and no less logged. She went up in the lift to nine, and along the corridor past the director's office, where a light still showed under the door, and through the door that looked like every other door and was not.

9S was dark except for the night nurse's lamp at the station and one other, by the east window.

M.K. was awake. She was sitting up in bed with the reading lamp on and a paper book open on the blanket, face down, as though she had been reading and had stopped some time ago to wait. She looked up when Tanaka came in, and looked at her face, and nodded, once, as a figure in a column is confirmed.

"Suspended," said Tanaka. "Pending review."

"Yes," said M.K. "I thought it might be."

She did not ask how. She looked out of the window at the fog over the lake, and Tanaka stood by the bed and found that nothing else was required.

Then M.K. reached, with her right hand, to the drawer of the bedside locker, and the tremor came into the hand as it moved and went out of it again as soon as it closed on something. She drew out a small book. It was old, bound in faded cloth the colour of wet sand, no bigger than a hand, and its spine had been mended, neatly, by someone patient.

She held it out with both hands, as she held her cup, so that it did not shake.

"For the old gentleman," she said. "The last one, I think."

Tanaka took it. It was very light. It smelt of the ninth floor.

She did not open it or ask what was in it, because it was a patient's gift for a friend, and what was in it was not in the chart. She put it in her bag, beside the folded copy of the twelfth memorandum, and said goodnight, and went down in the lift and out into the logged, lit, remembering city, carrying it.
