Chapter 26: The Night of Copies

Friday, January 18 – Saturday, January 19, 2109. The resistance farmhouse, the border zone

Watch log. Friday, 18 January 2109. Farmhouse, border zone. Observer: Volkov, D.

Approaches: three. The farm track from the lakeshore road, eleven hundred metres, one gate. The field path from the north, unlit, soft after the thaw. The marsh edge behind the barn, passable on foot to anyone who knows it, which is four people, three of whom are in this house.

Method, as given by Osman, K.: every journal is copied by hand into two others, in two other towns. Each copy checks the other two. There is no index. There is no centre. If any one book is taken, two remain, and they agree, and where they do not agree the difference is written in the margin in another hand. No seizure matters.

Object tonight: one confession, dictated once, written twelve times. No original.

Dmitri Volkov wrote the last line and looked at it for some time.

He had spent four years in Border Security guarding the single record. Every report he filed went into it, and The Judge turned it into a probability, and the probability decided. Tonight he was to protect a night designed so that there would be no one true thing to protect.

He found he did not mind. He closed the log and went to count the doors.

At 21:00 the kitchen held seven people and a great deal of wire.

Nikolai had taken the dresser. In place of the plates stood a receiver, a small transmitter, a bank of old relay switches from a salvage dealer, and a notebook listing forty-minute intervals from nine in the evening to seven in the morning. At the end of each interval he would move the relay point to a different mast along the shore. Anyone listening for a fixed signal would find one for forty minutes, then nothing, then find it again somewhere else.

"It's not clever," he said, when Dmitri looked at the list. "It's just tedious. Tedious is harder to follow."

"Yes," said Dmitri.

Kira sat at the end of the long table with the roster on paper in front of her: twelve lines, twelve towns, twelve keepers' names, and beside each a second name, the hand that would copy beside the keeper. Dmitri had not been given the roster. He had asked not to be. He needed to know how many there were, and on which shore, and which one was late; he did not need to know which kitchens they were in. What he did not know could not be taken from him.

At the stove, in the chair Elena had moved there for her, sat Elise Vance.

She was very small. Dmitri had carried her across the drowned stretches of the causeway two weeks ago, and she had weighed less than a full kitbag. She sat very upright, with a blanket over her knees and her white hair cut short, and her right hand rested on the arm of the chair, and the hand trembled. Beside her, on a crate with a folded sack on it, sat Old Songs. He was not humming. Dmitri had noticed that at once. In the house on this side of the lake the old man sang with words every evening now, and hummed between, and was seldom silent. Tonight he was silent.

"Roll call," said Nikolai, and opened the line.

They answered by town. Six on this shore and six on the other, in the order Kira read them, each voice arriving thin and a little late through the old relays, as if from further away than it was.

"Kestrel Point," said the fourth voice. "Calloway. Ready. My daughter's asleep on the settle, so I'll be quiet."

Dmitri logged it. Kestrel Point: present. Keeper: Calloway, R. The other eleven he logged as present and nothing else.

The six on the Chronos shore answered last. Their voices were no different from the others. Dmitri knew, as they all knew, that from the first word each of those six had been speaking into a life-log, and that every word Elise Vance said tonight would enter six Chronos citizens' permanent record as they wrote it down, and the record of anyone else in their kitchens. In Chronos nothing could be taken out of a log. That was the gravest thing a person could do there. Nikolai had explained it to him once as a technical fact. Tonight Dmitri understood it as a tactical one.

When the roll call was finished, Kira put down her pencil.

"Twelve," she said, to the stove. "We could have had twenty. There are more than twenty books now that would have sat up tonight. You asked for twelve."

"Yes," said Vance.

"May I ask why?"

The old woman looked at her. It was the first time, Dmitri thought, that the two of them had been in a room together. Kira had come out that afternoon, for the first time since the crossing, and had been kept busy with the roster since. They looked at each other now across the length of the table, the young woman who had sat in a white chair and the old one who had designed the room it stood in.

"It is a number I owe," said Vance.

She said nothing else. Kira waited, as she waited for a system to answer, and when it did not she nodded once and wrote something small at the foot of the roster, and did not ask again.

Marcus was not there. Dmitri logged that too, because an absence was a fact. At 0.58 a single night in an outlaws' farmhouse, logged by any camera or any neighbour or any Chronos citizen who saw him, would carry him over the line. He had sent one line on paper with Kira, folded once, which she had read in the yard and put in her pocket. Then she had come in and squared the roster to the table's edge.

At 21:30 Vance began.

She did not stand. She did not raise her voice. She spoke towards the receiver on the dresser in a voice that was dry and exact and not loud, and she said each sentence twice, because people write slowly.

"My name is Elise Vance."

A pause. Twelve hands, in twelve kitchens, on two shores, and beside them twelve more.

"My name is Elise Vance. In the winter of 2064 I began to assemble a design for the government of frightened people."

The pause again. Then, again, the same words, at the same pace.

"I was twenty-eight. I was afraid, and I was good at my work, and I believed those were the same qualification."

Dmitri stood by the door into the yard, where he could see the kitchen and the window and the dark beyond it, and listened.

She said little he had not already read. That was not the point. The point was that she said it herself, in order, with her own name on the front of it, into twelve books at once. She spoke of the 2060s, and of the Fracture Years before them, which had frightened her. She spoke of a design assembled from the principles of seventeen contributors, and she did not name one of them. "Names would make this a court," she said, twice, "and a court is one record." She spoke of the introduction she had written that winter, and quoted one line of it, the line about a truth decided for a public, and did not soften it.

She said that the storms had been planned.

"The border between these two shores was left unresolved on purpose," she said. "It was left unresolved so that a population's tolerance for contradiction could be measured there, and raised. The storms that fell on your towns were not an accident of the overlap. The overlap was kept so that there could be storms."

Twice.

Dmitri thought of his father. He thought of the number 2471, and of the words lethal threshold confirmed, and of the page he had carried in a card folder inside his coat in November. His face did nothing. He had learned long ago that it did nothing when it mattered most, and he let it.

She said that the deletions from the record had been planned, and that the record had been built so that they could be. She said that in March 2066 she had written a warning in her own hand in her own copy, and that her listeners had read it, and that she had told no one.

"I was afraid," she said, "and I was right to be. I told no one, and I was wrong to do that."

Between paragraphs Old Songs handed her the cup. He did it without being asked, each time, at the end of a paragraph, as if he knew where the paragraphs ended before she did. Her right hand closed on the cup and was steady. She drank, and gave it back, and he held it on his knee until the next time. He did not hum. He did not look at anyone. Dmitri thought that he had not seen the old man so still since Wing C.

"I have lived quietly since," said Vance, near the end. "I will not say where, or under what name, or who kept me. They are owed that."

In the record of the nation across the lake, Dmitri knew, a patient called Mrs. Margit Kerr had died in a sealed ward on the fourth of January, certified in her son's hand. The men who were looking for Elise Vance tonight were looking for a living woman. Nothing on any page connected her to a dead one. She had just made sure that nothing would.

Security. 22:00–01:40. Farm track: clear. Field path: clear. Marsh edge: clear. Barn: clear. Elena Volkov at the gate, with a rifle. Rifle not raised.

He walked the approaches every forty minutes, when Nikolai moved the relay. It was cold and still. Out on the lakeshore road nothing moved.

At the gate his aunt stood with the rifle across her body and her collar up. She did not turn when he came up beside her. She counted him, he thought, out of the corner of her eye, the way she counted trucks back into the barn.

"She talks like a report," said Elena.

"Yes."

"I thought she'd talk like a sermon." She was quiet a moment. "Ivan would have argued with her all night."

Dmitri did not answer. There was nothing in it that needed an answer.

At 01:40 a car came along the lakeshore road from the east with its lights dipped, and slowed at the end of the farm track, and stopped.

01:40. Vehicle, dark saloon, lights dipped, halted at the track end. Two men out. Standing. Looking up the track. No torches.

"League," said Elena, very low.

"Possibly."

"They've got a leaflet face. Both of them."

Dmitri did not move. Elena did not raise the rifle. The two men stood at the end of the track and looked up it towards the one lit window of the farmhouse, and one of them said something to the other, and neither of them came any nearer.

Then, from further down the road, there were headlamps.

A gray vehicle came round the bend from the west at the regulation speed, with the Border Security mark on its door, and slowed as it reached the saloon, and stopped beside it. A torch came on inside it. It did not point at the farmhouse. It pointed at the two men. After a short conversation that Dmitri could not hear, the men got back into their car, and the car turned in the road and went back the way it had come.

The patrol vehicle did not follow it. It stayed where it was, at the end of the track, with its engine running and its lights on.

The radio on Dmitri's shoulder clicked.

"Border Security," said a woman's voice, flat and formal, on the frequency he had not used since December. "Routine patrol, lakeshore road, until morning."

He pressed the key. "Understood."

A pause. The carrier stayed open. Then, much lower, as if she were speaking to someone else in the vehicle and did not mind whether he heard:

"Tell her to go slowly. People write slowly."

The carrier closed.

Elena looked at him. He looked back at her. Neither of them said anything, because there was nothing to say that would be true and also useful. He had told Captain Sokolov the night and nothing else. He had never once known what she would do with what he told her.

01:52. Patrol vehicle stationary at track end. Lights on. Remaining.

At 02:35 the knock came over the line.

It came from one of the kitchens on the other shore. Dmitri heard it from the yard, through the open kitchen door: three knocks, heavy, official, and then a door, and then men's voices, two of them, speaking in the level Chronos way. Border directorate. We have reason to believe—

In the farmhouse kitchen Vance stopped in the middle of a sentence. Kira's hand went flat on the roster. Nikolai's fingers were on the switch that would cut that relay.

Then the keeper spoke. She was a woman, and not young, and she did not raise her voice.

"Wait," she said. "I'm writing."

There was a silence on the line. It went on for some time. Dmitri, in the doorway, found he was counting it. Seven. Eight. Nine.

"Would you say the last sentence again, please," said the keeper, to the line, to the farmhouse, to the woman by the stove. "I didn't get the end."

Vance said it again. She said it twice, at the same pace as before.

On the other shore the officers did not leave. Nor did they take anything. Through the relay, faintly, there was the scrape of a chair, and then of another. The Chronos men had sat down in the kitchen. They were listening.

Nikolai took his hand off the switch.

"That's two more logs," he said.

02:50–04:10. Read-back.

It was Kira's part of the method, and she ran it like an audit. Each keeper in turn read one paragraph aloud, from their own book, in their own voice, and the other eleven followed in theirs and said agreed or differs, and where a book differed the difference was written into the margin of every book in the other hand, and nothing was crossed out.

The six on this shore read first. As each one finished, Kira said released, and on this shore a courier left a kitchen with a second copy wrapped in oilcloth and began to drive towards the causeway. Dmitri logged each release. By 03:30 there were six.

The six on the other shore read after.

There were four differences in the whole night. Dmitri logged all four. A date written as a numeral in one book and a word in another. A which for a that. A sentence that one second copyist had written twice. And in one book on this shore, in the opening paragraph, the keeper had written frightened where Vance had said afraid.

Vance heard it in the read-back. She lifted her head.

"I said 'afraid'," she said, to the dresser. "Not 'frightened'. They are different words."

The keeper, far off, apologised. Vance shook her head, though the keeper could not see it.

"Write both," she said. "In the margin. Say which one I said."

The keeper did. Dmitri heard the pencil. He logged it: Difference four. Margin. Both words kept.

04:15. Alexei Volkov onto the Drowned Causeway, western head. Fog: dense, real, from 02:00. Load: six bundles, Veridican second copies, oilcloth. Exchange: at the Chronos end, with a courier from the other shore, who will be logged doing it. Radio: open, receive only on his side. Instruction: click once for moving, twice for stopped.

His brother had driven to the causeway head at 03:40 to meet the couriers, and had gone out onto the sleepers in the fog with the six bundles in a canvas bag against his chest, grinning, and had said on the radio, If I'm not back by breakfast, eat mine.

Dmitri stood in the yard with the radio and the watch and counted him across.

He had crossed the causeway twice, both times in fog, once with a dead woman in his arms who was not dead. He knew where the sleepers were awash and where they were gone, and how long each stretch took at the pace Alexei would go, which was faster than it should be. He counted the stretches in his head against the clicks. Moving. Moving. Moving.

At 04:50 the clicks became two.

Then nothing.

Then, through the radio, very faintly, the sound of a motor: not a boat, a vehicle, on the marsh road at the Chronos end. And Alexei's breathing, close to the microphone, slow and deliberate, the way he breathed when he had lain down in cold water and was telling himself not to shiver.

Dmitri did not need to see it. He could see it. Lamps at the Chronos end of the causeway. Directorate lamps, white, sweeping the reeds from the road. His brother flat among the reeds on the last stretch, with six books against his chest, in water to the waist.

He pressed the key and spoke into it low and even, in the voice he had used on suspects, on camera arcs, on frightened men in doorways.

"Do not move. The lamp is sweeping left to right. Eleven seconds a pass." He watched the second hand. "It is past you. It is coming back. Down. Stay down."

Two clicks. Stopped.

He counted. He counted the passes of a lamp he could not see by the sound of a motor he could barely hear, and he counted them aloud, in order, and every forty seconds he said the same four words, It is past you, and every time the radio answered with two clicks. Five minutes. Seven. His hands were very steady. His face did nothing.

At nine minutes the motor changed pitch.

"Stay down, Lyosha," said Dmitri. "They're turning."

He heard himself say it. He did not take it back.

The motor went away along the marsh road, and faded, and was gone. He counted sixty seconds after the last of it, and then another sixty, because thirty seconds counted.

"Clear," he said.

One click. Moving.

04:50–05:01. Near-miss, Chronos end. Directorate lamps, one vehicle, marsh road. Subject in reeds eleven minutes. No contact.

He looked at the entry. Then he added a line under it, in the same hand, because the log was a record and a record should be complete.

Contractions: one.

07:20. Alexei Volkov returned, western head, on foot. Wet to the knees and above. Load: six bundles, Chronos second copies, oilcloth. Condition: grinning.

He came up the farm track in the first gray light with the canvas bag held in both arms like a child, and his trousers black with marsh to the thigh, and his teeth chattering, and his face split open with that wide sudden grin that changed the whole of it. The patrol vehicle at the end of the track had gone at seven. Elena had lowered the rifle, which she had never raised, and leaned it against the gatepost, and she put both hands on his face when he reached her, hard, and then let him go.

In the kitchen he put the bag on the table in front of Kira and opened it.

Six books. Six second copies, in the handwriting of six people on the other shore, strangers, who had sat up all night in their own kitchens and written down every word of a confession spoken into their permanent record by a woman they would never see. Kira took the first one out and opened it. Dmitri looked over her shoulder. The hand was round and careful and slanted backwards. In the margin of the first page, in a different hand, was the word afraid, and beside it, frightened, and beside that, very small, she said: afraid.

"Six here, six there," said Alexei, dripping on the flagstones. "Every shore's got the other's handwriting. Nobody on either side can burn it without burning the neighbours'." He grinned at his brother. "You swore."

"I contracted," said Dmitri. "Once."

"I heard you."

"It was necessary."

"It was lovely." Alexei sat down heavily on the bench and began to unlace his boots. "Say it again."

"No."

Dawn came in over the lake, white and slow, the fog thinning to a low bright band across the marsh.

Dmitri stood at the kitchen window and wrote the last entry of the night.

07:40. Twelve towns. Twelve keepers. Twelve second hands. One confession. Original: none.

He looked at the last word. It was true. She had spoken each sentence twice into the air of a kitchen, and the air had kept nothing, and twenty-four people had kept all of it, no two in the same hand. If a man came to the door now with a warrant and a sealed bag, there would be nothing in this house for him to take that was not also in eleven others.

He had never once felt as sure of anything he had guarded as he felt of this.

Behind him, Kira had fallen asleep at the end of the table with her head on her folded arms and her hand still on the roster. Nikolai was switching off the relays one by one. Alexei was asleep on the bench in his socks. Vance sat by the stove with her eyes closed and the blanket over her knees and her right hand, for once, still, holding the old man's sleeve.

And Old Songs, who had not made a sound all night, began, very low, to hum.

It was not the Lakeshore Air. It was not anything Dmitri had heard in Wing C, or in the barn, or in this kitchen on any evening since. It was short, and plain, and it went round, and came back to its beginning, and at its end it lifted, like a question, and did not answer itself.

The old man hummed it again. Dmitri stood at the window and listened, and did not write it down.
