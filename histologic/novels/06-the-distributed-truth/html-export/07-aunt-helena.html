Chapter 7: Aunt Helena

Saturday, November 17, 2108. Kovač's flat, and the Chens' flat by the old river, Veridica Capital

The plant on the windowsill was dying of kindness again. Helena Kovač knew this, in the way she knew most things about herself, as a finding she had not acted on. She watered it on Saturdays and on Wednesdays, and on any other day when she came home and could not think what else to do with her hands, and its lower leaves had gone yellow and soft, and she had read somewhere that this meant too much water and not too little. She watered it anyway. It was a small, stupid, private way of being sure of something.

She watered it now, at a quarter to nine, and sat down at the table with her coffee and the files.

The flat had two rooms and a kitchen the width of a corridor. It was very neat, because she did not own enough to make it otherwise. The table by the window was the only thing in it that was ever untidy: a stack of intake procedures she was revising, in pencil, and a folder of release forms waiting for her signature on Monday, and her glasses case, and the small screen she used for the news.

At nine the screen gave her the weekly round-up. The Lakes Talks: the first week. She watched it the way she watched everything the state broadcast, with her coffee going cold and her attention on what was not being said.

The presenter was pleased with the week. There was the Old Customs House in the fog, very picturesque, with the Synthesis flag. There was Professor Park, small and white-haired, pouring tea. There was a line about "constructive first exchanges" and a line about "the delegation's firmness on the December interval" and a shot of Commissioner Chen at the table, thin and earnest, with his glasses pushed up, saying something the broadcast did not let her hear. Then a shot from a pool camera set high on a wall, looking down into a garden.

A gravel path. A bare tree trained flat against old brick. A wooden bench, and two men on it in coats, with cups in their hands. One of them was Marcus Chen. The other had turned towards him and was laughing, with his head back, at something Chen had said.

The frame lasted perhaps two seconds.

She put her cup down.

She had not seen that face in person since 2093. He had been nineteen, and thinner, and the hair had been longer and had fallen over his eyes, and he had not laughed like that, as far as she could remember, since he was a small boy. But she knew it at once, the way you know your own handwriting on an old envelope. The narrow face. The blue eyes, the family eyes, that Adrian had too.

Julian.

She sat very still at the table with her hands flat on the files.

She had followed his career for fifteen years from across the lake. It was not difficult. Chronos published everything; that was the whole of Chronos. Its Temporal Archive posted its appointments in its public lists, and she had read them, once a year, in the Commission's library, through the Synthesis exchange. Kovač, J., Archivist, Third Grade. Then Second. Then Senior Historian. Then the line she had read four times over, some years ago, sitting in a library carrel with her coat still on: holder of the Archive's senior reconciliation key. She had not known exactly what it meant. She had understood that it meant everything. The backups of a nation that recorded every heartbeat, and one key, and one man.

And now the man who held it was on a bench in a walled garden in the neutral zone, laughing, beside Veridica's most trusted restorer. The dead historian. The face of the Doctrine. The man every screen in the capital had called, for a year, the one who put it back.

She understood it in one frame. She had spent eleven years at Ashford learning to understand a room from a single glance at a roster. She did not need the rest of the broadcast. She turned it off.

The letter had come on Wednesday.

She had come home at seven and found it on the mat inside the door, pushed underneath in the afternoon by someone who had come up four flights of stairs and gone down again without knocking. No stamp. No address. Only H. Kovač on the front, in a hand she had last seen on a letter three years before, and before that on birthday cards, and before that in a child's exercise books.

She had stood in her coat in the doorway and opened it.

One line.

They're going to use him. Don't let them.

That was all. No signature. He had never signed anything; he had always said a signature was a lie you told a machine. She had read it standing in the doorway, and then sitting at the table, and then in bed with the light on, and she had not known who him was.

She had thought, at first, with a fear so sharp and so shameful that she had had to put the letter down, that he meant himself. That Adrian was in some kind of trouble in the strip, where the storms were worst and the outlaws lived, and that he was telling her, in the only way he knew, that someone was going to use him, and asking her, for the first time in eight years, to stop it. And she had thought, sitting there: and what could I do? And had been ashamed of the thought before she had finished it.

She had not answered his letter three years ago. She had not known how. She had not answered this one either, because there was nowhere to send it.

Now she took it out of her coat pocket, where it had been since Wednesday, and read it again at the table in the Saturday light.

Him. Not Adrian. His brother.

She worked, now, at the Commission. It had surprised her to be asked.

In February of the previous year the Ministry had dismissed her as Director of Ashford, without charge. Under the new Doctrine her ten false certificates had been amended in the record as corrected entries, which was a phrase she had turned over for some weeks, the way you turn over a stone you have been handed, looking for the insect underneath. There had been no prosecution. There had been, instead, a letter in the summer, from the Commission, asking whether she would consider the post of Registrar of Testimony.

She had considered it for a day. She had said yes.

The public intake was on the ground floor of the Commission's building, and it was hers. A plain hall, with a long counter and six writing desks and pens on chains. In the wall by the street door was a steel deposit slot, which anyone in Veridica could use at any hour of the day or night, and which dropped whatever was put into it into a locked receiving tray behind the wall. The hall opened at nine. Two cameras watched it, one over the counter and one over the slot. Every deposit, slot or counter, went into the intake log with the time and the item and the number of sheets, and when a Commissioner or a court wanted something released, she signed for it herself, on paper, in ink, and they countersigned.

She had insisted on the paper. The Commission's technical office had offered her a very good system. She had thanked them and asked for a ledger.

Paper could not be adjusted. It could be burned, or lost, or forged, but it could not be quietly changed in the night so that it had always said something else. She had learned that at Ashford, in four years of keeping a folder.

The folder.

She thought about it now, at the table, with Adrian's letter under her hand. Four years, at Ashford, of writing down the names of the patients the protocols went too far with. Three or four a year, and then more. Their names and dates and what had been done to them, in her own hand, in a folder in a locked drawer. Because on paper nobody could adjust them. Because she had needed, very badly, for there to be one true record of what was happening in her building.

And that was all she had done. For four years. She had kept a folder.

She had said it aloud once, at Ashford, with the recording off. I kept a folder. That's what I did. She had laughed when she said it, and it had not been funny, and it was less funny now.

The state was using her family. It had done it before. It was doing it again, now, on a bench in a garden, and she had seen it happen on a screen at nine o'clock on a Saturday morning, and she knew exactly what she would do if she did nothing. She would write it down. She would put it somewhere safe. She would be very precise about it.

Not again.

She pushed her glasses up her nose. Then she folded the letter back into its envelope and put it in her coat, and put her coat on.

The Chens' flat was on the third floor of a building near the old river, with a green door and a bicycle chained on the landing. Ms. Osman had been out since the morning, Mr. Chen said, when he opened it; there was a listening session in a district town. He said it as though apologising for her absence, and then as though he was not sure he should have said it, and stood back to let her in.

She did not go further than the door. She stood just inside it with her hands in her coat pockets, the right one closed round the envelope, and looked at the room: books, a kitchen table with a journal on it, closed, two chairs, a window onto the river. It was the flat of two people who had not yet got used to owning anything.

He had his glasses pushed up on his forehead and a pencil in his hand. He looked tired. He looked, she thought, like a man who had spent four days being very careful and had come home and found the care would not switch off.

"Mr. Chen," she said. "I won't keep you. There is something you should have, and something you should know. The second first."

He waited.

"The Chronos historian at the talks. The one on the broadcast this morning, in the garden." She said it flatly, in order, the way she would have briefed a committee. "His name is Julian Kovač. He is my younger brother's elder son. Adrian's brother."

Mr. Chen did not move. But she saw his hand close on the pencil, and she saw that he had known. Or had not let himself know, which with him, she suspected, was a separate column.

"He was born in 'seventy-four. Adrian the year after. At nineteen Julian went across the lake to study at the Temporal Archive's school, in Chronos. He did not come back. My brother had said he would disown him if he went, and he went, and my brother did." She paused. "Nobody in the family has said his name since, in my brother's hearing. It is as if there had only been one boy. And then Adrian went into the strip, and there were none."

"I'm sorry," said Mr. Chen.

"I drove him to the checkpoint," she said. "In 2093. His father wouldn't. He had one bag and a list in his coat pocket of things to take, with every item ticked. He thanked me at the barrier. Thank you, Aunt Helena. Very correctly. As if I had done him a service at a counter." She looked at the window. "That was the last time."

She had not meant to tell him the rest. She found she was telling it anyway, standing by his door with her hands in her pockets.

"He was a quiet boy. Very exact. Adrian took my kitchen clock apart when he was six to see what was inside it. Julian wrote down what time it said before he did it, and what time it said afterwards, and gave me the list." She did not smile. "He kept lists of everything. Mostly of what the adults said that didn't match. What his uncle said at Christmas and what the record said. What his mother remembered about a holiday and what his father remembered. What I had told him one year and told him differently the next. He kept them in exercise books, in columns, and he wasn't unkind about it. He simply wanted to know which was true. And nobody could tell him."

Mr. Chen had sat down, slowly, on the arm of the chair nearest the table.

"He wanted to live somewhere that forgot nothing," said Kovač. "So he went to the one country that doesn't."

She watched him hear it. She had watched a great many people hear things, across a great many tables, and she knew what it looked like when a sentence went in and found something already there. He was looking at the pencil in his hand. A man who had kept the entries of his own life in his head for months in a white room, so that they would not be taken; who kept columns; who checked. She did not need to say it, and did not. He heard himself in every sentence. She could see him hearing it.

"The state has used my family once," she said. "Adrian was sent to Kira."

She had read the acquisition file in February 2106, in her office at Ashford, with the recording off. Her nephew's name in it, in a Ministry typeface, as the person of interest who had made a young architect's contamination finding believable. She had read it twice and put it back and gone on being Director for six more months.

"Now you are sent to Julian."

She did not ask what he had been ordered to do. She did not need to. There was only one reason for a state to put its most trusted face on a bench beside the man who held its enemy's backups, and it was not the tea.

Mr. Chen did not deny it.

She noted that. She noted, too, that he did not try to, and did not look away, and did not say I can't discuss that, which was what a Ministry man would have said and what the file, whatever it was, would certainly have told him to say. He simply sat on the arm of the chair and looked at her, and let the silence be the answer, because he would not tell her a lie and could not tell her the truth.

It was, she thought, more than most of them would have given her.

"I have read his monograph," she said. "The one on the Lost Hour testimonies. The Commission's library has a copy through the Synthesis exchange. Nobody else has taken it out." She paused. "He cites you eleven times, Mr. Chen. In the footnotes he calls you the witness who kept his own record. He does not write like that about anyone else in the book. I looked."

Mr. Chen said nothing. He had gone rather pale.

"He will trust you," said Kovač. "He was always going to. You are the thing he went across the lake to find, and you are on this side of it." She heard her own voice, flat and dry, and let it stay that way; it was the only one she had for this. "I'm not going to ask you what you were told to do. I'm going to ask you something else." She pushed her glasses up. "What will it cost him, Mr. Chen?"

He opened his mouth.

"I'm not asking what it costs you," said Kovač.

He closed it again.

The flat was very quiet. Somewhere below, on the river path, a child was calling a dog that was not coming.

"There's one more thing," she said. "It's a question. I don't have the answer. I've been turning it over since nine o'clock, and I don't like it."

He waited.

"Chronos never lets Julian out of the Archive. He has not travelled, as far as any published list shows, since he was given the key. Not to a conference, not to the Synthesis, not across his own province. His monograph was presented in his absence. He gave his lectures from the Archive over the link." She had it in order in her head, from fifteen years of reading appointments in a library carrel, and she laid it out like a column of figures. "He holds the one key that can open four million people's past. Chronos does not let that key out of the building. It never has."

She looked at him over her glasses.

"So why send him to talks now?"

Mr. Chen did not answer. She watched him look for an answer and not find one. Then he reached across to the table, and pulled a small notebook towards him, and wrote something in pencil in a small, precise hand.

She could read it upside down from the door. She had read a great many forms upside down across a great many desks.

UNCHECKED.

He did not write anything after it. He looked at the word for a moment, and then up at her.

"I don't know," he said.

"No," said Kovač. "Nor do I."

She had said what she came to say. She turned to the door, and then stopped, with her hand on the latch.

Her right hand was still in her coat pocket. It had been round the envelope since she left her own flat. She had carried it to the Commission on Thursday and Friday, and had it on the desk beside her in the intake office, and had slept with it on the bedside table, and had not once, since Wednesday evening, let it out of her hand for anyone else to hold.

She took it out now and held it out to him.

"This came on Wednesday," she said. "Under my door. It is the first letter Adrian has written me in three years."

Mr. Chen took it. He opened the envelope carefully, the way a historian opens things, and took out the single sheet, and unfolded it, and read the one line.

They're going to use him. Don't let them.

She watched him read it twice. She watched it go in, and find something there too.

"It was addressed to me," said Kovač. "I think it was meant for you."

She opened the door and went out before he could answer.
