Chapter 24: The Remedy Was Ready

Wednesday, January 9, 2109. Marcus and Kira's flat, Veridica Capital

Incident: open. Opened 19:00, Wednesday, 9 January 2109. Location: the flat, front room, floor. Present: Osman, K.; Chen, M.; Reeves, M.; Volkov, N. Method: all material in date order, left to right. No conclusion until the row is complete.

Kira wrote the header in pencil at the kitchen table and laid it face up at the far left of the front room, against the skirting board, where the first sheet of a fault always went.

Then she cleared the floor.

It took eleven minutes. She rolled the rug, pushed the table against the wall and moved Marcus's stacks of books from under the window to the sill, one stack at a time, so that none of them would slide. What was left was pale old boards running from the door to the window, nineteen of them. She counted them twice. Nineteen boards, and ten weeks of material. It would fit if she gave each week two boards and did not let any day spread.

Marcus watched from the kitchen doorway with the kettle in his hand and did not offer to help. When she laid something out, it had to be laid by one pair of hands, or she could not trust the order.

Maya came at 18:52, eight minutes early, with a card folder buttoned inside her coat. Nikolai came up the stairs behind her at 18:58 with a folder of his own, and the look he always had when he carried paper through the city: as if the stairwell had been counting him.

Maya had brought the six sheets on Monday night, still in her coat, and had stood in the doorway while Kira read them. Kira had not drawn a conclusion then. She had spent two days checking each line against something that was not Maya: the dates against the Commission calendar, the routing codes against the intake's printed log, which Kovač had let her read at the ground-floor desk and then taken back. Every line had held. That was not a conclusion either. It was a condition for having one.

"Sit where you can see the whole row," she said. "Please."

They sat on the floor against the wall, Maya with her knees drawn up, Nikolai cross-legged with his folder on his lap, Marcus on the end with his lists folded in his hand. Kira knelt at the left and began.

Item one. Thursday, 1 November 2108, 21:00. Cabinet Office, top floor. KINDRED offered to Chen, M. Deadline one week.

Marcus had kept the card. She had not known that. He took it out of the back of his lists and handed it to her without comment: the First Minister's office, 21:00, tonight, alone. She had watched him put it into his inside pocket in the east corridor ten weeks ago. She laid it on the first board.

Item two. Tuesday, 6 November, 08:14. Technical Director's document store. First draft created: architecture later presented as the National Concordance of Testimony. Author office: Technical Director.

Maya's first sheet. Her handwriting was small and upright, with the times underlined twice, and at the foot of the sheet she had signed and dated it, M. Reeves, 7.1.2109, like a witness. Kira laid it on the second board, five days to the right of the card, and left the gap between them empty. The gap was the point.

Item three. Monday, 26 November, 06:58. Commission public testimony intake. Deposit: Principle Three, pp. 13–17, copy X. Public-deposit code. Camera: no visitor. Note: Chen, M. had sent a written question to the Technical Director's office on Friday 23 November, concerning the KINDRED annex. Reply promised for that Monday. No reply received.

That one was hers, copied fresh from the first journal. UNCHECKED: 06:58. No visitor. She had written it seven weeks ago and left it in its column, because a fact that cannot be explained should be recorded and not explained.

She laid it down. Her hands were steady. Somewhere under her breastbone, very quietly, the old voice began: Layer one: key exchange. Asymmetric, lattice-based—

She stopped, and counted the sheets in her hand instead. Nine left. She counted them again. Nine. The voice went back down.

Nobody had noticed except Marcus, who said nothing.

Item four. Wednesday, 28 November, 06:51. Intake. Deposit: Principle Five, pp. 23–28, copy X. Public-deposit code. Camera: no visitor. Note: on Tuesday 27 November the talks secretariat published the agenda for round three, "the claimed strip and the broadcast overlap".

"The border was on the agenda," said Nikolai, "and the next morning the page about the border was in the tray."

"Yes."

"And the week Marcus asked how you reach Chronos's true source, the page about the true source."

"Yes." Kira laid the sheet. "Don't draw it yet."

Item five. Routing layer, intake records, both deposits. Record created by: a terminal inside the Ministry network. Not by the slot scanner. Office: not identified. Method of paper entry: not identified.

Maya's fourth and fifth sheets. Kira laid them below items three and four, not beside them, because they were not new days. They were what lay underneath two old ones.

"The slot stamps every public deposit," said Maya. She said it to her knees. "It's the scanner that makes the record. Always. These two were typed. Somebody on the inside made a record for a thing that hadn't come through the slot, and gave it the public code so the desk would see an ordinary deposit." A pause. "Kovač said the tray has two sides."

"She said that to me too," said Kira. "This morning. As if she hadn't said it to you."

"She wanted it said twice," said Marcus.

Item six. Tuesday, 4 December. Sizing exercise assigned to Reeves, M. by the Technical Director's office as "archive capacity planning". Scope: "testimony, all sources, including handwritten, national scale". Completed in two days. Cited in one revision of the architecture, same week.

Maya's third sheet. She did not look at it when Kira put it down.

Item seven. Thursday, 6 December. Kestrel Point. First payment to Varley, E., contributor to the Kestrel Book, for false entries. Paid by a man presenting a card: "Ministry of Historical Integrity. Public Outreach." Office number given. No name.

This one was in Ruth Calloway's hand, copied on lined paper from the Kestrel Book and sent up the lakeshore with Alexei on the second of January: the church-hall evening, the red margins, Varley's confession, and two cards pinned inside the back cover, one cream and one white. Ruth had drawn them to scale, with a ruler, written their wording underneath in capitals, office numbers included, and had the page countersigned by her daughter. Kira had read it again last night with a calendar beside it.

"The first card says Ministry of Historical Integrity, Public Outreach," she said. "The second card, the twentieth of December, has the new crest and says National Concordance of Testimony, Outreach. Same office number on both. No name on either. Ruth took it for the Ministry reorganising itself. New letterheads, same clerks. That's what anyone would take it for." She put her finger on the date. "The sixth of December. The Concordance was announced on the eighteenth. He was paying a man to make the town's book look unreliable twelve days before there was anything to register it with."

Nobody said anything. On the wall above them the clock over the kitchen door went on.

Item eight. Sunday, 9 December, evening. Commission building, fifth floor, Technical Director's office. Chen, M. informs Wei, T. of the publication plan: Monday week, 14:00. Reply: "Good."

Marcus unfolded the page from his lists himself, and handed it to her. It was the same entry he had written that night, in pencil, and under it, in the margin, a later note in the same hand: Smile held. One beat too long. He had not shown her the margin before.

She laid it down.

Item nine. Monday, 17 December, 11:40. Technical Director's document store. Last revision of the architecture. Revisions since 6 November: forty-one.

Item ten. Monday, 17 December, 14:00. Publication, three nations.

She put the two sheets side by side on the same board, with no space between them. Two hours and twenty minutes. Forty-one revisions over six weeks, and the last one finished before lunch on the day the pages went out, as a person finishes the last line of a speech before they walk out to give it.

Item eleven. Tuesday, 18 December, 12:00. First Minister Maitland confesses KINDRED and announces the Concordance: four numbered provisions, an initial allocation of thirty-eight million, a date before The Judge. Elapsed since publication: twenty-two hours.

"Too well made," said Marcus. It was the first thing he had said about the row. "I said it that night. I couldn't find the word for what it was." He looked along the boards, from the card to the speech. "It was ready."

Item twelve. Tuesday, 18 December, afternoon. Licence issued for the League of the Record's stewards' channel, under the Ministry heading "Public order: support to lawful public assemblies". Source: public procurement register. From that day the line has paid for nothing except League assemblies.

Item thirteen. Thursday, 20 December. Second payment to Varley, E. Same man, same office number. Card: "National Concordance of Testimony. Outreach."

Nikolai leaned forward and took the two sheets out of her hand before she could lay them. He did not ask. He put them down himself on the last board, one above the other, and squared their edges with two fingers, the way his brother squared a folder.

"Two budget lines," he said. "One ministry."

He sat back.

Kira looked at the row.

Thirteen items on nineteen boards. It was not complete, because nothing was. But it had a shape now, and she let herself see it the way she saw a fault in a live system once every log was on the table: not as a story, which could be told any way you liked, but as a sequence of states, each of which made the next one possible.

"There's one more," said Marcus, "and it isn't a date."

"No," said Kira. "It's a sender."

Dmitri had driven the case notes in himself on Saturday night, the fifth, with the marsh still on his boots, because Tanaka had said for Commissioner Osman and he would not hand them to a courier. Kira had not yet been out to the farmhouse. Elena had closed the road to everyone for ten days after the crossing. So everything Kira knew about the woman in the east-window bed she knew in a Chronos physician's small, exact hand.

She took two sheets from her own folder and laid them below the row, not in it. They did not belong to a date.

03 January 2109. Evening. Patient states she sent three documents.

04 January 2109. Afternoon. Visitors show the patient photographs of five published items. Patient taps the lower corners of two of them (Principle Three, Principle Five). Patient: "Copy X isn't mine."

"Three," said Kira. "The Introduction. The strip. The Warning. Songbooks, bindings, the old gentleman. No numeral on any of them, because her copy has none." She touched the two sheets from the intake. "And two more that came another way. On a morning when we'd asked a question. On a morning when the border was on the agenda. With no visitor, and a record typed inside the Ministry."

"XI came out of the Ministry's own correspondence," said Marcus. He said it slowly, as if he were reading it off the boards. "X came in through the Ministry's own network."

Kira looked at him for a moment. Then she took a fresh sheet and wrote it down, exactly as he had said it, and under it: Status: guess. She laid it beside the case notes, face up, where everyone could see the word.

"It's a guess," she said. "I'm filing it as one. Nobody in this room knows whose copy X is. We don't know whose XI is either. We know where each one came in."

"Read the page," said Nikolai.

He meant Three. She took their published copy from the folder and read it aloud, in the level voice she used for everything that frightened her.

"Maintain three backup systems that appear independent and are not. Should outsiders corrupt the record, it may be restored from the true source. Should insiders reform it, it may be restored from the true source. Ambiguity, controlled, is both defence and instrument."

When she stopped, Maya reached across the row and put a small square of paper down beside it. On it, in her upright hand, was a component number, and under the number, four words.

Restore path from master.

"It's in every draft from the ninth revision on," said Maya. "Every registered page is scanned and sealed as a master. That's the part he presented. The part he didn't present is that the master can be restored to the index from the master copy, on the authority of the Technical Director, so that a burned book can be restored." She said the last three words in a voice that was not quite her own. "I read it twice on Monday. I'm a technician. I know what restorable means. If you can restore a page from the master, you can put the master back any way you like. Restorable means editable."

"From the true source," said Kira.

"I don't know the principles. I know components."

"You put it exactly that way."

Marcus had not moved. He was looking at the square of paper as if it were a face he ought to recognise and did not.

"If a book burns," he said, "we restore it." It was Wei's line from the session room on the eighteenth, the one that had made the room applaud. "He looked at me when he said restore. I wrote that down too."

"And then a book burned," said Nikolai.

Nobody needed him to say which. The chandlery on Harbour Street, on Christmas Eve, with a League leaflet pinned to its door that morning and Wren Calloway going out through the back window over the water. The Kestrel Book had survived in Ruth's coat. Nothing else in the reading room had. By the first week of January a great many people along the lakeshore were afraid of a fire, and a government was offering them, with great kindness, to keep a copy of everything.

"So this is what it is," said Kira, to the row. "An index of every journal in the country. Every keeper, every contributor, every countersignature. That's a list of everyone who remembers. Indexed. Weighed by The Judge. And every page held as a master that can be restored from the source." She paused. "We published the principle that says how that works. We published it at 14:00 on the seventeenth, in three nations, so that nobody could say it had been hidden. And twenty-two hours later they built it into the remedy and we applauded."

There was one more page: Singh's photograph of page 30, identified since the fifth of December as part of Principle Six by a strip of paper a finger wide.

Kira laid it at the right-hand end of the row, past the last item, on the nineteenth board, where the conclusion of an incident report went.

She did not read it aloud. Everyone on the floor knew it.

When the record fails, the people will believe whoever restores it. Arrange to be the one who restores it.

"The Lost Hour failed the record," she said, "and the Doctrine restored it. We were the restorers. We knew that in December." She looked along the row. "This is the second time. The record failed for seven months. Thirty-one dead. People who couldn't tell which pier was real. Then we published, the war stopped, and the record was restored." She touched the speech. "By absorbing every book that had kept it while it was failing."

"The League's the extreme," said Nikolai. "Burn them all. So that registering them all sounds like the middle."

"And Varley," said Maya, "so the free books look like they need it."

Kira did not answer. She did not need to. The row answered.

She thought of the handwritten page from the last binding. It will make whistleblowers into criminals and reformers into its own restorers. She had heard it read in a farmhouse kitchen in December and thought it described what had already been done to them: the Commission, the Doctrine. She had not thought it described what was being done that same week, with their help, while they counted couriers.

It was very cold in her. It was always cold now, when it was worst. She had stopped being surprised by that.

Marcus got up.

He went down the row slowly, reading it the way he read a shelf, one spine at a time. He stopped at the left-hand end, at the card and Maya's first sheet, and crouched down with his forearms on his knees and looked at them. He looked for a long time.

Thursday the first of November. Tuesday the sixth of November, 08:14.

Kira watched his face and did not help him.

"If I'd said yes," said Marcus, at last. He did not look up. "If I'd done what the folder said. Made a friend of him. Got the key. Run the annex. Chronos's Judge reads a record that doesn't support its 0.94, and the war's over by determination in a month." A pause. "Their way. Nobody publishes anything."

"Yes," said Kira.

"And I said no. I didn't say it to them. I said it in a garden, into Julian's log. And then we published." He touched the edge of Maya's sheet with one finger, not the card. "And five days after they offered it to me, somebody in the Technical Director's office opened a new document and started building the thing you'd need if I said no."

He stopped there. Kira saw him reach for the next word and saw him not take it. It was not one of his holes. She knew what those looked like. It was a word he had found and put down again, deliberately, because it could not be checked.

She did not say it either.

"Five days," said Marcus.

"Five days," said Kira. "That's what's on the floor. That's all that's on the floor."

He went back to the wall, unfolded his lists, wrote something very small under UNCHECKED, and folded them again.

"We take it to The Judge," said Maya. "On the fourteenth." It came out as a statement, and then she heard it and made it a question. "Don't we?"

Marcus shook his head. He did it gently.

"No."

"It's a public session. The drafts are—"

"The drafts are metadata," said Marcus. "Timestamps in a store that belongs to the office that made them. The first thing he'll say, very kindly, is that metadata can be forged, and he'll be right. The second is that someone on the technicians' floor pulled a restricted history without authority, and everyone will look at who's the best they have at intakes." He did not look at her. "And the third is the one I mind most. A hearing is one record. Put everything on this floor in front of one Judge, and it's weighed once, by people who've been ready for us since the sixth of November." He looked at the row. "We don't beat an index with a hearing."

Maya's jaw was tight. She was angry, not with him, and did not know yet where to put it.

Nikolai was looking at his hands. He had been looking at them for some time.

"Then we beat it with copies," he said.

Kira had been listening to part of that. With the rest of her mind, on the back of the header sheet, she had been drawing, and she looked down now and saw what.

Three small circles in pencil, joined by three lines into a triangle. Beside them another three. Then more: eleven little triangles, some sharing a circle with the next, so that the shape went on across the paper like a lattice with no edge. Books checking each other in threes. Every book held by two others, in two other houses, in two other hands. And nowhere on the page was there a line to the middle, because there was no middle. She had left it out without deciding to.

She did not explain it. It was not ready. She turned the sheet face down on the first board, where it had been, and put her hand flat on it for a moment.

Marcus had seen. He saw everything she did with her hands.

It was twenty to ten. The tea had gone cold in four cups along the wall. Outside, a tram went down the hill ringing once for the corner. The row lay on the boards in the lamplight, and it was, Kira thought, the cleanest piece of work she had ever seen. Not theirs. Every piece of it true, and every piece put where it would be found, at the hour it would be found, so that four honest people would do exactly what they had done.

She stated the problem, because that was what she did.

Input: a published design, a war, and four people determined to end the war by publishing the design. Expected output: exposure. Actual output: a national index of everyone who remembers, built on the principle that was published, run by the Commission that published it, and asked for by the public.

She looked at it until it was only words.

Then she looked up at the three of them against the wall: Maya with her chin on her knees, Nikolai with his hands open on his folder, Marcus with his lists.

"They didn't stop our publication," said Kira, very level. "They finished it."

Marcus looked at the row for a long time. Then, very quietly, he said, "Then we don't give it a centre."
