Chapter 27: The Compiler's Confession

Monday, January 21, 2109. The resistance farmhouse, the border zone

Sixteen days.

Elise Vance had kept the count since the morning she came off the causeway, because counting was a way of holding something, and her hands were no longer reliable for the purpose. Sixteen mornings in a farmhouse kitchen in the Veridican border zone. Sixteen mornings of the same stove.

It was an old iron stove with a crack across the door, and the fire showed through the crack in a thin orange line, like light under a door in a corridor. Mikhail lit it every morning before anyone else was up. She had watched him do it from the chair by the wall for sixteen days, and she knew his method now as she had once known the order in which he shelved the periodicals: paper, then kindling, then two split logs laid crosswise, then the damper opened the width of two fingers, never three. At nine he closed it to one. He did not look at it when he did it. His hand knew.

She had asked him, on the third morning, why two fingers. He had looked at the stove for a while and hummed a falling phrase, which she had learned meant no, and which in this case meant, she thought, no reason you would accept. She had let it go. It had been a long time since she had let an argument go with him. It had been a long time since there had been anyone to let it go with.

This morning her right hand was bad.

It was always worse in the mornings. It trembled at rest and stilled when it held something, which was what the tremor of her kind did, and which she had read about in the clinical literature long before it was hers. So she held things. The cup, when there was a cup. The edge of the table, which was scrubbed pale and had a burn mark near the corner in the shape of an iron. Once, at dawn on Saturday, at the end of the long night, Mikhail's sleeve. She had not decided to. She had found her hand there and left it, because to take it away would have required an explanation.

Her interface reported zero to a relay in a hospital on the far shore. In Chronos she was dead, certified in her son's hand. In Veridica she had never been anyone. She had not been so unrecorded since the year the interfaces became law.

It did not feel like freedom. It felt like being very light.

At 13:55 Nikolai's receiver on the dresser gave a small click and began to hiss.

He had patched it that morning before he left for the capital, and shown her the one switch she was to touch, and then, apologetically, taped over the others. The house was otherwise empty. Elena had taken the truck to the village. Dmitri was on the lakeshore road. Kira was somewhere in the yard or beyond it, collecting what the couriers had brought in overnight. Mikhail sat on his crate by the stove with his hands between his knees and did not look at the receiver.

At 14:00 her confession was read in the town squares of both shores.

She had not asked for that. It had been Kira's idea, and Ruth Calloway's, and the idea of a keeper on the other shore whose name Elise had not been told: that on the Monday after the night, at fourteen hundred, the hour the Lost Hour had begun and the hour the Day of Testimony began, one keeper in each of two towns would stand where the town gathered and read from their own book, aloud, all of it.

The line carried two of them at once.

"My name is Elise Vance," said a woman's voice, a little hoarse, with the lakeshore in its vowels. Behind it, wind, and a gull, and a crowd being quiet. Ruth Calloway, by the ferry office on Harbour Street.

And half a sentence behind her, from the other shore, a man's voice, younger, slower, exact in the Chronos way: "My name is Elise Vance. In the winter of 2064—"

She sat very still.

She had heard her own name read aloud perhaps a hundred times in her life. At school, at the university, at the defence of her dissertation, at the Archive desk when she signed in on Thursdays. Never like this. Two strangers, on two shores, out of step, saying it to crowds, each of them carrying the sentence a little way and handing it on, so that she heard the opening line twice, overlapping, the second voice entering as the first moved on. It was like a round. She thought that before she could stop herself thinking it, and then was irritated, because it was the sort of thing he would have said.

In the man's square, every listener's interface was recording. Every one of them now carried her sentences in a log that no one in Chronos was permitted to erase. It was their gravest law. She found that she could appreciate it without liking it.

"In the winter of 2064," said Ruth Calloway, "I began to build a design for the government of frightened people."

Assemble. She had said assemble. She had chosen it with care, twice, into a microphone, on Friday night. She had assembled it. She had not built it. The distinction had mattered to her for forty-five years. Other people had built it, from what she had put together.

The read-back on Friday had found no such difference, so it was not in the Kestrel book. It was in the reading: a woman in the wind who had said build all her life, of harbour walls and piers, and whose mouth had taken the word it knew. It did not matter. It was on the line now, in a square, in the ears of a town that had stood in the fog and watched its pier appear and disappear.

She let it stand.

Two voices went on, half a sentence apart, and carried her back to the 2060s, and she let them.

Thursdays. The reading room of the capital's Historical Archive, which smelled of dust and floor polish and, at eleven, of apples. She had come in from the systems department at the university with ink on her fingers because she still wrote by hand, in those years, when she wanted to think. She read what was on the periodicals trolley. That spring there had been a small quarterly in a plain gray cover, shelved by a junior archivist at Periodicals, Governance, 114, and she had read every issue the day it came in.

The junior archivist was twenty-five and had a notebook of lakeshore songs he took everywhere. He did not want them to be lost. He told her so, the first Thursday, as if it were an unremarkable thing to want. She was twenty-seven. She told him that rhyme was a compression algorithm: that a people without writing had found a way to make long texts short enough to carry in the head, with an error-correcting code built in, because a line that did not scan was a line that had been misremembered. He told her that rhyme was a kind of prayer. She had given him the prayer. It cost her nothing, and she had won anyway, as she always did, by conceding the first point and turning it. A prayer, she had said, was also a compression algorithm. It was simply one whose sender did not answer.

They had argued in whispers across the reading-room table for two years. At the next desk a senior archivist with a courtesy you could have set a clock by had pretended not to hear them, and at eleven had taken off his cotton gloves and peeled his apple with a pocketknife in one long curl.

In the winter of 2064 she had begun to assemble seventeen people's principles into one design.

She had been proud of it. She did not pretend otherwise, even now, even to herself, in a kitchen where no one could hear her think. She had been proudest of the introduction, and in the introduction proudest of one small word, a preposition, which carried the whole argument on its back. Truth, we have learned, cannot be decided by a public. It must be decided for one. She had changed by to for on the third draft and sat back in her chair at two in the morning with the feeling a mathematician has when a proof closes.

She did not let herself think the names. Not one of the seventeen. Not here, not now, not with her own voice going out over two squares on a patched line. She had not let herself think them in eleven years on the ward, and she did not intend to begin in a farmhouse. It was not superstition. It was method. A thing thought often enough is eventually said.

She had chosen the name Kerr for herself in 2098, on the day she was admitted to a sealed ward under her son's authority, when she needed a name that was not her own and would not be looked up.

She had chosen it for Anselm. He had been their teacher, hers as much as Mikhail's, though she had never been his junior and he had never once corrected her. He had been the last man she knew who thought an archive was where things are not lost. Not kept. Lost. He had made the distinction to her once across the reading-room desk, and she had written it down in the margin of her notebook, and had understood it only a great many years later.

She knew what had been done to him in 2067. She had known at the time. He had asked where the box had gone, politely, in writing, twice, and had been sent away to be helped, and had come back and peeled his apple in one curl and said, pleasantly, to Mikhail, that he did not know that shelf mark. She had already stopped going to the Archive by then; she had stopped the month the box went, and told herself it was prudence. She had taken his name thirty years later because she owed it to someone, and it was the only payment she could make that no one would notice. She thought about this for the length of one paragraph, as she always did, and then no further, because there was nothing further to think that would be of use to anyone.

"…the storms that fell on your towns," said the man's voice, from the other shore, "were not an accident of the overlap."

The Warning she had written in March 2066, by hand, in her own copy.

By then the document was typed and finished and was no longer hers. It had gone out. It was in other people's hands and other people's drawers, and she had understood, reading it through one last time at her own desk, that it would do exactly what it said. She had been right about everything. That was the discovery. Not that she had been wrong about the design, but that she had been right about it, completely, and that being right about a thing was not the same as being on its side.

I assembled these principles because I was afraid. I was wrong.

She had written that and told no one. Then she had gone quiet for forty-three years.

There was also a page she would not give anyone yet.

She felt it the way one feels a key sewn into a coat lining: a weight on the far side of a thought, exactly where she had left it. She did not go towards it. She turned from it, as she had turned from it every day since March 2066, and put her hand on the table's edge.

The readings went on. Two voices, two shores, her sentences arriving in a stranger's hoarse lakeshore contralto and a young man's careful Chronos tenor, a little apart.

She listened to them with the part of her mind that had once been paid to think about systems, and she found that it was thinking.

The design had been built to anticipate its reformers. She knew; she had assembled that part too. It modelled a reformer as a person with a centre: a leader, a movement, a commission, a court. One record to capture, or one to restore. It modelled every way a reformer might try to put the record right, and in every case it had an answer ready, because in every case the reformer needed the record to be one thing, in one place, with one keeper, and whoever held the keeper held the reform.

It had no model for this. A confession that existed only as copies. A book in every kitchen that had sat up on Friday, another beside it, no two in the same hand, and none of them the original, because she had spoken it into the air of a kitchen and the air had kept nothing. There was nothing to seize. There was nothing to restore. There would soon be, she understood, a reply to the design that the design had not written, and it would have the same shape, and the design would look for its centre and not find one.

She thought it calmly. She was not comforted by it. It was not the kind of thought that comforted. It was only the kind that was true.

She did not ask Tanaka for numbers any more.

She had asked, in the ward, for the first year. Rate of progression, expected course, the ordinary things. Tanaka had given them to her in her small, exact voice, without softening them, which was why Elise had asked her and not the others. Then, in the second year, she had stopped asking. It was not that she no longer wanted to know. It was that the answer had become the kind of number that changes nothing, and she had no use for those.

Her right hand trembled on the table. She let it, for a moment, and watched it, and then covered it with her left.

Soren had signed her death certificate on the fourth of January in his own hand. He had written the cause as consistent with her chart, which was exact, and he had straightened her collar in the mortuary, and gone upstairs to be charming. She thought of that once. She did not say it, even inside, in words. She let it be a shape, and then put it where she kept the page.

Mikhail was humming.

He had hummed it since dawn on Saturday, since the end of the long night, when he had sat silent beside her for ten hours and then, at first light, begun. It was short, and plain, and went round. It came back to its beginning. At its end it lifted, like a question, as every tune of his had lifted since she had known him, and did not answer itself.

The receiver on the dresser had gone quiet. The readings were over. Somewhere on two shores two crowds were standing about in the cold, not knowing what to do with their hands.

"What is it?" she said.

He did not name it. He looked at the stove, at the line of fire through the crack, and was quiet for so long that she thought he would not answer at all, as he mostly did not.

Then he said, "A song dies with the singer, unless it has many singers."

It was the sort of thing he had always said, when they were both young. A riddle, and not a riddle. The answer was inside the question, where he liked it. She did not argue with it. She found she did not want to.

He turned on the crate so that he was facing her, and sang it, with words.

> One hand writes and one hand checks, > one hand holds the light; > one voice fails and one voice mends, > and many make it right.

His voice was thin and cracked and entirely true. The last line lifted, and hung, and stopped.

He sang it again. Then the first line alone, and waited.

She sang it back. Her voice was very poor. It had always been poor, exact in pitch and slightly flat, which in the reading room he had once described, courteously, as the voice of a metronome with a head cold. She had not sung in front of anyone in fifty years. She did it now, and it came out thin and careful, and at the end of the line it lifted, because the tune did, and she could not have stopped it if she had tried.

He gave her the second line. She gave it back. The third. The fourth. The words were very simple. She would once have told him they were too simple, and been wrong.

Build by many hands. She had written that, at the end of the Warning, in March 2066, in her own copy, alone. She had written it the way a physician writes a prescription for someone else. She had never once practised it.

It was a round. He did not say so. He did not need to; she heard it in the shape of the tune, the place in it left open for someone else.

He began it. She came in on the second line, as he started it, and for a moment it held: his one hand holds the light against her one hand writes and one hand checks, two lines at once, two voices crossing and parting like the two readings on the line an hour ago.

Then he reached the third line, and the tune went to the place where a third voice should enter, under both of them, and there was nobody to enter.

It fell apart.

She heard it fall. She was listening for it, the way she had once listened for the place where a proof failed. The round was built for three. With two it had a hole in it, exactly the width of a voice, and the whole thing tipped into the hole and stopped.

He began again. She came in again. It fell apart in the same place.

The third time she tried to carry the missing line herself. She tried to sing her own part and the absent one, both at once, holding two lines of the same tune in her head and her throat, as if a single voice could be two if it was clever enough. It could not. She knew it could not before she tried. She tried anyway, and her voice went to pieces between the lines, and she stopped, and her right hand, which had been still on the table because she had been pressing it flat, trembled.

For one line, before it went, she was twenty-seven. She was sitting across a scarred table in the reading room with ink on her fingers, arguing in a whisper with a young man about whether a tune could carry a thought, and winning, and he was letting her. The green lamp. The apple at eleven. The smell of floor polish and dust.

Then she wasn't.

She was seventy-two, in a farmhouse kitchen, with a tremor in her hand and a man of seventy beside her, and the round had stopped because it needed a third voice and there were only two of them.

Mikhail did not say anything. He did not need to. He had written it that way on purpose. It was, she thought, the best argument he had ever made against her, and he had made it without a single word of his own.

He began it again.

The door from the yard opened.

Kira Osman came in with the cold on her coat and a canvas satchel over her shoulder, heavy with paper, and her hair coming loose from its knot. She had been out since morning. She had the look she always had, Elise had learned in three days, of a person counting something: the steps from the gate, the copies in the bag, the number of people in a room.

She stopped in the doorway.

Mikhail did not stop singing. Elise, after a moment, came in on the second line.

Kira stood in the doorway with her hand still on the latch and listened. She listened to one whole pass: the first line, the second line entering under it, the third line reaching for the voice that was not there, and the whole thing tipping and beginning to fall.

Her face did something Elise had not yet seen it do.

Then, without putting down the satchel, without closing the door, she came in on the third entry.

Her voice was off-key. It was not slightly off. It was a clear half-tone flat, and uncertain, and very quiet, the voice of a woman who, Elise would have wagered, had never once sung in front of another person and had not intended to begin today. She did not stop. She sang one hand writes and one hand checks under the two of them, into the hole in the round, exactly where the hole was.

And the round held.

It went on. Mikhail came round to the beginning again, and Elise followed him, and Kira followed her, still flat, not caring, and the three lines went round over one another in the warm kitchen with the fire showing orange through the crack in the stove door, and each time the tune reached its end it lifted like a question.

Nobody answered it. Nobody had to. It came round again.
