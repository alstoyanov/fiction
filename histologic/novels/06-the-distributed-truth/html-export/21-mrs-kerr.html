Chapter 21: Mrs. Kerr

Thursday, January 3, 2109. Ward 9S, the teaching hospital, the Chronos lakeshore city

03 January 2109. 04:02. Ward 9S, night. Visitors: three, unregistered, Veridican. Admitted to the ward by the attending physician (myself). Reason: a logged corridor is worse than a sealed room.

She wrote it in her head while she was still holding the door, and did not like how it read, and let them in anyway.

The linen room was the first door on the left inside the ward: a narrow room with slatted shelves on three walls, sheets and pillowcases and folded gowns stacked to the ceiling, and a single lamp. It smelt more strongly of the ninth floor than anywhere else on the ninth floor, because the linen went through the ward's own wash. Sharp, cold, faintly green. Yuki knew it from her own cuffs, every evening. She noticed it differently now, because the old man had lifted his head at the door and breathed it in, and then looked at her.

"Sit," she said. "On the step stool, Mikhail. Mr. Volkov, Mr. Volkov, you will have to stand. I am sorry. It is not a room for visitors. There are no rooms here for visitors."

Dmitri took the corner by the door, where he could see it open. Alexei leaned against the shelves with his arms folded and his wet boots leaving marks on the floor, and gave her a small, apologetic, unrepentant grin that she did not return.

"You understand the rule of this room," she said. "Of this ward. Nothing here is recorded. The walls are shielded. Every life-log that comes through that door stops at it and reads clinical seal until it leaves again. Mine, the nurses', the director's, the patients'. It is the only such room in Chronos." She heard her own voice, very low and very even, and made it lower. "It is not a hiding place. It is a clinical order. I would like you to treat it as one."

The old man began to hum.

It was barely audible: the Lakeshore Air, down in his chest, no louder than the ward's ventilation. She had heard that tune for a year through the walls of Wing C and she knew how to read it, as everyone who had been there knew. Rising meant yes. Falling meant no. Silence meant they're watching.

This was none of those. It was the sound he had made pushing the trolley past the glass: steady, pleasant, meaningless to anyone who did not know. He did not believe her about the room. He was being furniture, in case.

She found she could not blame him.

04:09. Director informed, per protocol.

She called Dr. Vale from the ward telephone at the nurses' station, a plain handset on a cord, because 9S ran on wires and paper and nothing else. The night sister had worked on the ward for eleven years and had never once asked Yuki a question she could not answer; she did not ask one now. She took her tea down the row of twelve beds to the far end and sat with the sleeping patients and their paper charts, and did not look towards the linen room at all.

Vale came up at 04:40.

He must have come from his flat across the road; he was dressed, and shaved, and his tie was straight. He was tall and stooped, as he had always been, and he had the manners he had always had, which were beautiful. He came into the linen room and looked at the three men one at a time, as a host looks at guests who have arrived early: the old man on the step stool, humming; the Volkov by the shelves; the Volkov by the door.

He did not look surprised. Yuki noted it, and filed it.

"Good morning," he said. "Please stay where you are."

That was all. He looked at her once, not unkindly, and went out, and she heard the door to his office close beyond the ward's wall.

Director's reaction: courteous. Surprise: none.

She did not finish the line. She put it in the place where she kept things, next to the others: an order blocked for now, a reason never given, a man who had stood surety for her in 2106 and walked her upstairs afterwards and never once explained why.

At seven the day sister came on and the night sister went off, and the ward woke up round them.

Her day on 9S was Monday, but she did the morning round anyway, as she would on any morning she found herself on the ward, because a sealed ward that stops running gets noticed. Pulses, pressures, the paper charts at the foot of each bed. Breakfast on trays. The lamp by the east window turned off, because the lake was light enough now. She did not go to the bed by the east window first. She went to it last, as she always did, and the patient was asleep, small and very straight under the blanket with her right hand folded inside her left, and Yuki stood for a moment and counted her breathing and did not wake her.

When she came back to the linen room, Alexei was waiting for her.

"He won't talk," he said, quietly, with a jerk of his head towards the stool. "He won't, here. So I will. I'm sorry, Doctor. I know what you wrote Kira."

"Then you know the answer."

"I know the question. It's not quite the same thing." He had the grace to look briefly ashamed of using her own distinction on her. "He thinks your patient's someone he knew. A long time ago. In the capital, in 2063. He read your notes and did the sum on his fingers and stopped humming, and I can count on one hand the times I've seen him do that."

Yuki folded her hands.

"I don't discuss patients," she said. "Not with you. Not with anyone."

"I know."

"I sent those notes to Commissioner Osman because a patient in my care has asked after a stranger for a year with an interest I could not account for clinically, and since the seventeenth of December she has asked every day, and eaten less, and I wished to understand why. I did not send them so that the stranger could walk across a marsh in the middle of the night and be brought into a sealed ward by—" She stopped. She had nearly said by a Chronos historian with an Archive card, and she did not want it said, even here. "By whoever brought you."

"No," said Alexei. "I know that too."

The old man hummed on the step stool, very low.

Confidentiality was the one wall she had built herself. Everything else in her life since 2105 had been built for her or against her. In a country that recorded everything, that wall was the only thing she had to give anyone, and she had given it to every patient on this ward, and above all to the woman by the east window.

She was not going to take it down for a guess.

At half past nine, the patient heard him.

Yuki was at the nurses' station writing up the round. The linen room was two doors down the corridor and round a corner, and its door was shut, and the humming inside it was no louder than it had been at four in the morning. She would not have said it carried at all.

Then the day sister came to the station, a little flustered, and said, "Doctor. The lady by the window is asking for you. She's sitting up. She says—" and hesitated, because it was not the kind of thing patients on 9S said. "She says who's singing the Lakeshore Air on her ward."

Yuki went.

M.K. was sitting upright against the pillows with her cup held in both hands. Her white hair was flat on one side from sleep. Her face, which was usually dry and exact and interested in everything, was not interested. It was very still.

"Doctor," she said. "Someone on this ward is humming the Lakeshore Air. Down the corridor. On the left." Her right hand trembled once against the cup, and steadied. "I would like to see him."

"There is no one on the ward who—"

"Doctor." Gently. The voice she used to correct a word in a memorandum. "I am seventy-two, and I am lucid beyond my chart, and I have heard that tune hummed exactly that way once before in my life. I would like to see him."

Yuki stood at the foot of the bed with the chart in her hand.

Presenting problem, she thought, because that was how she thought. A frail patient, long-term, asks to be introduced to an unregistered stranger on the strength of a tune. The stranger asks to be introduced to the patient on the strength of a sum. Neither will say what they believe. The physician cannot permit the first on a guess. The physician cannot refuse the second.

There was a way. She did not like it. It was the only one that protected the patient, and it meant taking one brick out of her own wall with her own hands, and seeing what came through.

"Stay here," she said. "Please."

She brought him out of the linen room into the corridor.

It was an ordinary hospital corridor, pale and clean, with a handrail along one wall and a window at the end over the lake. The old man stood in it in his too-big coat, humming, with his pale eyes on her face. Alexei had come to the linen-room door behind him and stopped there. She did not tell him to go back in.

"Mikhail," she said. "Listen to me carefully. I am going to tell you the name she is registered under on this ward. It is a breach of her confidence, and I am doing it for one reason only. If it means nothing to you, you'll go home on the next fog, and you will never come back, and I will never speak to any of you about this again. Do you understand?"

He hummed. A rising phrase, very small. Yes.

She had not said it aloud in two and a half years. On the ward it was M.K., and on the charts it was M.K., and in her notes, always, it was Patient M.K. The ward's one courtesy. She had written the full name exactly once, on the day in August 2106 when Vale handed her the file, and she had closed the file and never written it again.

"Margit Kerr," said Yuki.

The humming stopped.

It stopped in the middle of a phrase, at the place where the tune began to lift, as if a hand had been laid over it. And the corridor went quiet. Not the ordinary quiet of a ward in the morning, with trays and a trolley and somebody's radio in the day room. A different quiet, which she had not heard since Wing C, when the old man with the trolley went silent outside a cell and every prisoner on the corridor held still behind the glass.

Behind him, in the doorway, Alexei had gone completely still.

The old man's face did something. She watched it, because watching faces was her work, and she could not read it. It was not grief exactly, and not fear. It was the face of a man who has been handed something he put down a very long time ago and had not expected to be given back, and does not know yet whether he can hold it.

The name meant something to him. That was all she could see. What it meant, he did not say, and she did not ask.

She went ahead of him down the corridor to the bed by the east window.

The woman in the bed turned her head.

She was small and very upright against the white pillows, in a plain gray cardigan, with her white hair cut short. She had put the cup down on the locker and then picked it up again, and was holding it in both hands in front of her, to still the right. Behind her the window was full of the lake, flat and pale all the way to the Veridican shore.

She looked at the old man for a long time. He stood at the foot of the bed with his hands in his coat pockets and looked back at her.

"Hello, Misha," she said. "You look terrible."

He did not answer with words. He hummed one phrase: the third phrase of the Lakeshore Air, the one that climbed. It rose, and lifted at the end, and stopped there, like a question that already knew its answer.

Yuki took the pen from her breast pocket.

03 January 2109. 09:41. Ward 9S. Patient M.K. Visitors: three, unregistered. Patient identifies one visitor as "Misha". Visitor responds with a rising phrase (Wing C: yes).

She wrote on the back of the patient's own chart, standing. Her hand was steady. She was aware of it being steady, the way she was aware of Morrison's hands.

The patient had not taken her eyes off the old man.

"Doctor," she said, without looking round. "You'll want to write this down, I expect. You write everything down. It's one of the things I like about you." A pause. "My name is Elise Vance."

09:44. Patient M.K. states her name is Elise Vance.

The name meant nothing to Yuki. She wrote it anyway, exactly, and waited.

The old man had taken one hand out of his pocket and put it on the rail at the foot of the bed. That was all. He held on to it.

"I sent you the songbooks," the patient said to him. "Through the doctor. The blue one had my Introduction in it. I wrote it in the winter of sixty-four." Her voice did not change at all. "I thought you'd know it by the second paragraph. Did you?"

A rising phrase. Softer.

"The first," she said. "Of course you did."

09:46. Patient states she wrote the Introduction carried in the blue songbook. Carried by me.

Yuki looked at the last three words for a long moment.

She thought of the blue songbook in her bag on the fifth of November, in the lift, smelling of the ninth floor. She thought of the third book on the third of December, and the fourth on the seventh, the last one, held out to her with both hands. For the old gentleman. She had carried them across the lake three times. She had handed them over at the checkpoint to a Volkov, under her own name, with her own face logged by every officer on the Chronos side of the barrier. She had never once opened a binding. She had thought them a patient's kindness to a stranger she admired on a screen.

On the seventeenth of December she had read the Introduction with everyone else in three nations. Truth, we have learned, cannot be decided by a public. She had read it standing by the window of this ward, ten metres from this bed.

She set her pen down on the locker, very carefully, the way you put down something you are afraid you might drop.

"Soren," said the patient, without looking round. "Close the door. You're letting Chronos in."

Yuki turned.

Dr. Vale was standing in the doorway at the end of the row, one hand still on the frame. He had come in without a sound. He looked at the old man at the foot of the bed, and at his mother, and at Yuki, and then he stepped inside and closed the door behind him, gently, as he did everything.

"My son," said Elise Vance to Yuki. "You've met."

Yuki picked up her pen again.

Director present. Director states he is the patient's son.

He had not stated it. He had not said a word. He stood by the door with his hands folded and did not deny it, and that was a statement; she had been a physician long enough to write down what a silence said. She looked at what she had written, and then she added two lines under it, because the record had to be accurate, even the part of it that was only hers.

Hypothesis, August 2106: the director has a daughter.

Revised: the director has a mother.

Give her my regards, he had said in November, in the corridor outside, as she went in with the circular. She had taken it for his manners.

He asked her, courteously, to step into the corridor.

They stood by the window at the end of it, with the lake behind them and the handrail between them, and he told her. He did not lower his voice. There was nothing on this floor to lower it for.

"In August 2106," he said, "my mother read Mr. Okonkwo's article. The one about Ashford. She read it on the day-room screen, in there, and she asked the sister for a printed copy, and she read it again. Then she sent for me." He looked at the lake. "She asked me to find the émigré physician who had come over the border in August, and take her in, and keep her safe. She said you were proof it could be survived."

Yuki said nothing.

"So I did. I gave you her case myself, because she wanted you. I refused the salience proposal in this hospital because she asked me to. I refused stabilisation because she asked me to. I was overruled on the second and I told you so, and she asked me to tell you so myself." He paused. "I stood surety for you because I'd have done it anyway, by then. I should like you to know that part is mine."

"You never told me why," said Yuki.

"No."

"In two and a half years."

"No." He turned his head and looked at her with his beautiful manners, and she understood that he was not going to say he was sorry, and that he was not sorry, and that he would do every part of it again. "She asked me not to."

The day went on, because it had to.

A sealed ward that stops running gets noticed, so she ran it. The lunch trays came and went. A patient in the third bed slipped reaching for her slippers, and Yuki knelt on the floor with her and checked her wrist and her hip and filled in the incident form in triplicate. The three men stayed in the linen room, except when she let the old man sit in the visitor's chair by the east window, and then Alexei stood in the corridor with his back to the wall and Dmitri stood in the linen-room door and counted the people who came and went, and there were only ever four.

She did not ask what the two old people said to each other. When she passed the bed, they were mostly not saying anything. Once she heard the woman laugh, a short dry sound, and the old man hum something that fell.

At four the light went off the lake, and she stood at the window at the end of the corridor and looked out.

There was no fog. The lake was clear all the way to the Veridican shore, a long flat line of dark water under a sky going green at the edge, and on the far side, very small, she could see the floodlights of the lakeshore checkpoint coming on one after another. Nobody could cross the causeway in that. Alexei had said so at noon, flatly, looking at the same view: not tonight. So they would wait. On a ward where nothing was recorded, in a building where everything outside this floor was, they would wait for the weather.

Complicating factor, she thought, and did not write it down.

In the evening she went to take M.K.'s pulse.

The lamp by the east window was on again, low. The other patients were settling. The old man was in the visitor's chair beside the bed, with his hands on his knees and his coat folded over the back of the chair, and he was not humming. She noticed that before anything else. It was the first time since four in the morning that she had come into a room with him in it and heard nothing.

The patient was sitting up with her cup. She let Yuki take her left wrist without looking at her, and Yuki counted, and the pulse was a little fast and quite regular.

Then Elise Vance leaned towards the old man, very slightly, and spoke to him so quietly that it was plainly not meant for anyone else.

"I sent you three things," she said. "Only three."

He looked at her.

Yuki finished counting. She wrote the pulse on the chart. Then, underneath it, in the same hand, because she had written everything else and could not now start choosing, she wrote:

Patient states she sent three documents.

She capped the pen.

And then, standing at the foot of the bed with the chart against her chest, she counted in her head, as she had counted the songbooks, as she would count the steps of a checklist. What had reached Veridica. What had been read on every screen in three nations at two o'clock on the seventeenth of December, with Commissioner Chen on the Justice Center steps and the war stopping by nightfall.

The Introduction.

Principle Three.
