Chapter 19: The False Entry

Thursday, December 27, 2108. Kestrel Point, the claimed strip

The chandlery smelled of wet char. Three days had not changed that. It had rained on the twenty-fifth, a thin cold rain off the lake, and the rain had got into everything that was left and made it worse: the black beams, the heap where the ceiling had come down, the long table under the window that was now only a shape in the ash, like the outline of a body on a pavement.

Ruth stood where the table had been, as near as she could judge it, with her hands in her coat pockets and the book against her ribs.

It had been in her coat since Monday night. She had not taken it out except to write in it, and when she wrote in it she did not let go of it with her left hand. She knew that this was not reasonable. She had not tried to be reasonable about it.

Wren stood beside her in the doorway, which was no longer a doorway, only a gap with the frame burned out of it. She had a cough that came in threes, and a clean white bandage round her left wrist where the window frame had caught it, and she was furious. She had been furious since Christmas morning. She had woken up furious and eaten her breakfast furious and gone round Harbour Street with the bakery sisters picking glass out of the slip, furious, and Ruth, who had spent October waiting for her daughter to feel anything at all about anything, took it as a good sign.

"They pinned it on the door," said Wren. "In the morning. So we'd see it. So we'd know it was them."

"We don't know who it was."

"We know who wanted it."

Ruth didn't answer that, because it was true, and because she was thinking about something else.

A book could burn. She had known that, in the way you know that boats can sink. Now she knew it the way you know it when you have stood on the slip in the dark and watched one go down. If the book had been on its table on Monday night instead of in her coat, there would be nothing now. No doubled pier. No April pages. No night the war stopped. Seventeen people's handwriting, and the arguments in the margins, and all of it gone in the time it took to stack chairs.

And if a book could burn, it could be got at in other ways. A thing that could be destroyed from outside could be spoiled from inside. She did not know where the thought had come from. It had been there since Tuesday, sitting in her like a stone.

"We're going to read it," she said.

Wren looked at her.

"All of it. Out loud. Every entry, from April. With everyone who signed there to hear it." She took her hand out of her pocket, and the book with it. "Every countersignature checked by the person whose name it is. If it's your hand, you say so. If it isn't, you say so."

"That'll take all day."

"Then it'll take all day."

It took three hours, in the end, in the church hall, with the folding chairs in a ring and the tea urn ticking on the side table.

Ruth had sent Wren and the bakery sisters up and down Harbour Street at noon to say two o'clock, and by two o'clock the ring was full. Sixteen contributors and herself. Some of the neighbours who had only ever countersigned. The schoolmaster had brought his own pencil. Mrs. Aske sat nearest the urn with her stick across her knees and said she was not missing this, not after Monday. Emmet Varley came in last, apologising for the draught, and took a chair near the door.

Ruth read. When her voice went, the schoolmaster read, and then the engineer. Every entry, in order. After each one the reader stopped, and looked up, and whoever had countersigned it said mine, or came across the ring and looked at the page and then said mine. It was slow. It was, Ruth thought afterwards, the most boring thing she had ever organised, and she had organised nineteen years of ferry timetables. Nobody left.

Most of it held.

The April pages held: the foundation stone of the church hall, in the schoolmaster's hand; the old harbour wall; the pier pulled down in 2089, with the day and the cranes. Every hand that had signed them said mine to its own. The night of the pier held, Ruth's entry, the engineer and his wife underneath, and in the margin the wife's 21:52 beside Ruth's 21:50, both still there. The engineer's wife read her own margin aloud and said, "Well, I still think it was fifty-two," and people laughed, which was the first time anyone had laughed in that hall since Monday.

The Chronos charter held, the night half the street had come out and told each other there was a plaque by the church. Under it, in November, somebody had written in the margin there is no plaque, and under that, in another hand, I went and looked. There is no plaque, and under that, in a third, I remember a plaque. I have gone and looked. There is no plaque. I still remember it. All three said mine.

The night the war stopped held, with its margins fuller than its page.

It was after four and nearly dark outside the high windows when they came to the eighth of December.

Sat 8 Dec 2108, 02:00. Lower Harbour Street, the old pier. A Chronos boat, gray, no lights, tied up at the old pier. Three men ashore. They stood on the quay for about ten minutes looking up the street, then went back aboard and the boat went out. Witnessed. E. Varley.

And under it, two countersignatures.

Ruth read it aloud, and stopped, and looked up, the way she had after every entry.

Nobody said mine.

She knew the two names. Everyone in the hall knew them: a brother and sister, both past sixty, who had mended nets in the loft at the bottom of Harbour Street for as long as anyone could remember, and who had signed the book a dozen times between April and the summer.

"They're not here," somebody said. "They must be away."

"They've been away since August," said Ruth.

She had known it before she finished reading the entry. She had known it, if she was honest, as soon as she saw the names, and had read the entry through anyway, because that was the rule. In August the storms had been at their worst, and in the third week of it the ferry office had run four evacuation sailings for the lower end of Harbour Street, fifty-six people down the shore to the towns where the storms were thinner, and Ruth had written the manifests herself, name by name, in the same hand she wrote everything. She had written those two names. She had watched them go up the gangway with a suitcase each and a birdcage with nothing in it, because the bird had died in July and the sister couldn't leave the cage.

They had not come back. Nobody from the lower end had come back. The loft was shuttered. Their names were on a manifest in the ferry office drawer, and they could not have been standing on a quay in Kestrel Point at two in the morning on the eighth of December, and they could not have signed anything.

The hall had gone quiet.

She sent Wren for the manifests. It was two minutes to the ferry office and two back, and Wren did it in three, coughing, with the key on its string round her wrist and the August ledger held against her chest like something that might be taken off her. Ruth opened it on her knee to the third week and laid it on the floor of the ring beside the Kestrel Book, open at the eighth of December, so that anyone who wanted could look at both. The names were in her hand on the manifest, with the date and the boat and 1 cage, empty in the column for goods. People got up and came and looked, one after another, and went back to their chairs and did not say anything.

"There are more," said Ruth. She turned the pages. She had been turning them in her head as she spoke. "Varley's. From the eighth."

There were six. Lights on the old pier at 01:40 on the eleventh. A man in a Chronos officer's coat on the lakeshore road at 03:00 on the fourteenth. A Chronos flag on the harbour wall at dawn on the nineteenth, gone by eight. A boat again, on the twenty-first. Voices on the water, on the twenty-second. Every one of them at the lower end of Harbour Street, where nobody lived now. Every one in the small hours, when nobody else was about. Every one countersigned by the same two names, and not one of them with a word in the margin in anybody else's hand, because nobody else had been there to disagree.

That was the point, Ruth thought. That was the whole point of it. The book worked because people argued in its margins. Here was a place in it where nobody could.

She looked across the ring at Emmet Varley.

He admitted it within a minute.

He didn't argue. He didn't even look surprised. He sat on his folding chair by the door with his hands on his knees and went slowly pink from the neck up, and said, "Yes. Yes, I did. I'm sorry, Ruth."

"You signed their names."

"I practised." He said it as if it were a thing he was ashamed of on its own account, apart from everything else. "At the kitchen table. In the evenings. Two different hands. I had their signatures from the old pages, from the summer, and I practised them until they looked right." He looked at his hands. "I'm not very good at it. I didn't think anyone would look."

"Nobody looks," said the schoolmaster, "at the lower end."

"No," said Varley. "That's why."

Nobody in the hall knew what to do with him. Ruth could see it. He had been a contributor since April. He had written some of the best entries in the book, long and vivid and full of things other people hadn't noticed, the storm on the fourth of November with more detail than anyone else remembered, all of it true, all of it countersigned by people who had been there and said mine. He had brought biscuits on the seventeenth. He had carried Mrs. Aske's coal in October when her son was stuck down the shore. He was a decent neighbour, and he had sat at his kitchen table in the evenings learning to write in two dead-looking hands.

It was a book of many hands. So it argued in many voices.

"Out," said one of the bakery sisters. "Tear the pages out. Strike his name off the front. He's not a contributor if his entries are lies."

"Only six are lies."

"Six is enough."

"And the rest? The fourth of November? You want to tear that out too? That's true. I was there. I signed it."

"Then we keep his true ones and burn the—"

"Burn them," said the engineer, heavily. He was an old man and he had been sitting very still. "Burn his pages. That's what the League would do."

He heard himself. Ruth saw him hear himself. His face changed, and he sat back in his folding chair and put his hand over his eyes, and did not say anything else for a long time.

"Probation, then," said the schoolmaster, into the quiet. "He stays. His entries stay. Every entry he writes from now on needs a third signature, not two. Someone he didn't choose."

"And the six?"

"The six come out. Obviously the six come out."

Wren was sitting on the edge of the stage with her bandaged wrist in her lap, coughing in threes. She hadn't said anything in three hours. She said now, not loudly, in the ordinary voice she used for pointing out that the milk was off:

"If you tear them out, nobody will know he did it."

People turned.

"If you tear them out," said Wren, "the book looks like it was always right. And then next time somebody does it, nobody'll know to look. Mark them."

There was a silence in the hall that Ruth would remember for a long time: the particular silence of a room full of adults being told something obvious by a child and realising that it was obvious.

They voted by show of hands, and Ruth counted, and wrote the vote into the book with the date and the count, because a decision the book made about itself ought to be in the book. Exclusion: four. Probation, with the six entries removed: five. Probation, with the six entries kept and marked: eleven, and Varley did not vote, and nobody asked him to.

So Ruth took the red pencil from the ferry office, the one she used for cancelled sailings, and down the margin of each of the six entries, beside the practised signatures, she wrote in her own hand:

FALSE: countersignatures forged, confessed Dec 27, 2108.

Then she held the book out across the ring to Emmet Varley, with the red pencil.

He looked at it for a long moment. Then he got up and came across and took the pencil, and under each red line, six times, in his own round eager hand, he signed.

It was the being forgiven that undid him. Ruth saw it happen. He had been braced for the bakery sister, and for the pages torn out, and his name struck off the front. He had not been braced for eleven hands going up, and the book held out to him, and his own name under the red. He went back to his chair and sat down and began, quite quietly, to tell them the rest.

A man had come to his door. The first week in December, a Thursday: the sixth, because it was the day the freight barge was late and he'd been waiting in for his paraffin. A pleasant man, nicely spoken, in a good coat, not from the lakeshore. He said he was from the Ministry, public outreach, and that the Ministry was surveying the town books, the journals, to understand them better. He'd heard the Kestrel Book was one of the best. He'd heard Emmet Varley was one of its best writers.

"He'd read my entries," said Varley. "The fourth of November. He said it was vivid. He said I had an eye."

The man said the Ministry was worried. Not about Kestrel Point; about the other books, in other towns, the ones that weren't as careful. Anyone could write anything in a book like that. The Ministry needed to show people that an unregistered book couldn't be trusted, not really, not without somebody checking it properly. He wondered whether Mr. Varley might write a few entries. Vivid ones. Just to show how easily it could be done.

He paid in cash, and he left a card.

"I thought it was a kind of test," said Varley. "I thought, they'll come and check, and they'll find it, and it'll show them. I didn't think—" He stopped. "I didn't think about the net-loft people. I just needed two names from the lower end."

He came back, the pleasant man. On the twentieth, the Thursday after the First Minister's speech. Same coat, same manner, very pleased with how the entries had gone. He paid again, more. And he left a different card.

"He said they'd reorganised. He said the book would be registered soon anyway, now, with the Concordance. He said it would be safer for everyone once it was."

Varley took out his wallet and found both cards and held them out, and Ruth got up and took them.

The first, cream, the Ministry's crest: Ministry of Historical Integrity. Public Outreach. An office number. No name. The second, white, with the new crest from the broadcast, the open book under the two joined hands: National Concordance of Testimony. Outreach. The same office number. No name.

They've reorganised, Ruth thought, turning the second card over. Of course they had. The whole government had turned itself inside out in a week. Men who had been one thing on Monday were another thing by Thursday. She had seen it happen to the ferry company's head office twice in nineteen years, new letterheads, same clerks.

She opened the book at the back cover, inside, and took two drawing pins from the notice board by the urn, and pinned both cards there, side by side, the cream and the white. Then she turned to the next clean page and wrote down what Varley had told them, in order, with the dates he had given, the sixth and the twentieth, and the words he said the man had used, as near as he could remember them, and the cards, and where she had pinned them. She read it back aloud. Varley said yes, that's right. The schoolmaster and the engineer and Mrs. Aske signed beside it, three hands, and Mrs. Aske pressed so hard with her old fingers that the pencil broke.

They walked home in the dark, she and Wren, up Harbour Street in the cold. The fog had not come in tonight. The air was clear and still, and the lake lay black and flat at the end of the street, with no pier on it, only water, and far off across it the lights of the other shore.

They passed the chandlery. It was only a shape now against the lights, a black shell with the sky through its roof. Ruth stopped in front of it without meaning to, and Wren stopped too.

She saw it then, all of it, standing in the road with the book against her ribs. It was very plain once you looked at it.

The League had wanted the book burned. They had said so on their leaflets and pinned one to the door. Burn the books, restore the pure record, one nation, one record.

The Concordance wanted it registered. It had said so on every screen in the country, kindly. No one compelled. Keepers protected. And it had sent a pleasant man in a good coat to pay Emmet Varley to make the book look as if it needed to be.

One wanted it gone, and one wanted it kept. They were not the same thing, and she did not think they were the same people. But neither of them had asked the book. Neither of them wanted it left alone in a kitchen, arguing with itself in the margins, in seventeen hands, checked by nobody but the people who had been there.

Neither of them wanted it free.

At the kitchen table she took the book out of her coat and opened it to the page after Varley's, and wrote it down, plainly, as she had just thought it. Then she turned the book round on the table.

Wren looked at it. Then at her.

"Countersign it," said Ruth. "If you saw it too."

Wren read it through twice, with her bandaged wrist resting on the edge of the table. Then she took the pencil, and signed.
