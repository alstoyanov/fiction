Chapter 1: The Seventeen Minutes

Thursday, November 1, 2108. The Justice Center, Veridica Capital

Thursday 1 November 2108. 09:52. Justice Center, great hall, ground floor. Session: Historical Integrity Commission, ordinary sitting. Cameras: eleven. Broadcast light: on. Public benches: full. Agenda item one: casualties. Agenda item two: attribution. Observer: Osman, K., commissioner.

Kira counted the cameras on the way in because she always did, and because the number had gone up.

There had been eight at the inaugural session, in January of last year. Two had been added in the spring, when the broadcasters asked for better angles. The eleventh was new this week. It was set high in the east wall above the public benches, a small dark dome with a green light, pointing down at the commissioners' table. At her. At the chair to her left, where Marcus was sitting with his glasses pushed up on his forehead and a pencil in his hand that he was not using.

She took her seat and folded her hands on the table and did not look at it.

The great hall was full. It was always full now. The public benches rose in tiers towards the back, packed with people in outdoor coats: families from the lakeshore who had come in on the early trains, clerks on their break, a row of old men who came to every session and sat in the same places, and a great many people who simply wanted to be in the room when something was said. At the front, below the podium, the press desks were lit by their own screens. Over everything hung the faint hum of a room being broadcast, and the green lights.

Minister Chen Zhao came in from the side door at ten precisely, and the hall stood, and sat again.

He was silver-haired and courteous and seemed pleased to see every single person in the room. Someone had put a glass of tea at his elbow, as someone always did, and he let it steam. He thanked the commissioners, one by one, by name. When he came to the casualty report he did not look at his notes.

"We will begin with the dead," he said, "because they are the one fact both shores agree on."

It was said gently. The hall went quiet in the way halls go quiet for a thing that is true. Kira wrote nothing down. There was a pad in front of her and a pen beside it, and she did not touch either of them, because the room was recorded from eleven angles and a woman seen writing during the casualty report would be asked, afterwards, what she had written.

She counted instead.

The clerk who read the report was a young man with a careful voice who had clearly practised the town names.

Period: Wednesday 2 May 2108 to Wednesday 31 October 2108. Source: Commission casualty register, cross-checked against the published figures of the Chronos Administration of Temporal Defence. Cognitive casualties requiring admission, both shores: in excess of four hundred. The figure is provisional. Deaths attributed to broadcast conflict, both shores: thirty-one.

Thirty-one. She had known the number since Monday. It sounded different said out loud, in the hall, under the lights.

Schools closed for one week or more: fourteen. Streets evacuated: sixty-two. Districts affected, Veridican shore, in order of admissions—

He read them. Stillwater. Fenwick Landing. Colby Sands. Eastbrook. The claimed strip in its entirety, parish by parish. Northreach. Kestrel Point. She knew some of them from Marcus's statements two years ago, from the border testimony that had gone into the public archive on the fifteenth of November and out again with the hour. She knew others only as names on the war's maps. She counted them as he read. Twenty-three towns on the Veridican shore. He did not read the Chronos towns, because the Commission's register did not hold them, only their totals, and the totals went into the thirty-one without names.

Nobody in the hall said the word war. The broadcasts called it the conflict. It had begun on the second of May, when the Administration of Temporal Defence in Chronos had started pushing its history of the lakeshore into the claimed strip at full strength, and Veridica had answered within a week. Six months of two nations shouting their pasts at each other across a lake, and between them, in the towns the clerk had read out, the people who had to live in both pasts at once.

Twenty-three towns. Thirty-one dead. Over four hundred in hospital.

She did the arithmetic because it was the only thing she could do in that room with her hands folded. Thirty-one over six months was just over five a month. Just over one a week. One a week since May, on one shore or the other, in a kitchen or a street or at the end of a quay, dead of something that the certificates called cognitive failure during broadcast conflict, and that she had once seen written on her own.

Beside her, Marcus had put the pencil down.

Thomas Wei presented the attribution analysis at half past ten.

He stood at the technical table to the side of the podium, where the screens were, with his tablet in his right hand. He was tall and graying at the temples and entirely at ease, and he spoke without notes, in the quiet, even voice that made a large room feel like an office. He thanked his team. He thanked the Chronos Administration for publishing its own data, which had made the Commission's work possible. He was generous with credit. He always was.

Then he put the Gap on the screen.

Tuesday 13 December 2107. 03:12 to 03:29. Chronos lakeshore province.

The whole country knew it in outline: the seventeen minutes, eleven months ago, when something had happened to the record of the nation on the far shore, and the nation had called it sacrilege and gone to war over it. Very few people in Veridica had seen it laid out, and the hall leaned forward, a thousand people at once, with a sound like cloth.

Wei laid it out clearly. For seventeen minutes, in the early hours of a Tuesday, the life-logs of the whole Chronos lakeshore province, some four million people, held no content. Not missing. Not corrupted. Flat. Every log in the province carried its last recorded state across those seventeen minutes unchanged, as though the moment at 03:12 had simply been held until 03:29 and then let go. No breath, no word, no movement. For the sleepers, which at that hour was most of the province, the logs read asleep, heart rate steady, and nothing else. For the night-shift workers, the drivers, the nurses, the insomniacs, the same: the last state, carried over, seventeen minutes long.

"In a nation that logs every moment of every life," said Wei, "I think we can understand why this was felt as a wound."

He did not say sacrilege. He said wound, with sympathy, and the hall felt it.

He took them through it: the Chronos Judge's determination, in January, at 0.94, that the interval had been removed by "an act of the Veridican state"; the structure of the removal, as Chronos itself had published it; the Commission's review. It was correct in every particular Kira could check, and she could check most of them.

He offered two hypotheses.

"The first is that Chronos made this itself," he said. "Not maliciously, necessarily. A nation that believes its record is perfect has a great deal invested in explaining any imperfection as an attack. We have all seen, this last year, how much a grievance can be worth."

A murmur from the benches. Somebody near the back said yes.

"The second is less comfortable, and I want to be honest about it. The Lost Hour technique is not a secret any longer. This Commission published the full case archive, as it should have, as it was right to. Every seam, every tombstone, every method. It is possible that someone, persons unknown, has read our archive and copied what they found there." He let that sit. "Either way, the evidence does not support the determination that this was an act of the Veridican state."

It was reasonable. It was kind, even: it did not accuse Chronos of lying, only of grief. It left the Commission's own openness as the brave and costly thing it had been. And it was wrong, and Kira knew that it was wrong, and she knew that of the thousand people in that hall and the eleven cameras and whoever was watching behind them, there was exactly one other person who knew it as precisely as she did.

He was standing at the technical table, with his tablet in his right hand.

"I should say," said Wei, and turned slightly, towards the commissioners' table, towards her, "that Commissioner Osman noted in January that the structure of the interval resembles the Lost Hour. She was the first to say so, and she was right. It does." He smiled at her, pleasantly, the way a colleague smiles at a colleague across a room. A beat. "Resemblance isn't authorship."

The hall liked that. She could feel it liking it.

She inclined her head, very slightly, the way you acknowledge a courtesy. She kept her face level. She had had two years of practice.

It's the same hand.

She had said it first on the fifteenth of November last year, in the lee of the colonnade on the east side of the plaza, where two cameras overlapped badly and a third had been pointing at the same patch of pavement since the spring. Nikolai had held out a small tablet with a registry schedule on it. Not ours, he had said. A block of time on a primary core, some weeks ahead, in another nation's maintenance tables, with the owner field blank. He had pulled the structure of the reservation and she had read it, standing there in the cold with the book against her chest, and she had known it the way you know a voice on the stairs.

The same way of laying things out. The same care about leaving nothing behind.

It's happening again, Nikolai had said. Somewhere else.

And four weeks later, on the morning of the thirteenth of December, it had. The news had come in on the receiver in the flat at seven, an Administrator's voice she did not know, very calm, reading out a time. Zero-three-twelve to zero-three-twenty-nine. Marcus had been standing at the window with his coffee. He had put the cup down on the sill. Neither of them had said anything. They had both been in the colonnade.

The seven of them had known what it was before anyone announced it. That was the thing she could not put down. They had known the owner field was blank, and they had known whose habits filled it.

And then, in January, Chronos had proved it for her.

It had been a Thursday night, late, at the kitchen table. Chronos had published its analysis of the Gap that afternoon, in full, as Chronos published everything, to make its case to the world that Veridica had done this. Four hundred pages of structure. It was meant as an accusation. She had read it as a signature.

It had taken her under an hour. She had not wanted it to take so little. The chain around the seventeen minutes had been re-sealed backwards, from the far edge, from 03:29 towards the hole, block by block, the way nobody was taught to do it, the way no tool did it by default. And the tombstones in the index, the empty markers where references into the interval had been cut, were laid down in the order of removal, not the order of writing.

She had seen that seam twice before. Once around her own missing fact, on the sixtieth floor of the Spire, three years ago this month: Security Protocol Update 7.3, entered at twenty-two hundred hours on the eighteenth of November and gone without a trace, except for a quirk in the sealing that she had called a quirk. And once around the Lost Hour, in Nikolai's feed, in a cellar under a feed merchant's shop. The same backwards stitch. The same knot, tied the same way, by the same pair of hands.

She had written a note to the Commission that night. It was short and careful and technical, because anything longer would have been an accusation she could not prove. It said that the structure of the interval resembled the Lost Hour. It gave three points of resemblance. It did not give a name. She had filed it the next morning, and it had gone into the Commission's record, and eleven months later the man whose hands had made the seam had just thanked her for it in front of a thousand people.

None of the seven had said the name. They had agreed not to, all of them, in a farmhouse kitchen in the border zone in January of last year, when the question had been whether to expose him for the Lost Hour. The reasons had been good. They were still good. The proof was thin: her reading of a seam, an author cache, Maya's memory of a time. And the moment anyone stood up and said that the Lost Hour had been seeded, the Doctrine they had won, the one that let testimony stand where the record was absent, would fall with it. Every revived subject would be dead again on paper. Reeves would go free. Pell would have his argument. So they had chosen journals instead of a name. They had chosen many hands, writing slowly, instead of one voice saying one thing that could be taken away.

It had been the right choice. She had believed it in the farmhouse and she had believed it in January, with the Chronos analysis open in front of her. She had believed it in May.

Thirty-one.

She had never done the sum. She had refused to, all year, the way you refuse to look at a wound until it has been cleaned. She began it now, sitting at the commissioners' table with her hands folded, while Wei took a question from the press desks about the Chronos figures. If she had said the name in January. If she had said it, and been believed, and the Chronos Judge had been shown a seam with a Veridican hand in it that was not the Veridican state's, only one man's. If the determination had been reconsidered. If corrective broadcasting had never begun on the second of May. Then thirty-one minus—

She did not finish it. There was no number to subtract. There was no version of the year she could check.

Under the table her left hand had closed on her knee. And under her breath, without her permission, the old words had started.

Layer one: key exchange. Asymmetric, lattice-based. Rotation interval ninety seconds.

She had not recited in public for more than a year. It had faded, slowly, the way Tanaka had said it would, from every day to every week to almost never. It came back under load. It always had. Layer two: hash chaining. Block size sixty-four kilobytes—

She stopped it. She pressed her tongue to the back of her teeth and held her breath and counted the cameras again, one to eleven, from the east wall round, until the words went back down into her and stayed there. Nobody had seen. Marcus, beside her, had not moved, but his hand on the table had shifted a centimetre towards hers and stopped. He had seen.

At the technical table Wei thanked the press desk for an excellent question.

The session rose at twelve. Chen Zhao thanked everyone again, and said that the Commission's thoughts were with the lakeshore, and the hall stood while he went out through the side door with his tea. Then the benches began to empty in a slow roar of coats and voices, and the commissioners went out by the east door, into the corridor behind the great hall.

It was long and quiet and ran the length of the building, with tall windows on one side looking over the plaza and the doors of committee rooms on the other. The November light came in white and low. Kira walked down it at her usual pace and counted the windows, because it was there to be counted.

Helena Kovač was standing in the third window bay, with her hands in her coat pockets.

Her hair had gone further to gray since Ashford, and she wore the same rimless glasses and the same dark coat. She had been the Commission's Registrar of Testimony since the middle of last year, some months after the Ministry had dismissed her from Ashford without charge, and the Commission had found her a desk. In the spring the Commission had moved out of the Ministry's seventh floor into a building of its own across the plaza, and Kovač had been the first to unpack. She ran the public testimony intake on the ground floor of the Commission's building, the hall with the counter and the writing desks and the deposit slot in the wall, which opened at nine every morning and where any citizen could leave anything they remembered. She signed for every release herself, on paper.

"Ms. Osman."

"Director." It was not her title any longer. Kira used it anyway. Kovač did not correct her.

Kovač looked out at the plaza for a moment, and pushed her glasses up her nose with one finger, and Kira waited, because she had learned that this was what Kovač did before she decided something.

"Has he written to you?" said Kovač.

She did not say who. She did not need to. There was only one person they had ever had in common who might write to either of them, and he was somewhere in the claimed strip, in the parishes the clerk had read out one by one, under the storms.

"No," said Kira.

Kovač nodded, as if that were the answer she had expected and had needed to hear anyway. "Nor to me," she said.

They stood side by side in the window bay and looked at the plaza. Below them the crowd from the public benches was spilling out onto the steps and breaking up into the gray afternoon. Neither of them said that the storms were worst in the strip. Neither of them said that the strip was where outlaws lived, because nobody else would, and that an outlaw had no hospital, and that the Commission's register counted the dead who were brought to hospitals and the dead whose families reported them, and not the others.

"If he does," said Kovač.

"I'll tell you."

Kovač inclined her head. She did not thank her. It would have been out of character, and they both knew it.

Across the corridor, by the door of the second committee room, a young man in the dark blue of the Cabinet Office was waiting with a card in his hand.

Kira noted him because he was out of place. Cabinet Office messengers did not come to Commission sittings; the Cabinet Office had its own screens. He was standing very straight, looking along the corridor for somebody, and when Marcus came out of the east door with the other commissioners, talking to one of them about the Chronos figures, the young man stepped forward and said something quietly, and held out the card.

Marcus took it.

Kira watched him read it. He read it the way he read everything, all the way through, twice. His face did nothing. Then he folded it once along its length and put it in the inside pocket of his jacket, on the left, where he kept the current sheet of his lists, and said something to the messenger, who nodded and went away down the corridor towards the stairs.

She was twelve metres away. She could not read the card. She did not need to. She had seen the crest, the plain white card, the Cabinet Office blue, and she had seen where he had put it, next to the lists, which was where he put things he did not yet know which column to write in.

Thursday 1 November. 12:09. East corridor. Cabinet Office messenger. Card to Chen, M. Contents: unknown. Stowed: inside left pocket.

Marcus looked up and found her across the corridor, and held her eyes for a second, and she understood from his face that he would tell her tonight, and that it was not good, and that he did not yet know how bad.

Then Thomas Wei came out of the east door behind him.

He had his tablet in his right hand and his coat over his arm, and he was walking quickly, lightly, the way he always walked, a man with a great deal to do and no particular hurry about any of it. He passed between them, between Marcus and the window bay where Kira stood with Kovač, and as he passed Marcus he turned his head and smiled.

"Good luck," he said pleasantly.

He did not break stride. He went on down the corridor and round the corner at the end, towards the stairs, and was gone.

Marcus stood where he was, with one hand still at his jacket, over the pocket.

Kira did not move. Beside her, Kovač had gone very still, and had taken her hands out of her coat pockets, and put them back.

12:10, Kira thought, and wrote it down in the only place she could, where nobody else could read it. East corridor. Wei, T., passing. To Chen, M.: "Good luck."

The card had been in Marcus's hand for less than a minute. It had been in his pocket for less than one. He had told nobody what was on it.

Wei had known.
