Chapter 29: The Missions

September 14–17, 2106. The Safe House, the Border Zone

Elena laid it out on the scarred table on a Tuesday evening, with the lake going gray behind her and the nine of them standing round, and Marcus thought, watching her, that she was the best briefing officer he had ever seen, and that he had once thought she was a freelance data analyst who was terrible with directions.

"Here's where we are," she said.

She had a tablet, and a paper map, and a notebook in which she wrote nothing down, because she remembered everything. She spoke fast and flat and without wasting a word.

"Isaiah's piece has gone round the world. Synthesis ran it in full. Chronos is using it as propaganda, which we can't help. Memoria and Axiom have both called for international inspection of correctional facilities, which Veridica has refused. Inside Veridica, the Ministry says it's a forgery and the Spire has put out a statement that the factorepo cannot be altered, which is the first time in history the Spire has ever felt the need to say so." A small, dry pause. "People noticed."

"And Continuity?" said Kira.

"Suspended," said Elena. "Officially. The demonstration's cancelled. Reeves has vanished. Nobody will say where. There's an inquiry at Ashford, and Helena Kovač goes in front of it every day and tells them exactly what the Registry says, and they can't touch her." She looked round at them. "But suspended isn't dead. Phase Two isn't dead. It's delayed. Someone approved it at Ministry level before any of this, and that someone is still there, and still wants it. Kira's layer is still built. The network is still there. They'll try again. Quieter."

Nobody said anything.

"And you're dead," said Elena. "That's the other thing. Every one of you. It's in the Registry, certified, and in Veridica the Registry is truth." She tapped the table. "That's the best weapon we've got. Dead people aren't tracked. Dead people don't have telemetry. Dead people can walk through a checkpoint in the interior with a forged identity and nobody will ever match their face to anything, because the face belongs to a corpse."

She looked round the table.

"But a group of nine dead people," she said, "living together in a factory on the lakeshore, is a pattern. And patterns get found. Someone will notice that the resistance has suddenly got a neural correction specialist, and a Continuity engineer, and three men with the same face. Sooner or later someone will do the arithmetic." She put her hands flat on the map. "We have to split up."

They had known. All of them. They had known since the dawn they arrived, since the head count in the long room, nine. You do not stay together after a thing like that. You scatter, or you are caught together.

Knowing did not make it easier.

It took them three evenings to decide who went where. There was no argument, not really. Everyone knew where they were needed. It was only a matter of saying it aloud.

Tanaka said hers first.

"Chronos," she said. She was sitting very straight at the end of the table, the scar at her temple a thin pale line now. "They're using Isaiah's article to say Veridica is barbaric. That's convenient for them. It means they'll want to be seen to have nothing like it." She folded her hands. "But they use the same interface technology. The cradles in Ashford were Chronos-built. Morrison's calibration went into a Chronos architecture." She glanced at him. "If there's a Chronos Continuity waiting to be born, I want to be in the room when someone proposes it. And I want to be there to help the people who want to leave."

"I'll go with her," said Morrison. He had not spoken all evening. "I can't stay in Veridica. I built half of it." He looked at his hands, as he always did. "Somebody needs to be in Chronos who knows exactly what it looks like when it starts."

Tanaka looked at him. "Yes," she said. "I think so."

Alexei and Dmitri said theirs together, without looking at each other, as if they had rehearsed it.

"The resistance," said Alexei. "With Aunt Lena. There are other facilities. Not like Ashford, but not good. People in them who could get out, if someone went in." He grinned, briefly. "I'm very good at getting people out."

"And I am very good at planning how," said Dmitri.

"He is," said Alexei. "It's unbearable."

Marcus watched them. The Phantom and the enforcer. Two years ago one had been hunting the other through the fog without knowing it was his brother. Now they sat side by side at a table in a factory on the lakeshore and finished each other's sentences.

Old Songs, on his crate by the window, said, without turning round: "I'll go with the boys."

They all looked at him.

"The songs are only useful where the people are," said the old man. "The resistance has people. They'll need someone to sing for them." He was quiet for a moment. "They'll need someone to remember the ones who don't come back."

Nobody argued.

Nikolai said his last, and it was the one that made the room go quiet.

"Back into Veridica," he said.

Dmitri turned his head sharply. Alexei put his hand flat on the table.

"Kolya—"

"No. Listen." Nikolai was looking at his own hands, the way Morrison did. "Aunt Lena needs someone inside. Someone who knows the Registry, and the factorepo systems, and the dead space. Someone who can read the audit tables and find out who can delete a fact." He glanced at Kira. "Someone who can look for whoever approved Phase Two. That has to be done from inside. It can't be done from here."

"You'll be alone," said Dmitri.

"I'll have a new name." Nikolai almost smiled. "Built on top of a dead man's. Elena's people have one ready: a mid-level systems contractor. Very boring. Very dull. The kind of man nobody looks at twice." He looked up. "I've spent my whole life standing in the middle, Dima. Between you and Lyosha. Between the system and the people it was hurting. Holding things together that didn't want to be held." He shrugged, very slightly. "At least this time I'm choosing which middle."

"It's the most dangerous one," said Elena quietly.

"I know," said Nikolai. "That's why it has to be someone who's already dead."

He had kept a copy of his partition. Not much. Fragments he had pushed down into the dead space from the data-centre console on the night of the storm: the subject list, the schedule, the C-1 index line. And something else, which he did not mention and which Marcus would only learn about later: a backup of the session logs from the fourteen months at Ashford, compressed and scattered through blocks that nobody would ever read. Just in case, he said, when Marcus asked him about it, much later. I've learned to keep a copy of everything.

And Marcus and Kira.

"The border zone," said Kira. "Here. Near here."

Elena looked at her.

"The evidence is here," Kira said. "The storms are here. The people who've lived through twenty years of them are here, and they remember things the factorepo says never happened. That's what Isaiah's article was, in the end. A record kept by someone who was there." She glanced at Marcus. "Somebody should be collecting those. Writing them down. Verifying them. Putting them somewhere safe." A pause. "We're a historian and an architect. That's what we're for."

"Reform from inside," said Elena. "Starting from outside."

"Something like that," said Marcus.

He said it more steadily than he felt it.

That night, lying awake on the mattress with Kira asleep beside him, he tried to feel what he had felt a year ago, walking home from the fourteenth floor through the ordered, beautiful, monitored streets of the capital. Each entry was a small victory against chaos, a brick in the wall of certainty. He had believed that. He had believed it with his whole body.

He could not find the feeling. It was on his list. Item: what belief felt like. Status: lost.

What he found instead, when he reached for his reasons, was something much less comfortable.

I have to believe this can be fixed, he thought. Not because he could see how. He couldn't. Not because anyone had told him. Because if he did not believe it, then correction had won. They had reached into him and taken out every piece of hope he had ever had, and emptied him, and left him on a bed with his head in his hands. If he let that be permanent, if he walked out of Ashford and lay down in a factory and decided that nothing could be mended, then Reeves had been right about him. He was a baseline. He was finished.

He could not let it be permanent.

"I have to believe it can be fixed," he said to Kira in the morning, over Elena's terrible tea. "Veridica. The system. All of it. I don't know if I actually believe it. I don't know how to tell anymore. But I have to act as if I do. Because if I don't, then correction won. They erased the part of me that had hope. I can't let that be permanent."

Kira looked at him across the table.

"Then I'll hope for both of us," she said. "For a while. And I'll remember the parts you can't."

He had begun keeping two lists.

Tanaka had suggested it. After the field. You'll have to check your memories against other people's. So he had taken a second sheet from Elena's notebook and drawn a line down the middle of it, and at the top of one column he had written CHECKED, and at the top of the other UNCHECKED.

Every memory from Ashford that he could still reach, he wrote down. And then, one by one, over three weeks, he took them to the others and asked.

Garden, February. The gardener said: "You are doing well. Careful." He asked Dmitri. Dmitri said yes, those were the words, and the date was the twenty-sixth, and it was fourteen thirty-three. CHECKED.

Data centre, August 2. Reeves said: "I already have." He asked Kira. She was quiet for a moment, and then said yes. CHECKED.

Wing C, August 1. The field. He did not need to ask. He wrote it in its own column, a third one, narrower, that he had added at the edge of the page. HOPES.

It was humbling. He had expected it to be humiliating: the historian, the man who had lived his whole life by the record, having to go round a factory asking people whether his own life had happened. It wasn't. It was something else.

"This is what the factorepo should have been," he said to Isaiah, one evening, before he remembered that Isaiah was not there. He said it to the empty place at the table, and then to Kira. "Not one record. Not one system deciding what's true and nobody allowed to question it. Many witnesses. Everyone who was there. Checking each other." He looked at his two lists. "If you want to know what happened, you don't ask a machine. You ask everyone who saw it. And you write down where they disagree."

Kira was looking at him with an odd expression.

"Say that again," she said. "Slowly. I want to remember it."

He faced the number once more, before they left.

He took out the sheet of paper he had copied it onto, in the first week, from Nikolai's partition, and looked at it in the gray morning light by the window.

0.58 → 0.73 (adjusted; threshold compliance).

For three weeks it had been the thing he could not look at. The proof that the system he had given his whole life to had been falsified at the precise point where it decided his fate. He had thought, when he first read it in April, that it was the end of everything he had believed.

He looked at it now, and understood that it was the opposite.

If the system were truly infallible, if the factorepo could not be altered and The Judge could not be wrong, then there would be nothing anyone could do. A perfect system cannot be reformed. It can only be obeyed or destroyed. Everything Marcus had believed about Veridica, a year ago, walking home through the lit streets, had been a belief in a thing that could never be fixed, because it could never be broken.

But it had been broken. Someone had typed a seventy-three.

Which meant that it had been built by people, and was run by people, and was corrupted by people. And anything corrupted by people can be corrected by people.

Correction by people, he thought. Not of people.

It was not the faith he had had. He did not know yet what to call it. But it was something. It was a floor under the floor. And it was his. Nobody had put it there.

On the last night they sat together in the long room.

Elena's people had made a real meal: stew, bread, a bottle of something from Chronos that Alexei said was terrible and drank most of. The stove was lit. The broken windows let in the smell of the lake. Old Songs sang, and nobody talked for a while, and then everybody talked at once.

Somebody had put an extra chair at the table. Marcus did not see who. It was simply there when they sat down, at the end, between Tanaka and Alexei: a battered wooden chair with nobody in it and a cup of tea in front of it that nobody drank.

Nobody mentioned it. Nobody moved it.

Late in the evening, when the others were clearing up, Elena came and sat down beside him on the edge of his mattress.

It was the first time they had been alone together since a couch in a quiet apartment in the capital, eleven months ago. He did not know what to say. From her face, neither did she.

"You were the eighth," she said at last. "I told you once that I'd recruited seven people, and I didn't know if I'd saved them or destroyed them." She looked at her hands. "You were the eighth. I got you out. I still don't know which one I did."

Marcus thought about it.

"Both," he said. "I think it's allowed."

She looked at him sideways, startled. Then something moved in her face, small and rueful, that on anyone else he would have called a smile.

She got up and went back to the stove. There was a great deal more to say. It would have to wait. Some of it, he thought, would have to wait a very long time.

The brothers said goodbye to each other last, in the corner, very late.

Marcus did not mean to watch. He was lying on his mattress with his eyes half closed, and they were talking in low voices, and he heard them anyway.

"Kolya," said Alexei. "Listen. One more time."

And the two of them told it to him. Aloud. Together. The roof. The fog off the lake. The tar still warm. Me on the left, Lyosha in the middle, you on the right, because you were always on the right. They took turns, the way they must have taken turns as boys, each one picking up where the other left off, finishing each other's sentences. I was. Mama said I came out first. By thirty seconds. That doesn't count.

Nikolai listened with his eyes closed.

When they had finished, Alexei said, very softly, the way Elena had said it on the road:

"Who was born first?"

A pause.

"Dima," said Nikolai. His voice was steady. "By thirty seconds." A breath. "It doesn't count."

Marcus heard Alexei make a sound that might have been a laugh or might have been something else.

It had not come back. Marcus knew that, and so did the brothers. Nikolai had not remembered it. He had been told. He was answering out of a story, not a memory, a story given to him in a schoolbook and on a road and in a factory at midnight by the only two people alive who had been there. A memory rebuilt from testimony.

It was enough. Marcus could hear, in the corner, that it was enough.

"Promise," said Nikolai.

"Promise," said Dmitri and Alexei, together.

And for the first time since a checkpoint in the fog, twenty-two months ago, the three Volkov brothers chose to be apart. Not a cell. Not a triangle. Not glass. A decision, made with their eyes open, to go three ways into the world and trust that what held them did not need them to be able to see each other.

Marcus lay in the dark and thought that it probably didn't. It had held through a white chair. It would hold through a border.

They left at dawn.

The fog was on the lake, thick and gray, the way it had been on the morning they arrived. Elena's people had brought four vehicles into the yard, and they stood in the wet air beside them and said the things people say, which are never the right things.

Tanaka embraced Kira, and then, after a moment's hesitation, Marcus. "Check your memories," she said into his shoulder. "Every one. Promise me."

"I promise."

Morrison shook his hand. His grip was steady. Marcus noticed that, and noticed that Morrison noticed him noticing, and neither of them said anything.

Alexei hugged everyone, twice. Dmitri shook hands with a formal, precise grip and looked each of them in the eye for exactly as long as it took to be certain he had seen them. Old Songs did not say goodbye at all. He only hummed, as he got into the car: bright, rising, the phrase that meant yes.

Nikolai stood last by his vehicle, in a new coat that did not fit him, with a new name in his pocket. He looked at his brothers across the yard for a long moment. Then he got in, and the door closed.

Elena went with Alexei and Dmitri. She stopped at the door of the car and looked back at Marcus, once, and nodded, and got in.

The engines started. One by one the vehicles pulled out of the yard and turned onto the lakeshore road, some left, some right, and went away into the fog. Their lights grew faint and gray and were gone.

Marcus and Kira stood side by side in the cracked concrete yard of a factory that Veridica said had been abandoned in 2065 and Chronos said was a training facility, and which was, in fact, neither, and watched until there was no one left to watch.

Then Kira took his hand, and they went inside.
