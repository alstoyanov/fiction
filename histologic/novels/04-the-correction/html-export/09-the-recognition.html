Chapter 9: The Recognition

December 18, 2105. Wing C

Morning.

The skylight was gray. A tray came through the slot. He ate what was on it, because it was there. He put the tray back in the slot. The slot closed.

He sat on the bed.

Orderlies. The young one with the shaved head, and the older one with the braid. The door opened.

"Good morning, Mr. Chen. Session day."

He stood up, because that was what happened on session days. The woman's hand touched his elbow. He walked.

The atrium. Glass on both sides. Faces in the glass. He did not look at them. He looked at the floor, a pale smooth composite with a faint grain in it, and at his own feet in the gray soft-soled shoes, left, right, left.

The door at the end of the atrium. The corridor. White.

At the junction where the treatment corridor met the short passage from the east rooms, there was a place where two sets of orderlies sometimes had to stop and let each other pass. He did not know this as a fact. He knew it the way the body knows a step in a staircase, from having walked it thirty times.

They slowed. He stopped, because the hand on his elbow stopped.

Another set of orderlies was coming the other way. Between them was a woman.

He did not look at her. He was looking at the floor. But the floor was in front of her, too, so her feet were in the edge of what he saw: gray shoes like his, walking and then stopping. And for some reason, which he would not be able to explain afterwards, not even to himself, his eyes moved up.

Gray trousers. Gray top. Hands at her sides, clenched. Dark hair pulled back into a knot without pins, coming loose at one temple. A face.

She was looking at him.

Their eyes met.

It was not a memory.

He would try, later, when he could try things again, to describe it to himself precisely, because precision was the only thing he had ever been good at. It was not a memory, because nothing came with it. No place, no name, no time. The factorepo in his head, the one he had spent his whole life building, offered no entry.

It was a recognition. Without content. The way you know a word is on the tip of your tongue before you know what the word is. The way, reaching for the next stair in the dark, your foot already knows how high it will be.

I know you.

He did not know from where. He did not know why. He did not know, in that first second, even what knowing was, because it had been weeks since he had known anything that was not simply a fact, and this was not a fact. It was something else. It was warm.

It was like reaching for a stair that wasn't there, and finding, impossibly, that it was.

Her eyes were hazel. They were very steady. And they were looking at him, not at his gray clothes or his crooked glasses or the space where a person used to be, but at him, as if there were still someone there to be looked at.

Then the orderly with the braid said something, and the hand on his elbow moved, and he walked on, and she walked on, and she was behind him.

He looked at the floor. Left, right, left.

He did not look back. He did not think to.

But he was holding something. He did not know what it was. He was holding it the way you hold a small warm stone in your pocket without looking at it.

C-2. The white chair. The hood.

"Good morning, Mr. Chen," said the voice behind the mirror. It sounded almost bored. It sounded like a man going through his week. "Maintenance. This won't take long."

The hood came down. The hum. The interface under his skull said yes, the way it always did.

He felt it begin. The lifting. The turning. It went where it always went, to the places where things used to be: the café, the river, his mother's canvas bag, the folded flag. It found what it always found: a table and a cup and a timestamp; a bag and a citizen number; a flag. It turned each one over, quickly, the way a guard checks a door he already knows is locked, and put it down.

Dr. Moss's hand was warm in his.

And he was holding the stone.

He did not know he was doing it. He could not have done it on purpose, because he did not have purposes anymore. But the corridor was there, in him, the junction, the woman's eyes, I know you. And it did not feel like the café, or the river, or his mother. It did not feel like a thing he loved. He did not know yet what it felt like. It sat in him quietly, like a new entry in a ledger that he had not got round to categorising. December eighteenth. Treatment corridor junction. Approximately oh-nine-ten hours. Subject observed a woman.

A fact. Just a fact.

The machine went past it. Twice. Three times. It did not stop. It was looking for heat, and whatever this was, it was too new and too small and too uncertain of itself to be hot yet.

"Stable," said the voice, satisfied. "Lovely. Same time next week."

He was not sick in the corridor. He had not been sick after a session for weeks. There was nothing left in him to object.

The orderlies walked him back along the atrium. The honeycomb. The faces in the glass. He looked at the floor.

His door slid open. He went in. It slid shut.

And instead of sitting on the bed, which was what his body did, he stood in the middle of the cell. For no reason. He did not know why his body was not sitting down. He stood there with his hands hanging at his sides, a thin man in gray, and his eyes, without being asked, went up from the floor, and across the atrium, to the cell directly opposite.

7-K.

It was empty.

He looked at it for a long time. It was not a decision. He simply looked. The bed with square corners. The steel sink. A book, thick, with a faded green cover, lying on the end of the bed. He looked at the book. He looked at the square corners. There was something about square corners.

He was still standing there, forty minutes later, when they brought her back.

She was pale. That was the first thing. Paler than in the corridor. She walked between the orderlies with her shoulders very straight, as if she were holding them there by force, and when the door of 7-K slid open she went in so fast that the orderly with the shaved head had to take his hand off her arm.

The door sealed. She went straight to the sink and turned on the tap and put both hands under it and brought the water up to her face, again and again, and then bent over the sink with her hands braced on either side of it, the way he had once bent over his own, a long time ago, in October.

Her shoulders were shaking.

He watched her. He did not know he was watching her. He knew it only the way you know you are cold.

Then she straightened. She dried her face on the sleeve of her gray top. She turned around.

And she was not frightened. That was what he saw, and what went into him, through the glass, across the atrium, like light going into a dark room. She was furious. Her mouth was set. Her eyes were bright. She stood in the middle of her cell with her fists closed at her sides and she glared at the door to the treatment corridor as if she would like to take it off its hinges with her bare hands.

She had been in the white chair. He knew what the white chair was. And she had come out of it angry.

He did not know that you could do that.

Then she turned her head, and looked across the atrium, and saw him.

He was standing in the middle of his cell. He had not sat down. He was looking at her.

She looked back.

She did not look away. She did not smile, or wave, or mouth words through the glass, the way Samuel used to. She simply looked at him, steadily, for a long time, with her hazel eyes still bright with anger. As if she were checking. As if she were confirming something.

I know you, he thought again.

And this time something came with it.

It was not her. It was not Elena, or his parents, or the café.

It was himself.

A small white room with a table and two chairs and a camera in the corner. An interview room. The Enforcement Center. Kade across the table, not unkind, saying seventy-three percent. And a man on the other side of the table, a thin man with glasses, half out of his chair, his face hot, his voice too loud in the small room.

But I'm loyal! I reported her! I did everything right!

He remembered it.

Not as a filed thing, not as an entry with a timestamp. He remembered being that man. The heat in his face. The desperate, furious certainty. The feeling of having believed in something so much that you would stand up in a room full of cameras and shout it.

He had shouted. He had fought. Not very well, and not for very long, and not for anything that had turned out to be worth fighting for. But he had. Once, only ten weeks ago, there had been a Marcus Chen who believed in something enough to shout.

He stood in the middle of his cell and felt that man, very faintly, like warmth coming back into a numb hand. It hurt. It was not the dull, papery nothing of the sessions. It was a sharp, specific ache, and it was his.

The prose of his mind, which had been reduced for weeks to single words and broken entries and silence, began, just slightly, to run on.

He thought: She came back angry.

He thought: I used to be angry.

He thought: I don't know who she is, and I know her, and she looked at me as if I were still here.

He thought: Am I still here?

And then another thought arrived, underneath the others, cold and clear and very old. A thought that came from the historian, the Junior Historian at the Regional Factorepo, the man who had spent three years making sure that every fact was recorded and nothing escaped the record.

The cameras.

He did not look up at the dome in the corner of his ceiling. He did not need to. He knew where it was. He knew the small green light that did not blink. He had lived under it for ten weeks. It had watched him stop moving. It had watched him turn his face to the wall. It had watched him become stable, lovely, a beautiful baseline.

And it was watching him now, standing in the middle of his cell, looking at a woman across the atrium with something in his face that had not been there yesterday.

If they see this, the historian thought, they will take it.

It was not a fear he felt so much as a fact he knew. They would see it. They saw everything. And next week, in the white chair, the voice behind the mirror would not sound bored. It would sound interested. And the hand would go looking, and this time the small warm stone would be hot enough to find.

Across the atrium, in 7-D, the small woman with the silver-streaked hair had risen from her bed and was standing at her glass. Very still. Her hands were at her sides. She was looking at him with an expression he could not read.

In 7-A, 7-B and 7-C, three identical men sat on three beds in three identical postures, faces to their three white walls. They did not move. They seemed, if anything, to have become stiller. Stiller than stone.

He did not see any of that. He was looking at 7-K.

For the first time in weeks, Marcus Chen lifted his head.

He had not known it was down. He had been walking with it down, and sitting with it down, and sleeping with it down, for so long that it had become the shape of him. Now he lifted it. His chin came up. His shoulders, very slightly, came back. He stood in the middle of his cell, in the aquarium light of Wing C, with his head up, and looked across the atrium at the woman who had come out of the white chair angry.

She was still looking at him.

He held it for three seconds. Four.

Then, understanding without knowing how he understood, the way the body knows a stair in the dark, he let it go down again.

Slowly. The way a broken man's head goes down. He let his shoulders round. He turned, slowly, and sat on the edge of the bed, and put his elbows on his knees, and bent forward, and put his head in his hands. The glasses pushed up on his forehead. His fingers over his face.

To the camera, to the green light that did not blink, it was the same as every day. The same shape. The same stillness. The beautiful baseline.

But behind his hands, for the first time since October, his eyes were open.

And through the gap between two fingers, he could see 7-K.
