Chapter 17: The Ally

May 2106. Ashford Correctional Facility

We need someone on the staff.

It had been Isaiah who put it into words first, in a fragment at the start of May, but it had been in all of them for weeks. Dr. Yuki Tanaka had read it on her bed with a primer on her knees and had known, before she reached the end of the line, that it was going to be her.

IO-38. WE HAVE A CALENDAR AND A LIST AND A NETWORK. WE DO NOT HAVE A DOOR. NONE OF US CAN OPEN ONE. WE NEED SOMEONE ON THE STAFF.

She was the only one of the seven who had ever been staff. She had worked in this building for eleven years. She knew which of them brought biscuits on Fridays and which of them had daughters at school and which of them, in the canteen, lowered their voices when they talked about the east wing. She knew their faces. She knew, better than anyone else in Wing C, what their faces meant.

TY-12, she pricked back, that night, under a table of metric conversions. I WILL TRY. GIVE ME A MONTH. DO NOT EXPECT MUCH.

She tried Reyna first.

She had known she would, and she had known it was a mistake, and she had done it anyway, because she had shared an office with Reyna Moss for six years and could not believe, not entirely, not in the part of her that still brought her colleague's birthday to mind every March, that there was nothing there to reach.

It was the quarterly medical. Every Wing C patient had one: an hour in a small, ordinary examination room off the treatment corridor, with a real window and a real desk and a paper sheet on the couch, where someone checked blood pressure and weight and reflexes and asked about sleep. It was the only hour in the quarter when a patient was alone in a room with a doctor and no hood.

This quarter, the doctor was Reyna.

She came in smiling, the way she always came in, with her tablet under her arm and her short gray hair neat and her face soft with concern. "Yuki," she said. Not C-6. Not Dr. Tanaka. Yuki. And she took Yuki's hand in both of hers, the way she had taken ten thousand hands in the white chair, and pressed it. "How are you sleeping?"

"Badly," Yuki said. It was true.

"I'm sorry." Reyna meant it. "It gets better. It really does."

She took Yuki's blood pressure. She listened to her chest. She tapped her knees with the little hammer, and Yuki's legs jumped, and Reyna smiled as if it were good news. She made notes. She asked about appetite and headaches and whether Yuki was using the library privileges she had been offered in April, and Yuki said she was, which was true, and did not say why.

And when the tablet was down and Reyna was sitting on the stool beside the couch, as she had sat beside a thousand patients, Yuki spoke to her as one doctor to another.

"Reyna. You've seen what it does."

Reyna's smile didn't move.

"You've seen it," Yuki said. "You've sat beside it. You held Marcus Chen's hand every session from October to December. You watched him go." Her voice was very steady. She had rehearsed this for a week, in her head, the way she used to rehearse delivering bad news to families. "You watched the Samuel boy go. You saw their faces afterwards. You know what flattened looks like, Reyna. You worked the long-term wards for two years before you came to the east wing. You know that isn't healing."

Reyna was quiet for a moment. Her face was troubled. Not closed. Troubled, and sad, and full of feeling.

"It's hard, Yuki," she said softly. "I know it's hard. Healing is hard. I remember what my mother was like after her surgery. Weeks of it. She didn't recognise me for three days. We thought we'd lost her." She reached for Yuki's hand again. "But she came through. They come through. Marcus is so much better now. So calm. You should see his baselines."

"Reyna—"

"I know you've been frightened," Reyna said, and her eyes were wet. "I know how it looks, from inside. I was so sorry when they brought you in. You were always the best of us. But you're ill, Yuki. That's all this is. You got too close to it. You started seeing sickness where there's only recovery." She squeezed Yuki's fingers. "I pray for you. Every night. I pray you'll see it again, the way you used to."

Yuki looked at her.

She had been braced for Reyna to report her. For a flicker of fear, of suspicion, of the machinery of Ashford moving behind those soft eyes. She would have understood that. She had prepared for it.

She had not prepared for this. For Reyna to look at her with nothing but love, and pity, and tears, and a faith so perfect, so seamless, that there was no crack anywhere for a single doubt to get in.

Reyna did not report her. That night, and in the days after, Yuki waited for the orderlies, for a change in her session schedule, for the voice behind the mirror to say interesting. Nothing came. Reyna had gone back to her office and written, Yuki was sure, patient distressed; some residual anxiety; recommend continued support, and meant every word.

Because to Reyna she was not dangerous. She was sick. And you do not report the sick. You pray for them.

TY-15, Yuki pricked that night, and her hands were not quite steady. MOSS: NO. NOT HOSTILE. NOT REACHABLE. SHE BELIEVES THAT WE ARE ILL AND SHE LOVES US FOR IT. I THINK THE GENTLEST BELIEVERS ARE THE HARDEST WALLS. THERE IS NOTHING TO PUSH AGAINST.

She had known about Morrison since August.

She had not let herself think about it too directly, the way she had not let herself think about the triplets too directly for three months. You do not look at a thing too hard in a building where the walls have eyes.

But she had seen his hands. In her very first session, too frightened to notice much, she had noticed that. A small tremor in the fingers of his right hand above the console at the moment the probe engaged. It had been there every session since. It had got worse. In November, in the weeks when Marcus Chen was going under, she had watched Morrison sit at the console with his jaw so tight she could see the muscle jumping under the skin.

She knew that tremor. She had had it herself, at her kitchen table, reading the words to baseline.

The problem was never whether. It was when. Morrison was never alone with her. Reyna was always there, on her stool, holding hands. And the voice was always there, behind the mirror, listening.

Almost always.

It came in the third week of May, in C-3.

Twenty minutes into the session. The hood was down and the hum was steady and the probe was working its way through her husband again, the house in the hills, the cello in the corner, the hospital. She was reciting. Step one: confirm patient identity. Step two: confirm procedure. Step three: confirm site. Step four: confirm consent. The forty-one steps of the surgical checklist, the ones she had said aloud before every procedure for twenty years, with her husband folded into them, one step at a time, as she had folded him in over the last two months. Step nine: confirm allergies. The cello. Step ten: confirm anaesthetic plan. His hands on the bow.

Reyna had been called out. A problem with another patient, an orderly at the door, a quick apologetic squeeze of Yuki's fingers. Back in five minutes.

And then the voice behind the mirror said, "Morrison, I'm going to take a call. Hold her at current depth. Two minutes."

And the small red light above the mirror, the one that she had learned months ago meant monitoring, went dark.

She had perhaps ninety seconds.

She did not open her eyes. She did not move her head under the hood. She kept her voice low and level, exactly as she would have spoken to a colleague across an operating table.

"You don't believe in this."

At the console, the tapping of keys stopped.

"Dr. Morrison. You don't believe in any of it." She kept reciting, silently, underneath. Step fourteen: confirm equipment. "Your hands tell me every time."

"Be quiet." It came out hoarse and fast and frightened. "Be quiet. They record everything. Everything in here is—"

"The red light is off," she said. "I've been watching it for nine months. I know what it means." A pause. "I'm not asking you for anything. I'm telling you what I've seen. Your right hand shakes when the probe engages. It's been shaking since August. It shakes worst on the days when they take the most."

Silence.

"I had it too," she said. "At my kitchen table. The night I read the protocol. I put my cup down because I was afraid I'd drop it."

Nothing from the console. She could hear him breathing. She could hear, very faintly, the small sound of a man's hand pressed flat against a desk to keep it still.

The red light above the mirror came back on.

"Thank you, Morrison," said the voice, pleasant and unhurried. "Where are we?"

"Holding at current depth," Morrison said, and his voice was almost normal. "No change."

He did not report her.

She lay awake that night and the next, and waited, the way she had waited after Reyna. For the orderlies. For a change in her schedule. For a longer session, a deeper one, a voice behind the mirror saying interesting.

Nothing came.

And her next session, four days later, was gentler.

Nobody else would have noticed. Reyna did not. The voice did not. The probe went through her the same way, following the same links, finding the same heat. But she had spent eleven years reading cradles, and she could feel the depth the way a diver feels water pressure, and it was less. Not much. A fraction. A few percent off the gain on the deepest channels, the ones that did the real damage, the ones that dissolved the links rather than merely testing them. Her husband came up under the hood and was turned over and was put down again, and afterwards, lying in her cell, she reached for him and found him whole. Clearer than he had been in weeks.

She lay on her bed with her face to the wall and did not cry, because the camera was watching, and because a corrected woman does not cry.

But she knew.

The corridor came a week after that.

It was the escort change at the end of her session. The orderly who had brought her in handed her over to the orderly who would take her back, at the junction outside C-3, where there was always a moment of confusion, of tablets being checked and signatures being given. And Morrison came out of C-3 behind her with his tablet under his arm and his eyes on the floor, and passed her, close, in the narrow space by the wall, the way people pass each other in corridors.

He did not look at her. His lips barely moved.

"I'm sorry."

She kept her eyes on the floor.

"I built the calibration," he said, very low and very fast. "For the cradles. The new gains. Reeves brought me in last year. He said it was for trauma. For people who couldn't stop reliving things. Veterans from the border. People who'd been through storms. A way to take the edge off the worst memories so they could sleep." His voice cracked, just slightly. "I thought I was helping. And then he put it in the east wing. And I watched what it did to the Chen man. And I don't know how to stop it."

The orderlies were still talking at the junction. A few more seconds.

"You can help us," Yuki said, just as low. "You can be on the right side of this. Not all at once. Not tonight. But you can."

He said nothing. But he did not walk away.

"And you can start," she said, "by telling me what the triple cradle will do to the Volkovs."

She heard him take a breath.

He told her in one sentence. He told it the way people tell things they have been carrying alone for a long time, all at once, as if it had been waiting at the back of their mouth.

"Simultaneous integration," Morrison said, "will either break the bond and let him correct them one by one, or it'll overload all three and kill them. He doesn't know which. He says both outcomes are informative."

Then the orderly touched her elbow, and she walked, and Morrison went the other way along the corridor with his tablet under his arm and his eyes on the floor, and his right hand, as it hung at his side, was shaking.

She coded the report that night, in an arithmetic primer, by the light of her cell, with her back to the camera.

She took a long time over it. She wanted it exact. She had always wanted her reports exact.

TY-19. 29 MAY. FOR ALL SEVEN.

MORRISON IS OURS. OR NEARLY. HE HAS REDUCED MY DEPTH ON THE DEEP CHANNELS WITHOUT BEING ASKED. HE SPOKE TO ME IN THE CORRIDOR. HE BUILT THE CALIBRATION BELIEVING IT WAS FOR TRAUMA PATIENTS. HE IS ASHAMED AND FRIGHTENED AND HE HAS NOT REPORTED ME. DO NOT APPROACH HIM YET. LET HIM COME TO US.

ON THE TRIPLE CRADLE: HE SAYS IT WILL EITHER BREAK THE BOND OR KILL ALL THREE. REEVES DOES NOT KNOW WHICH AND CONSIDERS BOTH ACCEPTABLE. DV AV NV: I AM SORRY. WE HAVE UNTIL AUGUST.

MOSS IS NOT OURS AND NEVER WILL BE. BE KIND TO HER ANYWAY. SHE IS THE KINDEST PERSON IN THIS BUILDING AND SHE WILL BE THE ONE WHO RAISES THE ALARM. SHE WILL DO IT BECAUSE SHE LOVES US.

She stopped there, with the pin in her fingers.

Then, below the message, in the margin where no one else would think to look, she pricked one more line. It was not for the others. It was not for anyone. She had not written anything only for herself since August.

I refused once, and they made me a subject. He is about to refuse. I owe it to him to make it survivable.

She closed the primer and put it on the tray for the morning trolley, and lay down with her face to the wall.

Step forty-one, she recited, silently, in the dark. Confirm the patient is safe to leave.
