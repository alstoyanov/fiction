Chapter 6: The Library

December 5–10, 2105. Ashford Correctional Facility, the Library

The library at Ashford had four thousand, two hundred and eleven books, and Alexei Volkov knew where every one of them was.

He hadn't meant to learn. It had just happened, the way things happen when you spend six months shelving the same books under the same light for the same eight hours a day with your face arranged into the expression of a man who has had his soul politely removed. The mind has to go somewhere. His had gone into the catalogue. He knew that Principles of Factorepo Verification (Third Edition) lived on the second shelf of the fourth stack and had been borrowed eleven times since June, always by the same bald accountant from the east ward, who read it the way other men read scripture. He knew that the anthology of approved children's poetry had a torn page ninety-two and that nobody had ever complained. He knew the history section by heart: nine shelves of it, every one a variation on the same story, which was Veridica's story, which was the only story.

He knew the books nobody touched, too. Those were his favourites.

"Seven-seven-two," said the orderly at the desk, not looking up from her screen. "Returns."

Alexei took the returns cart without a word, the way a corrected man took things, and wheeled it slowly into the stacks.

The library occupied the ground floor of the east building: a long, bright room with tall windows onto the lawns, rows of pale wood shelving, and a few tables where inmates from the general wards were allowed to sit and read for an hour at a time under supervision. The patients liked it. It was quiet and warm and smelled of paper. It was, Alexei thought, the most humane room in the building, and it had very nearly broken his heart when he first saw it, because it reminded him of the reading room at the border-zone community centre where he used to meet people who wanted to cross.

He shelved the returns. Gardening for Wellbeing, back in the craft section. The Founding: A Pictorial History, back in the fourth stack. A novel about a heroic enforcer, back in fiction, where it had been borrowed forty-one times and was falling apart.

He handled every one of them gently.

That was the one thing about his time in correction that he could never have explained to anyone, not even his brothers, who knew everything else. It had started in his first weeks here, in July, when they first let him shelve. He had picked up a book, an ordinary book, and felt, very faintly, as if another hand had just let go of it. As if the warmth of someone else's grip were still in the cover.

It was nonsense, of course. It was a corrected brain misfiring, or a lonely brain inventing company. He knew that.

But he had kept noticing it, and after a while he had started paying attention to who. Which books went out to which patients. Which ones came back with a corner turned down, or a page softened from being read again and again. Which ones people kept longer than the loan period and returned apologetically, and which ones they returned the next morning without having opened them.

The library, he had slowly realised, was a map. Not of the books. Of the people. Of which minds in Ashford were still reaching for something and which had stopped.

He knew, for instance, that the bald accountant in the east ward still believed. That the girl from the south ward who kept borrowing the children's poetry was trying, very hard, to remember something about her own childhood that she couldn't quite reach. That the old woman who took out The Founding: A Pictorial History every week, and returned it every week with the same page flattened, was looking at a photograph of the city where she'd been born.

Nobody in Wing C borrowed books. Nobody in Wing C was allowed to. Books went to the Wing C cells only on request, on the meal trolley, and in six months Alexei had filled exactly three requests: two for Tanaka, the doctor in 7-D, both medical, both refused by the orderly and returned to the shelf; and one, in November, for the tall journalist in 7-E, a dictionary. Which, Alexei thought, was either a very sad request or a very clever one.

The meal trolley came through the library at quarter past eleven, as it did every day, on its way from the kitchens to Wing C.

Mikhail Petrov pushed it. Everyone called him Old Songs, including the orderlies, including, Alexei suspected, some of the doctors. He was sixty-eight years old, thin and slightly stooped, with a long white ponytail and a full white beard and pale blue eyes that always seemed to be looking at something a little way beyond whatever was in front of them. He had been at Ashford longer than anyone could remember. Longer than some of the staff. He was so thoroughly, so harmlessly corrected that they let him wander the service corridors with the trolley unescorted, and nobody ever looked at him twice.

He was humming. He was always humming.

Alexei, shelving, did not look up. Neither did Old Songs. The trolley passed within half a metre of the stack where Alexei stood, and the old man's tune dipped, just for a bar, into a different key, and then climbed back up again.

It meant good morning. It had meant good morning for five months.

Alexei had met him here, in this room, in his second week at Ashford.

He had been barely holding on. Six months at the border facility had worn him thin, and the bond had held, but only just; he had come to Ashford feeling like a rope with half its strands cut. And the calm voice in Ashford's white rooms had spent that first week on his father, day after day, rubbing at the memory of Papa's study, the warning, question things, don't just accept what you're told, and Alexei had felt it thinning under the pressure like paper held against a flame. He had come into the library shaking. He had picked up a book and put it down and picked up another, not seeing any of them.

And there had been an old man in the corner of the library, sorting magazines, humming.

It was a song Alexei knew. That was what had stopped him. Not the words, there were no words, but the tune. It was an old tune, pre-Histologic, the kind of thing grandmothers sang in the border zone when they thought no one was listening. His mother had hummed it. Over the washing up, in the kitchen, with the fog against the windows. He had not thought of it in fifteen years.

It had gone through him like warmth through cold hands.

And a strange thing had happened. Standing there, listening, he had reached for the memory of Papa's study, the one the machine had been rubbing at all week, and found that it was still there. Clearer than it had been in days. As if the song had been sitting on top of it, holding it down.

He had turned and looked at the old man. The old man had not stopped humming. He had not looked up from his magazines. But after a moment, very quietly, without moving his lips much, he had said:

"They look for what you love."

Alexei had not moved.

"So I hide what I love in what I've memorised." The old man put a magazine on a pile. "They don't look there. They think it's furniture."

Then he had gone back to humming, and never said another word to Alexei, then or since. But that was enough. Alexei had gone back to his cell and sat with his face to the wall and, for the first time since his arrest, begun to build something.

He had taken the memory of Papa's study and folded it into the old tune. Not metaphorically. Literally, the way you fold a letter into an envelope. He had sat for hours running the melody in his head, bar by bar, and each bar he tied to a piece of the memory. The desk with the green lamp. The smell of solder. Question things. The worry in Papa's eyes. Until he could not hum the song without the room coming up with it, whole, the way the smell of a kitchen brings back a whole childhood.

Then he had done it with everything else he could not afford to lose. Mama's hands. The roof. His brothers. He had put them into songs and multiplication tables and the order of the stops on the lakeshore tram line, and the machine had come looking for them in the white chair, and found a tram timetable, and passed over it, bored.

And he had taught it to his brothers. Across the glass, with gestures and looks and the thing between them that the technicians could see on their screens and never understand. Dmitri had put his in the rotation schedules of cameras. Nikolai had put his in code.

Hide what you love in what you've memorised.

The bond had kept them alive at the border facility. This was what had kept them whole since. An old man humming over a pile of magazines, and three brothers holding on from outside.

He finished the returns and wheeled the empty cart back to the desk. The orderly didn't look up.

On the way, he passed the window at the end of the room, the one that looked east across the lawns toward the long, low, windowless side of Wing C. He could not see into it from here. Nobody could. But he knew exactly where everyone was. He had spent six months walking into and out of that atrium twice a day with his face empty and his eyes taking in everything.

He knew where 7-H was.

He thought about the man in it.

He had watched him arrive. He had watched his first night, when they took the boy in 7-G and brought him back with nothing in his face, and the man in 7-H had put his hand on the glass. Alexei had seen Dmitri see that. Had felt, through the bond, Dmitri's quick, unwilling respect. That one still has a heart.

And then two months. He had watched every week of it. The man in 7-H coming back from the white room paler and quieter and slower, like a lamp being turned down. Alexei had watched him stop looking at things, and then stop moving, and then stop being anything but a shape on a bed with its head in its hands.

And the three of them, sitting in their triangle, perfectly calm, perfectly corrected, had let it happen.

We could have told him, Alexei thought, and the thought was not new, but it cut as if it were. Something. Anything. One word through the glass. "Hide." "Hold on." We knew exactly what they were doing to him and we knew how to stop it and we sat there and watched, because we had a rule.

He had helped people cross the border for ten years. That was who he was. That was the whole of who he was. He had sat in cold safe houses with strangers who were crying into cups of tea he'd made them, and he had told them the truth, and then he had got them out. He had never once, in ten years, looked at someone who was drowning and decided it was safer to watch.

Correction had not taken that from him. It had been the first thing he folded into the song.

So why, he thought, am I letting caution take it instead?

He could feel his brothers. He always could.

Dmitri, out in the garden, was a steady, cold weight at the edge of Alexei's mind. Watchful. Precise. The same as he had been at twelve, when he appointed himself the oldest and started telling the other two where to stand. Dmitri had made the rule. Dmitri believed in the rule the way he had once believed in Veridica, completely, because it was the only thing standing between them and disaster, and Dmitri had never in his life been able to look at disaster without trying to organise it.

Nikolai, in the technical wing, was fainter and more jittery. Worried. Nikolai was always worried. He had stood between his two brothers for fifteen years, and the worry had worn a groove in him that correction had not been able to fill. Nikolai agreed with the rule, too. But Nikolai agreed with the rule the way he agreed with everything, reluctantly, with his eyes on both sides at once.

And Alexei had agreed. He had agreed for six months.

He had stopped agreeing on the day the woman arrived in 7-K.

He had seen her through the doors of the vestibule, four nights ago, walking in from the transport. He had been on his way back from the library under escort. Dark hair coming out of its pins. A face that was frightened and trying very hard not to be. And then, in the morning, in her new cell, the thing that had made him stop in the middle of the atrium until the orderly touched his arm: she was standing at her glass and counting. He could see her lips move. He could see her eyes going from the camera to the door to the slot to the vent, logging, measuring. She had been in Wing C for less than an hour, and she was already mapping it.

She was going to fight. He could see that as clearly as he could see the camera domes.

And she was going to lose, because she was going to fight the machine the way the man in 7-H had fought it, head on, with everything she had, not knowing where to put the things she loved. The first time, she would get lucky. The second, maybe. Then the calm voice would find the door she didn't know she'd left open, and it would go in.

Unless someone told her where the blind spots were.

Alexei stood at the window and thought about Dmitri's face if he found out.

Then he thought about Mama.

Save each other, she had said, with her eyes clear for the first time in months, in that hospital room in the border zone, with the fact storm siren going and the machines screaming. Promise me. Save each other.

He had promised. They all had.

But she hadn't said only each other.

He picked the book with great care.

It had to be something nobody would ever look at twice. Not poetry, not history, nothing an orderly might flip through out of curiosity. It had to be dull. Transcendently, reassuringly dull. The kind of book that, if it were ever found in a Wing C cell, would make anyone who found it assume the inmate had asked for it at random and was even more corrected than they'd thought.

He went to the back of the sixth stack, where the technical manuals lived. The ones nobody touched.

Municipal Water Systems: Maintenance and Distribution (Pre-Histologic Edition, Revised). Faded green cloth. Six hundred pages. Borrowed, according to the stamp inside the cover, twice in forty years.

He opened it at random. Page forty-seven. Section 4.3: Pressure Regulation in Secondary Distribution Networks. A paragraph about valve failures, about what happens when the pressure in a secondary pipe falls too low for too long: the network begins to draw contamination back in, and must be flushed, and the system must be made to

He took the stub of pencil the librarians used for marking damaged pages. He underlined, very lightly, one word.

remember

He looked at it for a long moment. He was The Phantom. He had written a hundred messages on walls in the border zone, for a brother who hadn't understood them. This one felt heavier than all of them together.

He closed the book.

The request slip took thirty seconds. He'd seen the orderlies fill them in a thousand times: Wing C. Cell 7-K. Inmate request. Title. He filled one in in the neat, dull hand he'd practised for the purpose and dropped it in the tray with the other slips for the evening trolley, where the orderly on the desk would stamp it without reading, because nobody ever read them, and because the book was so obviously worthless that it could not possibly matter.

Then he went and stood by the trolley bay, as if waiting to be told what to do next, and waited for Old Songs.

The old man came through at five, pushing the empty trolley back toward the kitchens for the evening meal. He stopped at the bay. He picked up the stamped slip and the book. He looked at the title. He looked at the cell number.

Then, for the first time in five months, he looked directly at Alexei.

His pale blue eyes were not distant at all. They were extremely sharp. They went over Alexei's face, slowly, as if reading it. Alexei held still and let them.

Old Songs put the book on the lower shelf of the trolley, under the tray covers. He pushed the trolley on toward the kitchens.

And as he went, he hummed a little louder.

Alexei walked back into Wing C at dusk, behind the orderly, with his brothers.

The trolley was already on its evening round. He watched it without appearing to watch it, his eyes on the floor ahead, his face slack. Old Songs stopping at each cell. Sliding each tray through. Moving on. He reached 7-K. The slot opened.

Alexei saw the woman step toward it. He saw her take the tray, and pause, and look down.

Then the orderly touched his arm and he went into his cell, 7-B, and sat down on his bed in the same instant as his brothers sat down on theirs, in the same posture, hands on knees, face to the white wall.

He did not look at her again. He didn't need to.

It hit him about a minute later.

Dmitri's fury. It came through the bond like a blow to the chest: cold, enormous, and underneath it, bright and sharp as broken glass, fear. Not for himself. Dmitri had never in his life been afraid for himself. For Alexei. For all three of them. What have you done?

Alexei sat very still and let it hit him. He did not turn his head. He did not open his eyes. He let Dmitri be as angry as he needed to be, the way he had let him be angry in the hospital room with Mama dying between them. Because it was love, and because it was fair.

Then, slowly, where the camera could see it and would see nothing, he moved his right hand on the mattress. Forefinger over middle finger. Hooked. Held.

Mama said save each other, he thought, into the weight, into the anger, as hard and as clearly as he could.

She didn't say only each other.
