Chapter 10: The Whisper

January 5, 2106. Ashford Correctional Facility, the Library

Library privileges came on a Tuesday, in the form of a small blue card that Supervisor Hayes slid through Kira's meal slot with the breakfast tray.

"Congratulations," Hayes said through the speaker grille. She said it the way she said everything, politely, tiredly, looking at a point somewhere to the left of Kira's face. "Dr. Moss says you're making very good progress. One hour, Tuesdays and Fridays, escorted. You may borrow two books at a time."

"Thank you," Kira said, and meant it, and hated that she meant it.

She turned the card over in her fingers after Hayes had gone. It was a plain blue rectangle with her cell number printed on it and a magnetic strip. 7-K. LIBRARY. ESC. It weighed almost nothing. It was the first thing in a month that anyone in Ashford had given her rather than taken.

Good progress.

She knew what that meant. It meant she had learned to lie.

It had taken her four weeks and eleven sessions to get good at it.

The first session she had survived on instinct, dragging her mind down into protocol 7.3 whenever the machine went for Adrian. The second had been worse, because the voice behind the mirror had been ready for her. It had not gone for Adrian at all. It had gone for her mother's kitchen, the arguments, the counting, and she had not built any defences there, and she had come back to her cell and lain on the floor for an hour because the bed felt too far away.

After that she had started building properly.

She did it the way she would have designed a security system. Assume the attacker knows your architecture. Assume they will probe every surface. Don't try to make anything impregnable; make it boring. Put the valuables inside something so dull and so dense that an attacker scanning for value will skim straight past.

So she had taken the specifications she knew best, the ones she had written herself, line by line, over three years, and she had made them into rooms. Protocol 7.3 was the biggest. She put Adrian in it. The bridge went into the section on key rotation. The warehouse went into the hash chaining. Her mother went into the node address tables, four hundred lines of numbers that she could recite in her sleep. The fact she had entered on November eighteenth went into the verification layer, where it belonged.

And in the white chair, when the hum came and the machine began to lift and turn, she recited. Not frantically, the way she had the first time. Steadily. Line after line. Letting it see small, dull crumbs of the real things when it went looking, a flash of the bridge, a glimpse of her mother's face, enough to satisfy it that there was something there to find and that it was finding it. And then back down into the numbers, and the numbers, and the numbers.

It worked. Not perfectly. She lost things. She had lost, she was fairly sure, the colour of Adrian's jacket on the night of the falling stone, and the name of the café, and something about her father that she could no longer quite locate, only feel the absence of, like a gap in a row of teeth. But she lost them slowly. And the things that mattered, she kept.

Dr. Moss had begun to praise her calm.

And the voice behind the mirror had begun to say interesting less often.

That frightened her more than anything. The first time, it had sounded delighted. Lately it had sounded patient. As if it had decided there was no hurry.

The orderly who walked her to the library was the older woman with the braid. Her name, Kira had learned from a tag, was Ines. She talked about her grandchildren the whole way through the east corridor, not to Kira exactly, but in Kira's direction, the way people talk to a pet.

The library was the first room in Ashford that made her want to cry.

It was so ordinary. Tall windows onto white lawns, a sky the color of wet paper. Pale wooden shelves. Tables. The smell of old paper and floor polish. A handful of inmates from the general wards sitting at the tables, reading, their faces absorbed and quiet and entirely present. One of them, a bald man with a pencil behind his ear, was reading a factorepo manual and moving his lips.

At the desk, an orderly stamped a card without looking up. "One hour. Stay in the stacks or at the tables. No talking."

Kira nodded.

She walked into the stacks the way she walked into any system she didn't know yet: slowly, reading the structure. Fiction on the left, by the windows. History in the middle, nine shelves of it, heavily used. Reference and technical at the back, under the gallery. Cameras: two at the ends of the main aisle, one above the desk, one above the tables. Fixed, all of them. Which meant, in a room this full of tall shelving, that there would be places they couldn't see.

A man was shelving books in the history section.

She knew him before she saw his face. It was the overalls, and the walk, and something about the angle of his head: one of the three identical men from the triangle in Wing C. She had watched them for a month, going out every morning and coming back every evening, sitting down in the same instant. Too precise, she had logged. She had filed them, provisionally, as the most thoroughly corrected people in the building.

He was working with a returns cart. Gentle, unhurried movements. Blank face. As she passed the end of his aisle he glanced up, just for a moment, and his gray-blue eyes met hers.

He looked at her one beat too long.

Then he went back to his shelving, and she walked on, and she filed that too. Librarian. One of the triplets. Eye contact: longer than necessary.

The man from 7-H was sitting at the end of the farthest table, by the window.

She stopped walking.

She had not known he would be here. She had not known he was allowed to be here. For a month she had watched him sit on his bed with his head in his hands, and she had come to think of him as a kind of fixture, like the camera domes, something that simply existed in Wing C and did not move. And here he was, in the library, in a chair, with a book in front of him on the table.

He had not opened it. His hands lay flat on either side of it. He was looking at the window, or at the lawn beyond the window, or at nothing.

But he was less gone.

She could see it from here. She could not have said exactly what it was. His posture was the same. His face was as still as ever. But since the corridor, two weeks and more ago, when their eyes had met at the junction, something had been happening across the atrium, and she had watched it happen. He still sat with his head in his hands. But he sat facing her cell now. And a few times, when she had looked up suddenly, she had caught the glint of the cell lights on his glasses, between his fingers, and understood that behind his hands his eyes were open.

And now here, in the library, his eyes were following things. She watched them follow the orderly across the room. She watched them follow a woman from the south ward to the poetry shelf. When a book fell off the returns cart in the history section, his eyes went to it.

Flattened people didn't do that. She had been watching flattened people for a month. Their eyes didn't follow anything.

She noticed one more thing, and she did not like it.

The blue card in her pocket said TUESDAYS AND FRIDAYS. She had been given it this morning. And here he was, on a Tuesday, in the library, when she had never once seen him leave Wing C except for sessions. They had both been given library privileges, apparently, in the same week.

Coincidence, she thought.

She was an architect. She did not believe in coincidence. Coincidence was what you called a correlation before you had found the cause.

But she wanted this one. She was surprised by how much she wanted it. So she noted it, and put it down, and did not pick it up again.

And then, looking at him in the gray window light, she finally knew where she knew him from.

It was the glasses, and the angle, and the thinness of his face in profile. She had seen that face before, in October, on a screen, over breakfast in her apartment before a twelve-hour shift at the Spire. A small news item on page nine. A photograph of a young man in a suit and glasses, looking earnest and a little frightened, outside the Enforcement Center.

JUNIOR HISTORIAN CONVICTED DESPITE REPORTING OUTLAW LOVER. "LOYALTY NO DEFENCE," SAYS JUDGE.

She remembered reading it and feeling a small twist of discomfort, and then telling herself that the Judge knew best, and going to work.

Marcus Chen.

That was who he was. That was what he had believed. He had done everything the system asked, and it had sent him here anyway. And she had read about it over coffee and gone to work, and six weeks later the system had sent her here too.

She did not decide to do it. She simply found that she had turned, and was walking toward the back of the room, toward the reference shelves under the gallery, along a route that would take her past the end of his table.

As she passed, she let her hand brush the corner of the table. Not his hand. Not his book. Just the wood, two inches from his fingers.

He looked up.

She kept walking. She did not look back. She walked into the reference section, between two tall shelves of technical manuals, and stopped in front of the water systems, which were the dullest books in the building, and pulled one out at random, and opened it, and pretended to read.

Her heart was going so hard she could hear it.

Stupid, she thought. There's a camera at the end of every aisle. They'll see him get up. They'll see him come back here. They'll see us—

Behind her, wheels on the floor.

She turned her head a few degrees. The librarian, the triplet, was pushing his returns cart along the back of the stacks. It was piled high with books. As she watched, he stopped it at the end of her aisle, exactly across the mouth of the gap between the shelves, and began, slowly and methodically, to sort the books on its top shelf.

The cart was about a metre and a half tall with the books on it. It stood directly between the aisle and the camera at the end of the room.

He did not look at her. He did not look at anything but the books.

Kira stared at the side of his face for a long moment. Then she filed it with page forty-seven, where it belonged.

Marcus Chen came around the end of the shelves a minute later.

He moved slowly, like a man walking in his sleep, and he stopped a metre away from her, facing the shelf, and pulled out a book of his own. He did not look at her. She did not look at him. They stood side by side in the narrow aisle with their backs to the cart and their faces to the water systems, and the silence of the library went on all around them, the pages turning, the orderly's stamp at the desk.

When he spoke it was barely a breath.

"I know you."

She kept her eyes on the page. Section 7: Reservoir Maintenance.

"I can't remember from where," he whispered.

"You don't," she said, just as quietly. "We've never met."

A pause.

"You're Marcus Chen," she said. "You were in the news."

Another pause, longer. When he answered, his voice was very small, and it had something in it that made her close her fingers around the edge of the book.

"...Was I?"

She understood. It came to her in one piece, the way the shape of a system comes to you when you finally see the diagram. He did not remember. They had taken it. His arrest, his trial, the headline, the photograph outside the Enforcement Center. They had taken his own story and left him standing here in a library, asking a stranger whether it had happened to him.

"Yes," she said. "In October. You reported an outlaw. They convicted you anyway."

He was silent.

"I'm Kira Osman," she said. "I was a systems architect at the Factorepo Spire. On November eighteenth I entered a security protocol update into the factorepo. I spoke it aloud and watched it verify. Three weeks later it was gone. Not corrupted. Not altered. Gone, as if it had never been entered. I reported it." She swallowed. "They say I never entered it. They say I falsified my work. Ninety-two percent."

She had told it a dozen times. To her supervisor. To Kade. To Dr. Moss, who had smiled. To the voice behind the mirror, which had said state your crime, please. Nobody had ever answered her.

Marcus Chen stood beside her in the narrow aisle, holding a book about municipal reservoirs he was not reading, and was quiet for so long that she thought he had drifted away again.

Then he said, very softly: "If you entered it, it happened."

She turned her head and looked at him before she could stop herself.

He was still facing the shelf. But something had come into his face, a kind of focus, like a lens being turned. For a moment he did not look like a man who had been emptied. He looked like what he had been: a Junior Historian at the Regional Factorepo, who had spent three years of his life deciding what was true.

"A fact doesn't stop being true because the record lost it," he said. "The record is supposed to follow the fact. Not the other way round."

Kira looked at him.

She had been in Ashford for a month. She had been accused by her supervisor, by an enforcer, by The Judge, by a gentle doctor and a calm voice behind a mirror. And the first person in all of it to believe her was the man everyone in Wing C had given up on.

She found she could not speak for a moment.

"How do you do it?" he whispered.

His voice had changed. The focus was gone, or going. What was left was raw.

"You came out angry. I saw you. How do you resist? They almost erased me. I think they did erase me. I think I'm..." He stopped. "I don't know what's left."

"Your real memories are still there," she said. "I think. Most of them. They just can't find the door. You can't find the door."

"How do you resist?"

She told him. Fast, in a whisper, the whole of it compressed, the way you'd explain an emergency procedure. "It follows feeling. Not information. It goes where the heat is. So don't give it heat. Think technically. Think of the most boring things you know, things you've memorised, procedures, formats, numbers. Put what you care about inside them. Give it the dull things and let it skim."

He was listening. She could feel him listening, the way you can feel someone holding their breath.

"And don't," she said. "Listen to me. Don't let them see you coming back."

He turned his head a fraction.

"I've been watching you," she said. "For two weeks. You're facing my cell now. You're opening your eyes behind your hands. You're here, in the library, following people with your eyes. I can see it. If I can see it, they can." She pressed her thumb hard into the edge of the page. "You're their success, Marcus. I've heard the orderlies. Beautiful baseline. If they see you recovering, they won't just try again. They'll finish it. You have to stay broken. On the outside. As long as it takes."

He didn't answer. But she saw his shoulders move, very slightly: down, and in, and a little rounded. The shape of a broken man. He did it perfectly. He'd had a lot of practice.

"I can't remember enough," he whispered. "I don't know how to keep anything. There's nothing left to put in the boring things."

"Then I'll remember for both of us," Kira said. "Until you can."

She did not know she was going to say it until she had said it. It came out of her fully formed, the way a line of code comes sometimes, perfect and complete, before you know what you are writing.

He was very still.

Behind them, the wheels of the cart moved on the floor.

The librarian had finished sorting. He pushed the cart slowly away along the back of the stacks, and the camera at the end of the room could see the aisle again, and in it a woman from Wing C reading a manual about reservoir maintenance, and a man from Wing C a metre away with his head bowed over a book he was holding upside down.

Kira reached over without looking and turned his book the right way up.

About ninety seconds, she thought afterwards. That was all it had been. Ninety seconds in a library aisle.

Ines walked her back to Wing C at the end of the hour, talking about her grandson's school play. Kira did not hear a word of it.

The door of 7-K sealed behind her. She stood in the middle of her cell and found that she was shaking.

Not from fear. That was the strange thing. She had been afraid for a month, and she knew what fear felt like in her hands. This was different. This was something she had not felt since the night at the Spire when she saw her fact had vanished and understood that nobody, nobody at all, was going to believe her.

She had been believed.

She sat down on the bed. After a while, when the shaking stopped, she reached for the book at the end of it, the thick, faded green book with the cracked spine that she had kept for four weeks without knowing why. Municipal Water Systems: Maintenance and Distribution. It fell open at page forty-seven.

remember

She looked at the faint pencil line.

She thought about the old man with the trolley, walking away, humming. About the librarian's gray-blue eyes, one beat too long. About the cart, parked with perfect precision between an aisle and a camera, and then moved away again at exactly the moment it was no longer needed.

About three men in a triangle who sat down in the same instant, every evening, too precisely.

Someone here is awake, she thought.

She looked out through her glass, across the atrium, at 7-A, 7-B and 7-C. Three identical men sat on their three beds with their faces to their three white walls, blank and peaceful as statues.

Someone here is awake, she thought again. And I don't know yet if that's an ally or a trap.

Across from her, in 7-H, Marcus Chen sat on the edge of his bed with his head in his hands. Between two of his fingers, very faintly, the cell light caught the glass of his spectacles.

He was looking at her.
