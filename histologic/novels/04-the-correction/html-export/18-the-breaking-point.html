Chapter 18: The Breaking Point

May 25 – June 4, 2106. Treatment Room C-2, and the Director's Office

The notice came through the meal slot on a Tuesday, on a small white card, the way every change in Wing C came.

C-1. SCHEDULE REVISION. SESSIONS: INTENSIVE. EFFECTIVE IMMEDIATELY.

Marcus read it sitting on the edge of his bed, and felt his hands go cold.

He did not let anything move in his face. He put the card down on the blanket beside him, face up, where the camera could see it, and put his head in his hands, in the shape of a broken man, and behind his hands he stared at the dark.

Intensive. Every two or three days. The way it had been in October and November, when he was going under.

Across the atrium he heard nothing, of course, but he knew that the card would be in the primers by morning. Kira would know. Tanaka would know. The brothers would know. And none of them could do anything about it at all.

Micro-drift, the voice had said in April. Nothing much. We'll keep an eye on it.

It had been keeping an eye on it.

The first intensive session was on the Thursday.

C-2. The white room, the white chair, the hood on its jointed arm. The orderlies strapping his forearms. Dr. Moss on her stool, taking his hand, her face creased with worry.

"It's just a precaution, Marcus," she said. "You've been so stable. We just want to make sure you stay stable. For the summer." She squeezed his fingers. "You're so important to us."

At the console, Dr. Morrison did not look up. His face was the color of old paper. His hand, above the controls, was very still, in the way a hand is still when someone is holding it still.

And then the door opened again, and someone else came in.

Marcus had imagined him many times. For seven months he had been a voice behind a mirror: calm, patient, pleasant, just behind Marcus's head, always. In the dark, in his cell, Marcus had given the voice a face without meaning to. Something cold. Something sharp.

The man who came in was neither.

He was in his fifties. Medium height. Thinning brown hair, neatly cut. A gray suit, not a white coat, well made and unremarkable. A pleasant, even-featured face that you would forget in a crowd. He wore no jewellery. His hands were very clean.

His eyes were the only thing about him that stayed with you. They were light brown, and they were interested. Deeply, genuinely, almost affectionately interested, the way a man's eyes are interested in a book he has been looking forward to reading.

"Good morning, Mr. Chen," said Dr. Reeves.

He did not come to the chair. He pulled a second stool up beside the console, next to Morrison, and sat, and crossed his legs, and folded his clean hands on his knee.

"I thought I'd join you in person today," he said. "It's been a long time since we met properly. You've been doing such good work."

Marcus looked at the ceiling. He let his eyes be slow. He let a lag come before he answered, the way Dmitri had taught him through the primers, the way the flattened ones did it.

"Good morning," he said.

Reeves smiled.

The hood came down.

He felt it settle behind his ears. The hum. The interface under his skull, saying yes, the way it always did, like a dog that loves the wrong master.

And before the probe even engaged he had begun.

Traffic incident, corner of Fifth and Main, fourteen-oh-two hours, two vehicles, minor damage, no injuries, verified by sensor network, entered. Property dispute, Linden Avenue, boundary fence, surveillance confirms prior agreement, verified, entered.

He had built the vault for four months. He knew its walls the way he knew the shape of his own hands. He poured the formats into the front of his mind until there was nothing there but data entry, flat and gray and endless, and in the white chair the machine went looking, and found a Junior Historian at his desk.

It did not go to Elena.

That was the first thing he noticed, and it frightened him. For seven months, every session had begun with the café, the river, the green dress, as if the machine could not quite leave her alone. Today it passed her by as if she weren't there. It went past his mother. It went past the folded flag.

It went looking for something else.

"There's someone new in here," said Reeves, softly, from the stool by the console. He said it the way a man says oh, look on a walk in the woods, seeing a bird he hasn't seen before. "Isn't there, Marcus? Someone new." A pause. "Let's see her."

Birth, Central Hospital, oh-three-fifty hours, female, three point one kilograms, verified, entered. Business transaction, market district. Personnel entry: Osman, K. Senior Architect. Location: Wing C, 7-K. Status:

He did not finish it. He never finished it. Finishing it would make it warm.

The probe went past the entry. He felt it go past. And then it stopped, and came back, the way a hand comes back to a pocket it has patted once and felt something in.

Personnel entry: Osman, K.

It began to press.

He had never felt it press like this. In October it had been greedy, fast, lifting and turning everything. This was different. This was slow. Patient. Exploratory. It did not grab at the entry. It felt along its edges. It went round and round the thing he had folded into the format, testing, lifting a corner here and a corner there, as if trying to find out how far down into him it went.

Traffic incident, Fifth and Main. Property dispute. Birth.

He poured more formats in. Hundreds of them. The whole of the fourteenth floor. Every entry he had ever made.

It kept pressing.

The library. It found the library. He felt it happen and could not stop it: the reference shelves under the gallery, the cart across the mouth of the aisle, the book about reservoirs, Kira's voice beside him, barely a breath. You don't. We've never met. He had kept that one in the vault since January, folded into a property dispute on Linden Avenue, and the probe had found the fold, and was lifting it.

Don't, he thought. Not that one. Not that one.

He did the only thing left. He let it have the fold, and dragged the rest of him down, down, into the formats, into the fish market and the traffic incidents and the births and deaths, and held on.

If you entered it, it happened.

He felt the library come loose. It came away from him the way a tooth comes away, with a pull and a sudden emptiness.

Business transaction, market district, fish. Verified. Entered.

"Interesting," said Reeves.

He sounded delighted. Genuinely, warmly delighted, the way a teacher sounds when a pupil does something unexpected and clever.

"You've learned to hide," he said. "Haven't you? Someone's taught you." A pause. "Who taught you, Marcus?"

Marcus let his mouth go slack.

He let his eyes drift across the ceiling and stop at nothing. He let a long time go by before he answered, and when he did, he let the answer be the wrong answer, the kind of answer a flattened man gives to a question he has not really understood.

"The trolley," he said slowly. "Comes at seven."

He did not know where it came from. It was the kind of thing Samuel might have said, once. The kind of thing that means nothing.

Silence from the stool.

Then Morrison's hand moved on the console. A quick movement, clumsy, the heel of his hand catching a row of controls. A shrill tone went up from the cradle, and the hood jerked, and the hum under Marcus's skull dropped out all at once into nothing.

"Sorry," Morrison said. "Sorry. Calibration fault. The left temporal pad, I think. It's been drifting." He was already reaching for the controls. "Abort for subject safety. I'm sorry, Dr. Reeves. I'll have it checked."

The hood lifted.

Reeves did not say anything for a moment. He looked at Morrison. Then he looked at Marcus, lying in the white chair with his slack face and his drifting eyes, and his light brown eyes were as interested as ever.

"Of course," he said pleasantly. "Have it looked at." He stood, and smoothed his jacket. "We'll continue on Saturday, Mr. Chen. You've given me a great deal to think about."

He went out.

Dr. Moss leaned over the chair and took Marcus's hand in both of hers. There were tears in her eyes. "You did so well," she said. "You did so, so well."

He was sick in the corridor, for the first time since November.

The orderlies were kind about it. They walked him back along the atrium with a hand under each arm. He kept his face slack and his eyes on the floor, and when the door of 7-H sealed behind him he sat down on the bed and put his head in his hands and shook.

He reached for the library.

The reference shelves. The cart. The book about reservoirs. Her voice.

There was nothing there.

Not blurred. Not filed. Nothing. A smooth blank place in him, like the place where his mother's name had been. He knew that there had been a library. He knew it the way he knew the date of the Founding. He knew that he and Kira had spoken there, the first time, in January, because it was in the primers, because she had told him afterwards, because it was a fact.

But the memory itself, the thing he had lived, was gone. They had taken it out of him with a slow, careful, interested hand, and they had not put anything back.

He sat on his bed with the smooth blank place in him and did not cry, because the camera was watching.

The Saturday session was worse, and the Monday session after that, and he lost other things. Small things. The colour of the scarf the girl wore who walked in circles in the garden. A line from one of Isaiah's primers. The taste of the warm sweet drink that came on the evening tray. He noticed each one afterwards, lying awake, probing the gaps.

But he did not lose Kira.

He kept her in the formats and held on, and every time the probe found the edge of her and began to feel its way along, he gave it something else instead. A memory of the garden. The sparrows. The girl walking in circles. He let it take those. He let it take anything, anything, that was not the core of her. And Morrison, at the console, twice more had calibration faults at the exact moment when Marcus thought he could not hold any longer, and twice more the sessions ended early for subject safety.

Reeves did not come back in person. The voice was behind the mirror again, as it had always been. But it had changed. It was not bored anymore. It was attentive. It listened to him the way Dmitri said the machine had listened to the brothers: not trying to break him, but trying to understand.

Good, it said sometimes, softly, when the probe found the edge of Kira and he pushed it away. Good. Very good.

As if something were being measured and the measurement was pleasing.

He did not understand that. He was too tired to try.

On the fourth of June, an orderly came to his cell in the middle of the afternoon.

Not a session day. Not the usual pair. A young woman he had never seen, with a tablet, and a tag that said ADMINISTRATION.

"Mr. Chen. The Director would like to see you. A routine stability interview." She smiled. "It won't take long."

He had not known there was a Director. He had never thought about it. Ashford had Hayes, and Moss, and Morrison, and the voice. Ashford did not, as far as he had ever been able to see, have anyone above them.

He walked with her through the atrium, and past the treatment corridor, and through the vestibule, and out into the main building, with its long bright windows and its soft chairs and its ordinary patients reading in the sun. Up a flight of stairs. Along a carpeted corridor. To a door with a small steel plate on it.

H. KOVAČ. DIRECTOR.

The orderly knocked, and opened the door, and stepped back.

The office was not what he expected either.

It was small. A desk, a window onto the pond with the fountain, two chairs, a shelf of files. A single potted plant, slightly overwatered. A woman sitting behind the desk, reading something on a screen, who looked up when he came in.

She was in her early fifties. Dark hair going gray, pulled back hard. A thin, precise face with deep lines at the corners of the mouth, and very tired eyes behind rimless glasses. She wore a plain dark suit. She looked, he thought, like a woman who had not slept properly in a long time and had stopped expecting to.

"Mr. Chen," she said. "Please sit."

He sat.

She looked at him for a moment. Then she reached across her desk and touched a panel, and somewhere in the ceiling, very faintly, a sound he had not noticed stopped. A hum. He had been hearing it so long, everywhere in Ashford, that he had stopped hearing it.

"That was the room's recording," she said. "It's off. So is the camera. You can look at the dome if you like. The light's out."

He did not look. He kept his eyes on the edge of her desk. He let the lag come, before he spoke.

"I don't understand."

"I think you do," said Director Kovač.

She folded her hands on the desk.

"Marcus," she said. "You were almost corrected. I've seen your baselines. I've seen December. I have never seen a reduction as complete as yours. You were, as far as the instruments could tell, finished." She paused. "And then you came back. Why?"

He should have lied. He knew he should have lied. Dmitri would have told him to lie, and Kira, and Tanaka. Everything they had built for five months depended on his lying.

He thought about the library. The smooth blank place in him, where it had been.

He thought: I am so tired of being a shape.

"Someone gave me a reason to," he said.

He heard his own voice. It was not slack. It was low and rough and very clear, and it was his.

"And because it isn't healing," he said. "What happens in that chair. It isn't correction. It's erasure."

The Director's expression flickered.

It was a small thing. The lines at the corners of her mouth deepened. Something moved in her tired eyes, very fast, and was gone.

"You think I don't know that?" she said.

She opened a drawer and took out a folder. An actual paper folder, thick, battered at the corners, held shut with an elastic band. She put it on the desk between them and took off the band and opened it.

"Eleven years," she said. "I've run this facility for eleven years. For most of them I believed in what we did here. I want you to know that. I'm not going to pretend I didn't. I believed correction healed people. I've seen it heal people. There are patients downstairs right now who came in unable to sleep for the nightmares, and who will go home able to sleep. That's real."

She turned a page. And another.

"And for the last four years," she said, "I've been keeping this."

They were case notes. Printed, not on screens. Photographs clipped to the corners. Salience charts. A face on each page, and under each face a date of admission and a date of transfer. Long-term care. Long-term care. Long-term care.

"Failures," she said. "The ones the ordinary protocols went too far with. The ones who didn't come back. I started keeping them on paper because on paper nobody can adjust them." She turned another page. "There were three or four a year, before. Then Wing C was opened, and Continuity began, and there were more."

She turned the folder toward him and put her finger on a page.

A young man with curly brown hair and a thin, animated face, smiling at the camera in his intake photograph as if someone had just told him a joke.

FERRIS, SAMUEL. ADMITTED MARCH 2105. CONTINUITY PRE-SERIES (CALIBRATION). REDUCTION TO BASELINE: COMPLETE. STATUS: RETAINED.

Marcus looked at the photograph for a long time.

"He was a calibration subject," Kovač said quietly. "Before the seven. They used him to set the gains on the new cradles. He was a student. He'd written an essay." She closed the folder. "I thought I was helping people. I thought the east wing was a research unit for trauma. And then I saw what Continuity was, and I didn't stop it. I kept a folder." She laughed once, without any humour at all. "That's what I did. I kept a folder."

"Why are you telling me this?" Marcus said.

She looked at him over her glasses for a long moment.

"Because you were our first success," she said. "Reeves showed you to the Ministry's observers in January. On a screen. Your December baselines. The most complete reduction to baseline ever recorded in a subject of your profile. They were very impressed." She took her glasses off and rubbed her eyes. "And then in February I was reviewing the garden footage. I review a sample of all the recreation footage every month; it's routine. And there was a man on a bench who was supposed to be empty. And he was looking at a woman."

Marcus said nothing.

"I have seen a great many empty people, Mr. Chen," the Director said. "Hundreds. I have seen them in this building for eleven years. Nobody who is erased looks at anyone like that." She put her glasses back on. "That's when I knew. That whatever Reeves had done to you, it hadn't held. And that something else was happening in my building that I didn't understand."

She hesitated. Then she reached into the drawer again and took out a single sheet of paper, and laid it on top of the folder.

"And then I did something I should have done a long time ago," she said. "I pulled Ms. Osman's file. The full file. Not the summary they give me. The acquisition file."

She put her finger on a line.

Record anomaly engineered (ref. R.) to trigger investigation. Associated person of interest used to support contamination finding: KOVAČ, ADRIAN.

"Adrian," the Director said, and her voice did something strange. "Adrian Kovač. My nephew. My younger brother's boy. I haven't spoken to him in six years, because he became an outlaw and I run a correction facility, and we didn't know what to say to each other at Christmas." She looked at the paper. "Reeves stole a fact to take her. And then he used my family to make it believable."

She turned the paper face down.

"So I know it's erasure," she said. "I've known for longer than I'll ever be able to forgive myself. You don't need to tell me."

He sat in the chair and did not know what to say.

He thought about the brothers, and the primers, and the garden stones. About everything they had built, and how carefully, and how it all rested on nobody on the staff knowing. He had just told the Director of Ashford that he was awake. He had sat in her office and said the word erasure in his own voice.

He did not know whether he had just saved them or destroyed them.

The Director was watching him. She did not promise anything. She did not reach across the desk to take his hand, the way Dr. Moss would have. She simply sat, a tired, precise woman in a plain dark suit, with a paper folder of failures in front of her, and asked him one question.

"If there were a way out," she said, "could you all be ready by August?"

Marcus looked at her.

All. Not you. All.

She knew. Not everything. But enough. She knew there was an all.

"Yes," he said.

She nodded once. She touched the panel on her desk, and in the ceiling the faint hum came back.

"Thank you, Mr. Chen," she said, in a completely different voice, brisk and administrative. "Your stability interview is complete. You may go."

That night he sat on his bed with an arithmetic primer on his knees and a pin in his fingers, and he tried to reach for the library one more time.

The reference shelves under the gallery. A book about reservoirs. A cart across the mouth of an aisle.

The smooth blank place. Nothing.

He pricked the message slowly. His hands were clumsy, and the holes were too large, and he did not care.

MC-22. 4 JUNE. FOR K ONLY.

I LOST THE FIRST DAY. THE LIBRARY. THEY TOOK IT ON THURSDAY. I KNOW IT HAPPENED. I CANNOT FIND IT.

TELL IT TO ME AGAIN.

He did not put in anything about Kovač. That would go to all seven, tomorrow, in a longer message, with more care. This one was only for her.

He sent it out on the morning trolley. He did not know what he expected.

It came back on the evening round. A primer. The same one. He opened it on his knees and held the first page up to the light, and saw that she had used every page.

She had written it down. All of it. Every word they had said to each other in the library, in January, in the reference aisle, in ninety seconds. Her careful small holes ran on page after page, under times tables and word problems and long division, in the order it had happened.

I KNOW YOU. I CAN'T REMEMBER FROM WHERE.

YOU DON'T. WE'VE NEVER MET.

YOU'RE MARCUS CHEN. YOU WERE IN THE NEWS.

...WAS I?

He read it sitting on his bed with his head bowed over the primer, in the shape of a broken man. He did not remember any of it. He would never remember it. It was gone.

But she did.

IF YOU ENTERED IT, IT HAPPENED.

A FACT DOESN'T STOP BEING TRUE BECAUSE THE RECORD LOST IT.

He had said that. He had said that to her, and now she was saying it back to him, in pinpricks, and he did not remember saying it, and it was still true.

The last page. The last line. Pricked very carefully, the holes small and even, like a signature.

I'LL REMEMBER FOR BOTH OF US UNTIL YOU CAN.

He closed the book.

He put his head in his hands, and behind his hands, in the dark, where the camera could not see, he wept for the first time since October. For the library. For the smooth blank place. For a woman who had given him back, in holes in paper, a day he would never have again.
