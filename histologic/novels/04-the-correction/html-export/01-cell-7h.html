Chapter 1: Cell 7-H

October 8–9, 2105. Ashford Correctional Facility, Wing C

The first thing Marcus did in Wing C was make a map.

He did not decide to do it. He simply found himself doing it, standing at the glass with his hands at his sides, turning his head slowly from left to right the way he had once scanned a column of unverified entries. It was the historian's reflex: when you do not understand a thing, you record it. Understanding comes later, or it doesn't, but the record remains.

Wing C. Atrium, curved, approximately forty metres long. Two levels of cells. Cells glazed on two sides: atrium face and neighbour face. Remaining walls white, opaque. Skylight. No visible clocks.

He counted the cells he could see. Twenty-two on his own level, as far as the curve allowed. Perhaps as many again above. Not all of them were occupied. Of those that were, he counted eleven people clearly, and parts of several more.

Cell 7-G, adjacent, left. The eager young man. He was perhaps twenty-five, with curly brown hair and a thin, animated face, and he had not stopped trying to communicate since Marcus arrived. He mouthed words with exaggerated care, pointing at himself. After the third attempt Marcus understood the shape of it: SAM. SAM-U-EL. He pointed at Marcus with his eyebrows raised.

Marcus hesitated, then mouthed his own name.

Samuel grinned and gave him a thumbs-up, delighted, as if Marcus had passed a test. Then he held up his hand, fingers spread, and closed it slowly into a fist, and opened it again. Marcus didn't know what that meant. Samuel did it twice more and then shrugged, cheerfully, and sat down on his bed.

Cell 7-D, across and to the right. The small woman with the silver-streaked hair. Mid-forties, Japanese features, hair pinned up in a tidy bun despite the prison clothes. She sat very upright. She had watched him arrive and she was watching him now, not rudely, but with a steady, clinical attention, as though he were a set of symptoms. When he met her eyes she did not look away. After a long moment she inclined her head, very slightly. A greeting, or an acknowledgment. Then she turned back to whatever she had been doing, which, as far as he could tell, was nothing.

Cells 7-A, 7-B, 7-C. Across the atrium, in a triangle.

He looked at the three identical men for a long time.

They had not moved. They sat on their beds in the same posture, backs straight, hands on knees, faces calm. At some point an orderly came along the upper walkway and opened their doors, one after another, and the three of them stood up at the same moment and walked out, and fell into step behind the orderly as if they had rehearsed it. They went through a door at the far end of the atrium and were gone.

Work detail, Marcus thought, looking at their overalls. They're the model patients. The cured ones. They've earned the right to leave the wing.

It was, in its way, encouraging.

The others were harder to look at. Four cells along from Samuel, a woman of about sixty sat on the floor with her back against the bed and her legs straight out in front of her, like a child's doll, and stared at the white wall. On the upper level a man stood at his glass with his palms flat against it, not looking out, not looking at anything. There were others: people who were simply there, the way furniture is there. They did not sleep and they did not seem awake. When Marcus looked at them for too long, something in the back of his throat began to ache.

Several occupants display marked flattening of affect, he told himself, reaching for the neutral words. Cause unknown.

Cause unknown. He had never in his life entered a fact with cause unknown. The factorepo did not like it. Everything had a cause, and every cause was recorded.

There was no sound at all.

He tested it, feeling foolish, by speaking aloud. "Hello." His voice came back to him flat and close, swallowed by the walls. He knocked on the glass and felt the vibration in his knuckles and heard nothing but the knock itself, dull and small, contained. Samuel, next door, did not look up. The sound had not reached him.

Cells are soundproofed.

He sat on the bed. The mattress was thin and firm and perfectly clean. The camera dome in the ceiling corner had a tiny green light that did not blink. He was used to cameras. He had lived his whole life under them, and he had told Elena that he found them comforting. In a world where everything is known, nothing can be hidden.

He looked up at the green light and found that it did not comfort him at all. The cameras in the capital watched everyone. This one watched only him. It was a different thing, and he had not known that until now.

Meals came through the slot by the door.

The first came at what he guessed was midday, though without a clock he could only judge by the skylight. He heard nothing: he saw movement at the edge of his vision and turned to find the slot sliding open, and a tray already half through it, and beyond the glass an old man pushing a steel trolley along the atrium floor.

The old man was thin and slightly stooped, with long white hair tied back in a ponytail and a full white beard. He wore gray like the others, but with a canvas apron over it. He moved slowly and without hurry, stopping at each occupied cell, sliding a tray into each slot, moving on.

When the slot opened, Marcus heard him.

It lasted perhaps two seconds, the time it took for the tray to pass through the gap in the soundproofing. The old man was humming. It was a low, wandering tune, slightly off-key, in a mode Marcus had never heard, with a lift at the end of each phrase like a question. It was not any anthem Marcus knew. It was not in any broadcast. It sounded older than the factorepo, older than the Founding, as old as something handed down by people who did not write things down.

Then the slot closed, and the sound was gone, and the old man moved on to Samuel's cell, and Samuel waved at him enthusiastically, and the old man nodded to him without smiling.

Marcus sat with the tray on his knees and did not eat.

It was the only human sound he had heard since the doors of Wing C closed behind him. It had lasted two seconds. He found that he was holding very still, waiting for the next meal, so that he could hear it again.

He ate eventually, because he had always eaten what was put in front of him. Rice, vegetables, a piece of fish, a cup of something warm and sweet. It was better food than he'd had in the Enforcement Center. He noted that. He noted the time of the meal by the angle of the light on the far wall. He noted that the woman on the floor did not eat hers, and that no one came to take it away.

And then, because there was nothing else to do, he began to write.

Not on paper. He had no paper. He wrote the way he had written every night for years, in his head, in the precise, formal cadence of a fact entry.

October eighth, 2105. Ashford Correctional Facility, Wing C, Cell 7-H. Subject arrived approximately oh-nine-thirty hours. Received by Supervisor Hayes. Informed that Wing C is a specialised unit. Informed that doctors there are the best available. Advised: "Do what the doctors ask."

It was absurd. There was no one to submit the entries to, no system to verify them. And yet, as he composed them, he felt his breathing slow and his shoulders loosen. The world was still there. The world still had a shape. A thing had happened, and he had put it in its place.

Adjacent occupant: Samuel. Approximately twenty-five years old. Friendly. Repeated hand gesture, meaning unknown.

Meal distributed approximately twelve-fifteen hours by an elderly male inmate. Inmate was humming.

He hesitated over that entry. It did not seem like a fact. It seemed like something else, something that the factorepo would have no field for. He entered it anyway.

Inmate was humming. The tune was unfamiliar.

In the afternoon, Supervisor Hayes came.

She stood outside his glass and pressed something on a panel beside the door, and a small speaker grille above the slot crackled, and then her voice was in the room with him, thin and slightly metallic.

"Marcus. How are you settling in?"

He stood up. "I'm fine."

"Good." She glanced at a tablet. "Your first session is scheduled for tomorrow morning. Someone will come for you after breakfast."

"A session with whom?"

"The treatment team." She said it the way people say the weather. "Dr. Moss will be there. You met her last night, I think."

"And Dr. Reeves?"

It was a small thing. He watched her face as he said the name, and saw it register, a tiny flicker, gone before he could be sure.

"Dr. Reeves oversees Wing C," she said. "You may not meet him directly. Most people don't."

"Supervisor Hayes, what are they going to do to me?"

"Help you." She said it too quickly, then seemed to hear herself, and said it again more slowly, as if trying to make it true by saying it carefully. "They're going to help you."

Marcus looked past her, at the cell next door, where Samuel was sitting cross-legged on his bed, drumming on his knees with his fingers and grinning at nothing.

"What about him?" Marcus asked. "What's Samuel here for?"

Hayes followed his glance. Something in her face softened, and then, strangely, closed.

"Samuel's doing very well," she said. "He's one of our best."

She switched off the speaker. For a moment she stood there on the other side of the glass, looking at him with an expression he couldn't read. Then she walked away along the curve of the atrium, and he watched her go until he couldn't see her anymore.

The three identical men came back at dusk.

The skylight had turned deep blue and the cells had brightened to compensate, so that Wing C glowed against the darkness like a lantern. The door at the end of the atrium opened and they walked in, one after another, with an orderly behind them. They went to their three cells in their triangle. The doors opened and closed. They sat down on their beds at the same moment, in the same posture, hands on knees.

Marcus watched them.

After a while he realised that one of them, the one in the cell nearest the atrium's centre, was watching him back.

It was not obvious. The man's face was as blank as before. His posture had not changed. But his eyes, gray-blue and very steady, were fixed on Marcus's cell, and they stayed there. When Marcus looked directly at him, the man did not look away, and did not react, and did not blink for what felt like a very long time.

Then an orderly passed along the walkway, and the man's gaze drifted, slowly and without apparent purpose, to the white wall of his cell, and stayed there.

Corrected, Marcus told himself. That's what it looks like when it works. Calm. Peaceful.

He did not know why the word peaceful made him feel cold.

They came for Samuel a little after the lights dimmed for the night.

Two orderlies in pale green walked along the atrium floor and stopped at 7-G. One of them pressed the panel. Samuel's door slid open.

Samuel was already standing. He had seen them coming. The eager, friendly animation had gone out of him all at once, and what replaced it was something Marcus recognised because he had felt it himself, in the interview room with Kade, at the moment he understood that the numbers did not care about his loyalty. It was fear. Not panic. Just plain, childlike fear.

The orderlies were gentle. One of them put a hand on Samuel's shoulder, not roughly. They said something to him that Marcus couldn't hear. Samuel nodded. He stepped out of the cell.

Then he stopped, and turned, and looked at Marcus through the glass.

He lifted his hand and pressed his palm flat against the glass wall between their cells. His fingers were spread. He looked at Marcus, and his mouth moved, and this time Marcus didn't need to be able to read it. It wasn't a word. It was just breath.

Marcus didn't know why he did it. He had not touched another person voluntarily since Elena. He stood up, walked to the glass, and put his own palm flat against it, against Samuel's, with only the cold smooth pane between them.

Samuel's face changed. Just slightly. Something in it steadied.

Then the orderly said something, and Samuel took his hand away, and they walked him along the curve of the atrium and through the door at the far end, and the door closed.

Marcus stood with his hand on the glass for a long time.

He did not know how long they were gone. Three hours. Four. The atrium lights stayed dim. Across the way, the small woman in 7-D was sitting up in bed, awake, with her arms around her knees. She was watching the door at the far end of the atrium. Once she looked at Marcus, and he saw in her face something he could not have named then, and later understood was grief. Not for herself. For what she knew was about to come back through that door.

It opened.

The orderlies walked Samuel back along the atrium floor. He walked easily, not supported, not stumbling. His head was up. When they reached 7-G, one orderly pressed the panel, and the door slid open, and Samuel stepped inside without being told, and the door slid shut.

He stood in the middle of his cell.

He looked around, slowly, the way a person looks around a room they are seeing for the first time. The bed. The sink. The white wall. The glass. His gaze travelled across the glass wall between their cells and stopped, briefly, at the place where their hands had been.

There was no recognition in it at all.

His face was calm. It was the calmest face Marcus had ever seen. Every line of worry and eagerness and fear had been smoothed out of it, as if someone had taken a cloth to a window. He looked at the place on the glass where Marcus's hand had been, and then at Marcus himself, standing just behind it, and his expression did not change. He was not being polite. He was not ignoring him. There was simply nothing in Samuel that knew there had been a hand.

He sat down on the bed. He folded his hands in his lap. He turned his face toward the white wall.

He was still looking at it when the skylight began to turn gray with the dawn.

Marcus did not sleep.

He lay on his back and did arithmetic, because arithmetic was all he had. Samuel had been doing very well. Samuel was one of our best. Samuel had been taken at night and brought back four hours later with nothing behind his eyes. And tomorrow morning, after breakfast, someone would come for Marcus.

A session. Then another. How many? Every few days? For weeks? Months? Up to one year. He tried to calculate it and couldn't, because he did not know the rate, and without the rate the calculation was meaningless. All he knew was the result. The result was sitting in 7-G, staring at a wall.

He had been told that correction would teach him to believe. He had lain awake in the Enforcement Center the night before, almost grateful, because he already believed and so, surely, there would be very little to teach.

But no one had taught Samuel anything. Samuel had not been persuaded or instructed or healed. Something had been removed from him. Something that had been there at dinner, waving and grinning and pressing its hand to the glass, and was not there now.

This isn't therapy, Marcus thought, and the thought arrived fully formed, like a fact verified and entered, immutable.

This is erasure.

And the worst of it, the thing that kept him staring at the ceiling until the light came, was that he still believed in Veridica. He believed in the factorepo and The Judge and the absolute, sacred order of facts. He believed in the system that had sent him here. He could not stop believing in it even now.

He did not know what they would do to a man who already believed.

He was very much afraid he was about to find out.
