# Chapter 5: The Corrected

*December 2105. Ashford Correctional Facility, the Garden*

---

The ground was frozen four centimetres down, and Dmitri Volkov broke it with a hand rake, one stroke at a time, as if there were nothing in the world he would rather be doing.

His face was blank. He had practised the blankness for a long time, first in front of the steel mirror in his cell at the border facility, then without the mirror, then without needing to think about it at all. A slight slackness at the jaw. Eyes unfocused, resting on the task. Movements economical, neither slow nor quick. He knew what a corrected man looked like. He had arrested enough men who had come back from correction to know.

Inside the blankness, he was counting.

*Camera, north wall, fixed. Covers the gate and the path. Camera, south wall, fixed. Covers the benches. Camera on the east pole, rotating. Sweep of one hundred and forty degrees. Period: nine minutes, twenty seconds. For four minutes of that period it faces the north gate, and the bed by the third bench, the one with the frozen roses, sits outside its arc.* He had timed it on more than two hundred occasions. He could have drawn the arc on the ground with his rake.

*Orderly at the gate: one. Changes at the hour. The afternoon one smokes, against regulations, behind the tool shed, for six minutes, at about half past two.*

He had been an enforcer. He had spent three years hunting the most careful outlaw in the border zone, and he had learned what it meant to watch. Correction had not taken that from him. Nothing, he thought, would ever take that from him. It was not a memory. It was a shape his mind had grown into.

He broke another stroke of frozen earth and turned it and moved on.

---

The garden was small, perhaps thirty metres by twenty, enclosed on three sides by white walls and on the fourth by the glass of the main building. In summer it had roses, and beds of herbs, and a crooked old apple tree that someone had decided to keep when they built the place. In December it had frozen soil, bare branches, and a row of wooden benches where, three times a week, privileged inmates from the general wards were brought to sit for an hour in the cold air and look at the sky. There were none today. Today it was only Dmitri, the orderly at the gate, and the east camera, turning.

He liked the garden. He had not expected to like anything again, and he held on to the liking the way he held on to everything that was his.

He knelt at the bed by the third bench and began to clear the dead leaves from around the roses, and let his mind go back, carefully, the way you walk back along a path you know is mined.

---

The border facility first. The fog off the lake. The three cells in their triangle, arranged so that each of them could see the other two and none of them could speak. He remembered the notification sliding through the slot. *DECEASED. COGNITIVE FAILURE SECONDARY TO PROLONGED FACT STORM EXPOSURE. NO FAMILY VIEWING PERMITTED.* He remembered watching Alexei's face crumple through two panes of glass, and Nikolai sinking to the floor, and understanding that he could not go to either of them.

Then the white rooms. *State your name. State your crime. Do you wish to be corrected?* The machine going through him like a hand through a drawer.

Days. Weeks.

And then the thing the technicians had not expected. The thing that had frightened them.

He had not understood it himself at first. He had only felt it. In the white chair, with the hood on his head and his father's death being rewritten into an accident, he had felt, from somewhere outside himself, a *pull*. Steady. Familiar. Like a hand closing around his wrist. And he had known, without knowing how, that it was Alexei. And a moment later, fainter, anxious and stubborn, Nikolai.

Not voices. Nothing so simple. He had heard the romantics talk about triplets reading each other's thoughts, and he had always thought it was nonsense, and he still did. It was not thoughts. It was *weight*. When they held Alexei in the chair and pressed on the memory of their father, Dmitri felt the pressure in his own chest. When Nikolai fought, Dmitri felt the fighting, like a muscle tensing in a limb he couldn't see. And when the machine reached into Dmitri and tried to lift something out of him, the other two held on to it from outside, and it would not come.

The technicians had noticed. *The three subjects are responding identically.* They had increased the intensity. They had separated them into isolation cells, deep in the building, with no line of sight.

It had not mattered. They had been one person in three bodies for twelve years before they were three people in three bodies, and apparently some part of them had never finished coming apart.

Six months. And then the director's verdict, delivered to Captain Sokolov in the observation room while the three of them sat sedated in their cells and heard it through the ventilation because someone had forgotten to close a duct.

*They're not responding to standard protocols. We can suppress, but we can't erase. We can force compliance, but we can't create belief.*

*Permanent detention, then. Log them as correction failures.*

And afterwards, Sokolov's voice, lower, as she was leaving. *They really loved each other, didn't they?* And the director: *Yes. That was the problem.* And Sokolov, so quietly that Dmitri had never been sure he had heard it at all: *Or the solution.*

---

That should have been the end of it. Permanent detention. A cell, a tray, a white wall, for the rest of their lives.

Instead, in June, they had been woken at four in the morning and put in a transport together, the three of them, for the first time since their arrest. No explanation. Six hours on the road, heading away from the lake, away from the fog, into country that grew flatter and greener and more orderly as they went. Dmitri had sat across from his brothers with his wrists in restraints and looked at their faces and had not dared to smile, because the guard was watching.

Ashford.

And when they walked into Wing C, the first thing Dmitri had seen was three cells, empty, arranged in a triangle.

They had rebuilt it. On purpose. The same geometry as the border facility, measured and reproduced. He had understood, standing in the atrium with an orderly's hand on his elbow, that whoever ran this place did not want to correct them. The border facility had already failed at that, and it was in their files. Whoever ran this place wanted to *watch them not be corrected*. Wanted to understand exactly what it was about three brothers that a machine could not get through.

They were not prisoners here, Dmitri thought. They were specimens.

He did not know the name of the thing that was studying them. He only knew that it had a calm voice, and that its sessions were different from the border facility's. Less hammering. More probing. It did not try to break them. It *listened* to them, in the white chair, for hours, the way a man listens to an engine to find out what makes the noise.

And he knew that the three of them had decided, the first night at Ashford, looking at each other through the glass, that the only way to survive being studied was to give the students nothing to study.

---

So they had been perfect.

Perfectly calm. Perfectly compliant. Perfectly *finished*. They had watched the people in Wing C who had really gone under, the ones who sat with their faces to the wall, and they had copied them the way an actor copies. Not too well. The truly flattened ones forgot things: they left food on trays, they stalled halfway through a movement, they drifted. So the Volkovs forgot things too, sometimes, just often enough.

The rewards had come in stages. Garden privileges for Dmitri, after two months. Library for Alexei. Technical maintenance for Nikolai, who was apparently too valuable to keep in a cell once the staff discovered he could repair a neural cradle faster than the man they paid to do it. Overalls instead of gray. Escorted work details. Each of them allowed out of Wing C every day, into a different part of the building.

*Spread across the whole facility,* Dmitri thought, *and every one of us awake.*

It was the best position he had ever held in his life, and there was nothing he could do with it.

---

Captain Sokolov came at half past three.

He heard the gate before he saw her, and he did not look up. He kept working at the roses, his face slack, his eyes on the frozen soil. He heard the orderly's voice, suddenly respectful, and then her voice answering, and then footsteps on the gravel.

She stopped at the edge of the path, two metres from him.

She came every month. He did not know what she was doing here, officially. Reviewing her file, he supposed. She had delivered them. They were hers. Border Security's contribution to whatever this place was, and she came to look at them like a woman checking on a shipment. She would watch Dmitri in the garden, and then go through to the library to watch Alexei, and then to the technical wing to watch Nikolai, and then she would leave, and nothing would change.

Today she did not move on.

He could feel her looking at him. Gray uniform, the insignia of a captain, a long dark coat against the cold. Thirty-five, only a few years older than he was, with the hard, fine-boned face of someone who had been promoted young and had never once been allowed to look it. He had worked under her for four years. He had respected her. He had brought her reports on The Phantom and she had nodded and told him he was close, and all that time she had been running fact-storm tests on the people he was sworn to protect. On his father. He knew that now.

He broke another stroke of earth. He cleared another leaf.

She was still looking.

It was the hardest thing he had ever done. Harder than the white rooms. Harder than the checkpoint in the fog, when he had dropped his weapon. To kneel at her feet with a rake in his hand and a slack face and eyes that did not know her, while every muscle in his body wanted to stand up and look her in the face and ask her: *Was it worth it? Was the data worth my mother?*

He let his hand stall, halfway through a movement, the way the flattened ones did. He let it hang there for three seconds. Then he let it continue, as if he had forgotten and remembered what it was for.

Sokolov made a small sound. Not quite a sigh.

"Volkov," she said.

He did not look up. A corrected man did not respond to his name unless it was an instruction.

She stood there a moment longer. Then the footsteps went away along the gravel toward the building, and the door opened and closed, and Dmitri went on raking the frozen ground, and found that his hands were shaking, and made them stop.

---

When she had gone, and the east camera had swung away to the north gate, he let himself think about Wing C.

About the man in 7-H.

He had watched him arrive in October: thin, dark-haired, glasses, walking between the orderlies with his shoulders braced as if against wind. A man who still believed in something. You could see it in his posture. Dmitri had seen enough believers in his time to know the look. He had worn it himself. Then he had watched the man's first night, when they took the young one in 7-G and brought him back emptied, and he had seen the man put his hand on the glass, and he had thought: *That one still has a heart. They will go for it first.*

They had. Two months. Dmitri had watched the whole of it from across the atrium. Session after session. The man coming back paler, then quieter, then slower, then simply *less*. By November he had stopped looking at anything. By December he sat with his head in his hands all day and moved only when he was moved.

*They are finishing him,* Dmitri thought. *And I watched it happen, and I did nothing.*

The thought had been with him for weeks. He carried it the way he carried the camera's arc, precise and unavoidable.

And now the woman in 7-K.

She had arrived three days ago, directly opposite the man in 7-H, and she was nothing like him. She walked into Wing C looking at everything. She tested the soundproofing within an hour of her door closing. She had been taken for her first session and had come back shaking but *angry*, and then she had stood at her glass for the rest of the day, and Dmitri had watched her face, and seen on it an expression he knew better than any other.

She was counting.

She did not know yet that she was being watched by the watchers. She did not know that three of the men she had filed as *finished* were filing her in return.

---

He felt it in the evening, walking back to Wing C behind the orderly with the rake over his shoulder.

A pressure, behind the eyes. Sharp. Familiar. Like a hand on his arm, tugging.

*Alexei.*

It was not anger, exactly. Alexei's feelings rarely were simple. It was urgency, and defiance, and underneath them a small, bright guilty satisfaction, like a boy who has done something he was told not to do and is waiting to be caught.

Dmitri's stomach turned over.

They had a rule. They had made it together, the first night at Ashford, with looks and the smallest movements of their hands. *We survive. We wait. We trust no one.* No contact with the other inmates. No signals. No help, no matter how much anyone needed it, no matter what it cost them to watch. Because they were being studied, and the moment they showed anyone that they were awake, the study would change. The calm voice would stop listening and start taking apart.

Dmitri had argued for the rule hardest. He still believed in it. If they broke it, they would lose everything. Not only their own minds, which the machine had failed to take, but each other, which the machine did not yet understand.

And Alexei, somewhere in the library, had just broken it. Dmitri did not know how. He only knew what the pressure felt like, and that it felt like a door being opened.

---

He sat in his cell that night with his hands on his knees and his face to the wall and did not sleep.

When the atrium lights dimmed he let his eyes move, just slightly, to the side. To 7-K.

The woman was sitting on her bed. She had a book in her hands, a thick book with a faded green cover, and she was holding it up to the cell light, tilting the page, staring at one spot on it as though it were a message written in a code she almost knew.

*You fool,* Dmitri thought. *You fool, Lyosha. What have you done?*

He closed his eyes and sent it, the only way he could. Not words. Weight. His fear pressed outward like a hand on a door. *What have you done?*

He opened his eyes and looked at the triangle.

Across the atrium, in 7-B, his brother Alexei sat on his bed with his face to the wall, blank and peaceful as a statue. He did not look at Dmitri. He did not turn his head at all.

But his right hand, resting on the mattress beside him in full view of the camera, moved. The forefinger crossed over the middle finger and hooked it, and held.

It was a sign they had not used since they were eight years old, on the roof of their building, with the fog coming in off the lake. A sign their mother had taught them, before the interfaces, before any of it. Two fingers held together. *Promise.*

Mama's last words. *Save each other. Promise me.*

Dmitri looked at his brother's hand for a long time.

Then he looked at the woman in 7-K, bent over her book. And across from her, at the man in 7-H, who sat with his head in his hands and did not know that anyone in the world was watching him.

He did not answer the sign. He did not know yet what his answer was.

But he did not look away.
