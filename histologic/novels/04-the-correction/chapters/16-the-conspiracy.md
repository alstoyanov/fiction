# Chapter 16: The Conspiracy

*April 17–30, 2106. Wing C and the Garden*

---

For three days after the primer, Marcus did not know what he felt about Elena, and then he realised that he felt all of it, and that this was allowed.

He sat on his bed with his head in his hands in the shape of a broken man and behind his hands he turned it over. Elena. Elena Reyes, who had asked him for directions she didn't need. Elena Vasquez, the enforcers' name for her. Elena Volkov. Ivan Volkov's sister. The aunt of three men who sat in a triangle across the atrium, who had her sharp cheekbones, he saw now, and the same way of going still when they were listening. The woman who had seduced him and lied to him and warned him and let him report her, and sat on her couch waiting for the enforcers with her hands in her lap, because she was tired, because she had recruited seven people and did not know whether she had saved them or destroyed them.

*My brother questioned a minor discrepancy.*

She had been talking about the triplets' father. On that last night, on the couch, with her voice shaking, she had been telling him about a man who would die a year later as Test Subject 2471. And Marcus had told her that her brother was sick. That doubt was a mental illness. That correction was therapeutic.

He had said that. He remembered saying it. He had the memory back now, whole, and it came with his own voice in it, steady and certain and completely wrong.

He was angry with her. He had not known he still could be. She had lied to him for eight weeks and used his loneliness like a lever.

He was grateful to her. She had tried to warn him. *When they come for you too, remember this conversation.* He had not remembered. They had taken it. But she had tried.

And he was afraid of her, because she was coming, and he did not know what he would say.

Three things at once. Angry, grateful, afraid. A few months ago he would have thought that meant he was malfunctioning. He had been learning, on a bench in the cold, from a woman who had loved a liar, that it might only mean he was a person.

---

In the garden, on the first Monday, in the four-minute gap, the gardener knelt at the bed beside his bench.

"From my brother Alexei," Dmitri said, without looking up, clearing leaves. "He asked me to tell you. He says: she recruited seven people and never knew whether she had saved them." A pause. "She is coming for eight."

Marcus looked at the roses. They had begun, in the last week, to put out small tight buds, dark red at the tips.

"Are you angry with her?" Dmitri asked. It was the first time the gardener had ever asked him anything.

"Yes."

"Good," said Dmitri. "So are we. Fifteen years." He moved along the stem. "We will be angry with her together, afterwards. When we are out."

---

Nikolai's files came in pieces.

There was too much to send at once. It came in schoolbooks and primers, in fragments, each one with the new key, each one decoded in seven cells by seven people bent over their knees as if doing sums. Isaiah put them in order. His prefixes and sequence numbers ran down the margins of every page, so that by the end of the second week Marcus could hold in his head, if he held very hard, the shape of the thing that had been done to them.

*PROJECT CONTINUITY. PHASE ONE: HUMAN SERIES.*

*Programme aim: to produce stable, genuine belief, as distinct from compliance, across all tested cognitive profiles.*

*Success criterion: subject not merely behaves as a believer but experiences belief as authentic and self-originated.*

He read that line many times. *Experiences belief as authentic and self-originated.* It was not enough, for Continuity, to make a man say the right words. It was not even enough to make him believe them. He had to believe that the belief was *his own*. That no one had put it there.

Isaiah's note under the fragment, in his careful pricks: *IO-31. THEY ARE NOT TRYING TO MAKE PRISONERS OBEY. THEY ARE TRYING TO MAKE PEOPLE MEAN IT.*

---

The seven types came in the third fragment. He had heard them from Dmitri in the garden, and from Nikolai's story, but seeing them in the flat administrative language of the file made his skin go cold.

*C-1 — BELIEVER.*

*C-2 — ARCHITECT.*

*C-3, C-4, C-5 — BOND.*

*C-6 — CLINICIAN.*

*C-7 — WITNESS.*

"If it works on a believer," Isaiah breathed in the garden that afternoon, face to the sky, "on a builder of the system, on a bond it couldn't break, on a doctor who knows the machine, and on a man trained to remember, then it works on anyone. That's the point. That's what we are. We're the *proof*."

"Proof of what?" Kira said.

The answer was in the fifth fragment. It was a single paragraph from a section of the aims document headed *Forward Planning*. Nikolai had copied it, he wrote, almost by accident, in the last minute before he backed out of the directory.

*PHASE TWO (subject to Phase One outcome and Ministry approval): adaptation of the Continuity method for population-scale delivery via the national neural-interface broadcast network. Pilot regions to be identified. Correctional context to be retained for Phase One only.*

Marcus read it on his bed with his back to the camera, and then he read it again, and then he closed the book and sat very still.

*The national neural-interface broadcast network.*

Every citizen of Veridica had an interface. Every interface received the broadcasts. He had received them himself every day of his life since he was eight years old: the gentle, constant stream of facts about every building he passed, every street, every date. *This structure built 2067.* It was how the factorepo reached into every head. It was how he had simply *known* the date of the Founding, on the morning after his enlightenment day, without anyone having to tell him.

And Continuity wanted to use it.

Not to tell people facts. To give them beliefs. To reach into every head in the nation and do, gently, invisibly, what the white chair had done to him. And the people who received it would not feel it happen. They would not sit in a white room with a hood on their heads. They would walk to work, and past the Spire, and home again, and at some point, without noticing, they would stop doubting. And they would believe, with their whole hearts, that the believing was their own.

*Not a prison technique,* Kira's note said, under his, the next morning. *A population technique. This is the pilot. We are the pilot.*

---

The fragment about Kira came in the fourth week of April, and Marcus watched her read it.

He could see her from his bed, across the atrium, through the gap between his fingers. She sat on the end of her bed with a primer on her knees and her head bent over it. He saw her read the line. He saw her go still. He saw her read it again.

*C-2. Status: in progress. Resistant (technical deflection). Acquired via record anomaly (ref. R.). Placement 7-K per protocol R-2.*

She did not move for a long time.

Then she stood up, and walked to her glass, and stood there with her arms folded, looking at nothing, and Marcus saw on her face an expression he had never seen on it before. Not the fury of her first session. That had been hot. This was cold. Cold and absolutely still, like deep water under ice.

In the garden the next day she explained it, in the four-minute gap, in a voice so level it frightened him.

"*Acquired.* That's the word. Not arrested. Not convicted. *Acquired.* Like equipment." She was looking at the sky. "And *via record anomaly*. My fact. The one I entered on November eighteenth. It didn't vanish. It didn't corrupt. They *took* it. They went into the factorepo, which is supposed to be impossible, and removed a verified entry, so that I would report it missing, so that I would be suspected, so that The Judge would convict me. So that they could *acquire* an architect." She breathed out. "They chose me. And then they made me into a criminal so they could have me."

"Who can do that?" Tanaka asked quietly. "Delete a fact?"

"No one," Kira said. "No one can. I built the verification layer. I would have told you last year that it was impossible." She was silent for a moment. "Someone can. Someone inside. And I don't know who. And I'm going to find out."

Marcus said nothing. He could not think of anything to say that was equal to her face.

"What's R-2?" Isaiah asked.

"I don't know," Kira said. "Nikolai couldn't open it. It's in Reeves's own directory. *Placement per protocol R-2.* A placement protocol." She frowned at the sky. "Probably a technical specification. Sightlines, maybe, for studying Architect-type subjects under observation. Something about the cell layout." She shook her head. "It doesn't matter. The fact matters."

Marcus looked at the white stones along the edge of the bed, and did not think about R-2 again.

---

The fragment about him came last.

Nikolai had held it back. Marcus understood that later. The others had seen it in the first week, and they had talked about it, and they had agreed that he should read it when the rest was already in him, so that it would have somewhere to land.

It came in an arithmetic primer on the evening of April twenty-eighth. Kira had pricked a single line on the first page, before the message itself, in her careful small holes.

*K. READ THIS SLOWLY. I'M HERE.*

He read it slowly.

*C-1. Status: SUCCESS. Reduction to baseline achieved 04 Dec 2105. Stable. Reserved for demonstration. Maintenance protocol only; no further intervention without authorisation (R.).*

*Acquisition: contamination index 0.58 → 0.73 (adjusted; threshold compliance).*

He read it three times.

He was a historian. He was a Junior Historian of the Regional Factorepo, and for three years he had spent his days looking at columns of figures and knowing, at a glance, what they meant. He knew what a contamination index was. He knew that it was expressed as a decimal and reported to the subject as a percentage. He knew what the threshold was. He had heard Kade say it, in the white interview room, with the camera in the corner.

*In Veridica, anything over sixty percent requires corrective intervention.*

*Point five eight.*

Fifty-eight percent.

Below sixty.

He sat on his bed and looked at the two numbers and the small printed arrow between them, and the word in brackets. *Adjusted.*

*The Judge's analysis shows a seventy-three percent probability that you've been contaminated.*

No. It hadn't. The Judge's analysis had shown fifty-eight. The Judge had looked at one hundred and twenty-seven hours of conversation and three visits to an apartment and eight weeks of a trained recruiter, and calculated, honestly, by its own lights, that Marcus Chen was fifty-eight percent likely to have been contaminated. Which was below the threshold. Which meant no corrective intervention. Which meant a note in his record, and perhaps a month of monitoring, and an interview, and then his life.

*He would have gone home.*

He would have gone home to his apartment, and his desk on the fourteenth floor, and Thomas saying *another day in paradise*. He would have grieved for Elena and hated her and got over it, slowly, the way people do. He would have gone on entering facts. He would have gone on believing.

And someone had looked at the fifty-eight, and needed a believer, and *typed* a seventy-three.

---

It was strange what he did not feel.

He had expected to break. He had braced for it, reading, the way you brace for a blow you can see coming. The last solid thing in him, the thing that had survived even the white chair in its strange, drained form, was this: *the facts are the facts*. The system had been cruel to him, but it had been *honest*. The Judge had not wanted him punished. The Judge had simply calculated, and the calculation had been correct, and the system had done what the calculation required. He had held on to that in the Enforcement Center, and on the transport, and in the first weeks here. It had been the floor beneath the floor.

And it was gone. Someone had changed a fact. Not some outlaw, not some doubter, not Elena. The system itself, the people inside it, the people who ran the machine that was supposed to guarantee that facts could not be changed. They had reached into the record at the exact point where it decided his life, and falsified it, and sent him here.

He did not break.

He sat on his bed, with his head bowed and his hands loose between his knees, and found that what he felt was not grief and not despair. It was something he had not felt in so long that he did not recognise it at first.

It was anger. Not hot. Not the shouting anger of the interview room. Clean, cold, and very clear, like the air in the garden.

*Someone changed a fact.*

It was a historian's anger. It was the anger of a man who had spent three years being careful, being honest, rehearsing every entry before he went home so that he would never put a false thing into the record. It was the anger of a man who had believed the record was sacred, and who still, underneath everything, *did*. That was what made it so cold. He was not angry because the record mattered less than he had thought. He was angry because it mattered exactly as much, and someone had lied in it.

It was the first emotion he had felt since October that was entirely, unmistakably his own. The machine had not given it to him. Kira had not given it to him. It had grown in him out of nothing but a number and an arrow, the way a fact grows out of evidence.

He held it, and it did not go away.

---

*I was their first success.*

He saw it now, whole. He had not been sent to Ashford because The Judge found him contaminated. He had been sent because Continuity needed a believer, and he was the best one they could find: a man who had done everything right, who would walk into the white chair still loving the system that put him there. They had needed to know whether they could take that love out of a man and leave him empty. And they had. December fourth. *Reduction to baseline achieved.* He was the proof, and they were keeping him, stable and beautiful, for August, to show to the Ministry.

*Look. Here is a man who believed. And here he is now.*

If Kira had not come. If she had not walked past him at a junction in a white corridor and looked at him as if he were still there. If she had not come out of the white chair angry, and stood at her glass, and looked across at him. If she had not said *if you entered it, it happened*.

He would still be sitting on his bed with his face to the wall. He would be Samuel.

He owed her what was left of him. All of it. There was no other way to count it.

---

He thought about the others, too, that night, lying awake in the blue light.

The triplets had survived because there were three of them. Kira had survived because she had built herself a vault out of specifications and put her heart in it. Isaiah had his house. Tanaka had her clipboard. And Marcus had survived, in the end, because of Kira.

Connection. Every one of them had held because of something that tied them to something else. The machine went looking for the heat in a mind, and the heat was always, in the end, other people. And if you had other people, if the heat was spread out between you and someone else, *held from outside*, the machine could not quite get its hands around it.

*Connection is the counter-technology,* he thought, and it arrived in him in the formal cadence of a fact entry, and he let it.

---

In the white chair that week, the voice behind the mirror sounded, for the first time since December, slightly less bored.

"Hm," it said, in the middle of the maintenance session. Marcus lay under the hood and recited fact entries. *Traffic incident, corner of Fifth and Main.* "Your stability readings are showing a little micro-drift, Mr. Chen. Nothing much. Nothing to worry about. Probably seasonal." A pause. The sound of keys. "We'll keep an eye on it."

*Property dispute, Linden Avenue. Birth, Central Hospital. Personnel entry: Osman, K. Location: Wing C, 7-K. Status:*

He did not finish the line.

He filled his mind with facts, and folded Kira into the formats, and held.

The hood lifted. Dr. Moss squeezed his hand and told him he was healing. He let his face do nothing at all.

---

On the last Friday in April, in the garden, when the east camera swung away toward the north gate, Marcus spoke first.

He had never spoken first before. In three months of four-minute gaps, it had always been Kira, or Isaiah, or Tanaka, and he had listened, and held on to what he could.

He said it to the sky, aloud, quietly, in a voice that surprised him with how steady it was.

"We're lab rats," he said. "That's what we are. They picked us, one of each kind, and put us in a box to see what would happen."

Nobody answered. Down the row of benches he felt Isaiah turn his head a fraction.

"But some of us," Marcus said, "have already proved that we can't be broken." He looked, very briefly, at the gardener kneeling by the north bed, raking. "And the rest of us are learning."

At the other end of his bench, Kira did not look at him. She did not move her face at all. But under the bench, below the level of the wooden slats, in the shadow where no camera had ever been angled to look, her hand found his.

It was cold from the air. He held it.

The camera ticked slowly round toward them. He did not let go until the last second.

Four months until August.
