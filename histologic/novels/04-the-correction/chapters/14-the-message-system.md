# Chapter 14: The Message System

*March 2106. Ashford Correctional Facility*

---

For ten years, before they caught him, Alexei Volkov had run the most elusive smuggling network in the border zone, and he had never once, in all that time, had as much fun as he was having now.

He could not show it. That was part of the fun. He stood at the returns cart in the library with his face as smooth and empty as a pond, stamping cards and shelving books, and inside, The Phantom was humming.

---

The first thing a network needs is a channel, and Ashford had given them one without knowing it: books.

Books went into Wing C on the meal trolley, on request. Books came back out on the trolley when the inmate had finished. Nobody read them in between. Nobody would ever read them. They were library books, stamped and catalogued and so dull that the orderlies who pushed the trolleys through the atrium did not even look at the titles. A library book in a Wing C cell was a piece of furniture.

So they became letters.

He started with pins. There was a cork board by the library desk with notices on it, and the notices were held up by pins, and the pins were replaced every week from a box in the desk drawer that nobody counted. He took six. He slipped one into the spine of each book he sent into Wing C, under the loose flap of the binding where a finger could find it, so that the person who received the book could send an answer back.

Then the code.

He kept it simple, at first, because simple was fast and because he had no way yet of teaching anyone anything complicated. Pinpricks under letters. The first letter of the message on the page whose number you started from, and then each letter shifted along the alphabet by that page number: page nine, shift nine; page forty-seven, shift forty-seven, which came round to twenty-one. An old smuggler's trick, older than the Founding. He had used it on the backs of cigarette packets in the border zone to tell a frightened family which night the crossing would be. Anyone could learn it in ten minutes. And anyone who had not been told there was a message would never, ever, think to hold the page up to the light.

The choice of book was a message too. He sent the list into every cell in the first week, pricked into a pamphlet on dental hygiene:

*Arithmetic: there is a message inside. Read it.*

*Poetry: no news. Hold.*

*Anything about water: danger. Say nothing. Wait.*

He had chosen that last one for Kira Osman, and for page forty-seven, and he was privately very pleased with it.

---

The second thing a network needs is routes.

That was Nikolai. The trolleys ran on a schedule, and the schedule was set by the facilities office, and the facilities office took its maintenance windows from the technical wing, and the technical wing, since August, had been Nikolai. It took him a week. By the end of it, without a single change to any official timetable that anyone would ever notice, the afternoon trolley passed 7-H and 7-K within ninety seconds of each other, the evening trolley passed 7-D and 7-E at the moment the orderly on the atrium desk went on her break, and every book Alexei sent from the library reached Old Songs at the trolley bay at quarter past five, when the kitchen was busiest and nobody was watching the bay at all.

"Easy," Nikolai breathed to him in the corridor one morning, and there was something in his voice Alexei had not heard since they were twelve. Something that sounded almost like pride. "It's easy. They built a machine to watch everything and they forgot to watch the timetable."

---

The third thing is placement. That was Dima.

The garden had six benches and thirty metres of flowerbeds, and Dmitri worked those beds every day. He began to arrange the stones.

Nothing anyone would notice. The edges of the beds were lined with smooth gray stones from the lake shore, placed there years ago by some long-gone gardener, and they shifted in the frost and had to be reset. So Dmitri reset them. And the stones along the bed by the third bench, the one nearest where Marcus Chen sat, became a clock. One stone set a little apart from the others at the left end meant *two o'clock*. At the right end, *three*. A white stone among the gray, anywhere in the row, meant *danger*. The small cairn of three at the corner of the north bed, which Dima built and knocked down and built again as if he could not quite get it right, meant *speak today*.

"You are enjoying this," Alexei whispered to him in the corridor.

Dmitri's face did not change. "I am an enforcer," he said. "I have spent my life setting up surveillance. It is interesting to set it up for once on the right side."

---

And the fourth thing a network needs, the one people always forget, is a signal that cannot be written down.

That was Old Songs.

He had agreed to it the way he agreed to everything, without words. Alexei had stood at the trolley bay one evening and asked him, in a whisper, and the old man had looked at him with those sharp pale eyes and gone on stacking bowls. But the next morning, when the trolley came through the library, his humming had a new shape. A rising phrase, bright, like a question answered. And later, a falling one, low and slow.

*Yes. No.*

And silence, when he passed a cell. Silence from Old Songs, who had hummed for longer than anyone in Ashford could remember, was the loudest sound in the building. It meant *they're watching. Not now.*

---

Kira Osman cracked the code in a day.

Alexei had expected her to be fast. He had not expected her to be *that* fast. He sent the dental hygiene pamphlet into 7-K on a Monday afternoon with the instructions pricked into it and a pin in the spine, and on Tuesday evening a book came back out on the trolley. *Principles of Factorepo Verification (Third Edition)*. She had asked for it herself on a request slip, the minx, knowing it would be sent from the library and come back to the library, knowing whose hands it would pass through.

He opened it at the returns cart with his heart going like a drum.

Page twelve. Pinpricks.

He decoded it standing at the cart, stamping cards with his left hand, reading with his eyes, doing the shifts in his head.

*KNOWN SENDER OF P47 NOW. THANK YOU. YOUR KEY IS WEAK. PAGE NUMBER VISIBLE ON EVERY PAGE. ANY SHORT MESSAGE BREAKABLE BY LETTER COUNT IN TEN MINUTES. PROPOSE SHIFT EQUALS PAGE PLUS DAY OF MONTH. SAME PAGE NEVER SAME KEY TWICE. ALSO STOP USING E FOR FILLER. IT IS THE MOST COMMON LETTER. K.*

He laughed.

It came up out of him before he could stop it, one short, bright, helpless bark of a laugh, in the silent library, in front of the desk, in front of the orderly and the bald accountant and the girl with the poetry book. Every head turned.

He turned it into a cough. A long, hacking, apologetic cough, bent over the cart with his hand over his mouth, until his eyes ran. The orderly at the desk frowned at him. The accountant went back to his manual.

*Oh,* Alexei thought, wiping his eyes, his face going blank again. *Oh, she's wonderful.*

He sent back a volume of approved poetry that evening. *No news. Hold.* But on the inside of the back cover, where she would find it if she thought to look, and she would, he pricked four letters with the new key.

*YES MAAM.*

---

Tanaka came next, three days later. She took the pamphlet and returned it in two days with the instructions decoded and, pricked under the words of a paragraph about gum disease, a short, precise note on the safest page ranges to use given the density of the paper, since pricks on thinner pages showed more when held to the light.

Isaiah Okonkwo took four days and then turned the whole thing into a filing system. Alexei had never seen anything like it. Every message that went through his hands came back with a two-letter prefix, a sequence number and a date, so that nothing could ever be lost or duplicated or misread out of order. *IO-7, 14 MARCH. RE: DO-3 (GARDEN STONES). CONFIRM WHITE STONE MEANS DANGER NOT WAIT. SUGGEST CAIRN BE THREE STONES ONLY; FOUR IS AMBIGUOUS.*

"He is running a newspaper," Dmitri muttered in the corridor.

"He's running an *archive*," Alexei said. "It's the most beautiful thing I've ever seen."

---

Marcus Chen was slowest.

Alexei had known he would be. He had seen, across the atrium, what it cost the man to hold a thought for more than a minute or two, the way his face went slack in the middle of a sentence and then came back, as if he had stepped out of the room and returned. The code was simple, but it needed him to hold a number in his head while he counted along an alphabet, and hold the letters he had already found while he looked for the next one, and he kept losing the thread.

Kira taught him.

In the garden, in the four-minute gaps, bench to bench, Alexei heard later from Dima. She did it with the patience of an engineer walking a junior through a system. She did not give him the whole thing at once. She gave him one letter at a time. *Page nine, day four. Thirteen. Count thirteen from the letter you see.* And when he lost his place she did not sigh; she simply said *again*, and he started again.

It took him eleven days to decode his first full message by himself.

Alexei knew the day it happened, because the book that came back from 7-H that evening, an arithmetic primer, had a single line pricked into it under the answers to a set of long-division problems. The pricks were clumsy, too large, uneven, the work of a man whose hands had forgotten small things.

*I READ IT MYSELF.*

Alexei stood at the returns cart with the primer open in his hands and could not, for a moment, see the page.

---

Seven of them now. Every cell in the list. All of them talking.

It was the first time, Alexei thought, that seven people in Wing C had been in the same conversation since the wing was built. Maybe the first time in the whole of Ashford. The building had been designed, glass wall by soundproofed wall, to make sure it could never happen. And here it was happening, in pinpricks and garden stones and an old man's humming, under the nose of every camera in the place.

And now that they could talk, the brothers began to teach.

---

They did it in fragments, over weeks, in pieces small enough to pass through the pages of a primer. Alexei wrote most of it. Nikolai checked the technical parts, and Dmitri added what he had learned about looking flattened, and Tanaka, when she read the first fragment, sent back corrections so sharp and so clinically exact that Alexei rewrote the whole of the second part to include them.

*How it works.* The interface in the white chair follows feeling. Salience. It goes where the heat is, and from there it follows the links outward: from a person to a place, from a place to a day, from a day to everything you felt on it. It is very good at heat. It is very bad at everything else.

*The blind spot.* Rote things. Rhythmic things. Procedures. Things you have done so many times they no longer mean anything: songs, tables, prayers, timetables, forms. It skims across them. It thinks they are furniture.

*So hide what you love in what you've memorised.* That was Old Songs' sentence, and Alexei wrote it out whole at the top of every fragment, and did not sign it. It was not his.

*Everyone builds a vault.* Find the most boring thing you know by heart. Put the things you cannot lose inside it. Not near it. Inside it. Tie each memory to a line, a step, a number, until you cannot recite the line without the memory coming too. And in the white chair, recite. Give it the vault's walls, and let it skim.

Kira already had hers: the Spire specifications. Isaiah had his: the house on Carver Street, room by room. Tanaka's, she wrote back, would be the surgical checklist, the forty-one steps she had recited before every procedure for twenty years, and she had already begun. And Marcus Chen, who had fooled the machine once already with the fact-entry formats of the Regional Factorepo, sent back three words that made Alexei put the primer down on the cart and stare at the far wall for a long moment.

*KIRA IN FORMATS.*

*How to look flattened.* That part was Dmitri's, and it was the most detailed, because it was the thing the brothers had done every day for nine months. A lag before the eyes move. A pause before the answer, even to a simple question. Forget a small task once a day: leave a cup unwashed, stop halfway through standing. Do not stall *correctly*. Stall at random. Stall when no one is watching, too, sometimes, in case they are. Tanaka added a note under that one: *The Volkovs' stalls have a rhythm. I noticed it in September. Others will. Vary it.*

Dmitri, reading it, went silent in the bond for a whole evening.

*One rule above all,* Alexei wrote at the end. *Never all look up at once.*

---

It nearly ended in the third week.

Alexei was not there. He heard about it afterwards, from Nikolai, who had seen it from across the atrium and had been unable to stop shaking for an hour.

The evening trolley had been on its round. An arithmetic primer had gone into 7-E with a long message pricked into it, Isaiah's reply to Tanaka about the garden stones. And the orderly on the atrium desk, a new one, young and bored and in no hurry to go on his break, had wandered over to the trolley as Old Songs pushed it past the desk, and picked the primer up off the tray.

He flicked through it. Idly. The way a bored man flicks through anything within reach.

Page one. Page two. Page three.

He stopped. He turned the book toward the light of the desk lamp.

And Old Songs, beside him, dropped the soup.

The whole pot. Four litres of it, steaming, straight off the trolley and onto the floor of the atrium, splashing up the orderly's trousers and over his shoes and across half a metre of pale composite. The old man stood there with his hands in the air and his mouth open and an expression of such helpless, trembling, senile distress on his face that Nikolai, watching, had almost believed it himself.

The orderly swore. He jumped back. He dropped the primer on the tray and stamped his feet and swore again, at length and with feeling, at the old fool and his soup and his trolley and the whole of the night shift, and went off to find a mop.

By the time he came back, the primer was in 7-E, and Old Songs was on his knees on the floor with a cloth, humming.

Nobody ever mentioned it again. The orderly asked for a transfer to the day shift a week later, and got it, and was never seen in Wing C again. And the next morning, when the trolley came through the library, Old Songs' humming was a little louder than usual, and a little brighter, and it rose at the end like a question answered.

Alexei, stamping cards at the desk, did not look up.

*Brave,* he thought. *You brave, mad old man.*

---

That evening, at the returns cart, he held a book in his hands and did not put it down.

It was an arithmetic primer, *Arithmetic for Young Citizens, Book Two*, the one that had gone into 7-H with *WELCOME* in it. It had been round the whole wing since then. He knew because he had sent it round. It had gone from Marcus to Kira, and from Kira to Tanaka, and from Tanaka to Isaiah, and from Isaiah, pricked with a filing prefix, back to the library, and out again to Nikolai in the technical wing, who had checked the route timings in its margins, and to Dmitri, who had knocked down a cairn of stones in the garden to answer it.

Seven sets of hands. Seven people who were not supposed to be able to speak to one another.

He stood at the cart and held it, and he felt what he always felt when he held a book that had been loved. The faint, impossible warmth of other hands. As if someone had only just let go of it.

For months, he had told himself it was nonsense. A lonely mind inventing company.

He did not think it was nonsense now. He was not imagining the other hands. He knew exactly whose they were.

He put the primer down on the cart, very gently, and looked out of the library window, across the white lawns, toward the long, low, windowless side of Wing C.

*They know how to hide,* he thought. *They know how to talk. They know how to look like the dead.*

*They don't know who we are.*

It was time, he decided, to tell them. Where the three of them had come from. What had been done to them at the border. What their father had been, and what their mother had said at the end. What it had cost to stay three people when the machine wanted to make them none.

He could not write it himself. It was too much his, and he would make it a story. It needed to be told by someone who could hold all three sides of it at once, who had spent his whole life standing in the middle.

He felt, across the building, in the technical wing, his brother Nikolai lift his head from a circuit board, and go very still, and understand.
