# Chapter 26: The Pursuit

*August 2, 2106, 02:00 – dawn. East of the Capital, and the Border Zone*

---

Alexei had not driven a car in twenty months, and the moment his hands closed on the wheel he was twenty-two years old again, on a wet road in the border zone at three in the morning, with a family of four lying on the floor in the back and the lights of a patrol somewhere behind him in the fog.

It felt like coming home.

He drove without headlights. Elena sat beside him with a small screen in her lap, green lines on black, the resistance's own map of the roads, the one that showed not what the factorepos said was there but what was. Marcus and Kira were in the back. He could see them in the mirror when the road curved and the light from somewhere caught their faces: Kira upright, watching the road behind; Marcus with his head against the window, eyes closed, his lips moving.

"Left in four hundred metres," said Elena. "Farm track. It cuts off the ring road."

"I know it," said Alexei.

She looked at him sideways.

"I used to run it," he said. "Twenty-one oh-one. Couple from the interior, two kids. The ring road was blocked." He took the turn without slowing. Gravel spat under the wheels. "You'd have been proud of me, Aunt Lena. I was *very* good."

"I know," said Elena. "I read your file."

---

Behind them the lights on the ring road had multiplied.

Alexei watched them in the mirror as the farm track climbed a low rise. Blue and white, strung out along the road to the west like beads on a wire. Six. Eight. More coming out of the capital. And above them, briefly, a smaller light, moving on its own, very fast, low over the fields.

"Drone," said Kira from the back.

"I see it."

"It'll have thermal."

"I know." He was already turning. Off the track, through a gap in a hedge he had known about for eight years, down into a sunken lane between high banks where the trees met overhead. The drone's light swept across the field above them and went on. "They built these lanes before anyone had drones," he said. "Farmers are cleverer than Border Security. It's one of the great truths of the border zone."

In the mirror, the second car came through the gap behind him. Then the van, bouncing, its wired door rattling.

---

The border zone began without warning, the way it always did.

There was no fence, no sign. There never had been. One moment they were on a Veridican country road between Veridican fields, and the next the road was the same road and the fields were the same fields and the whole world had a faint, familiar *wrongness* to it, like the air before a headache. Alexei's interface was receiving two broadcasts. It had not received two broadcasts in twenty months. Veridica's towers, saying *this road built 2071, county route 9, maintained by the eastern district*. And behind them, fainter, from across the lake, Chronos: *this road built 2074, agricultural access, decommissioned 2090*.

He had lived with that his whole adult life. He had almost missed it.

"Storm ahead," said Elena, looking at her screen. "Sirens have been going in the lakeshore districts since midnight. It's one of the heavy ones."

"A real one?" said Kira.

Elena laughed. Not kindly. "There's no such thing as a real one," she said. "There's just the ones we didn't make."

---

It hit them eight kilometres into the zone.

Not like weather. It never was. The road was a road under the wheels and the trees were trees on either side, and then Alexei *knew* that the road ended in two hundred metres at a bridge that had been demolished in 2088, and he also *knew* that the bridge stood and carried traffic, and his hands on the wheel wanted to brake and wanted to accelerate at the same instant.

He did neither. He had driven through a hundred of these. He held the speed and held the wheel and looked at the road, the real road, the black strip under his headlights, which he had turned on now because in a storm headlights did not matter. You could not hide from a patrol in a storm. You could only hope the patrol was in the storm too.

Beside him Elena was reciting something, low and fast, under her breath. Coordinates. In the back, Kira was saying something to Marcus, very steady, and Marcus was answering her in the flat monotone of a man reading entries from a ledger.

"*Date: August second. Location: county route nine. Status: in transit.*"

"Good," said Kira. "Again."

"*Date: August second.*"

And from the car behind, very faint, on the short-range radio Elena had clipped to the dashboard, three voices, in rhythm.

"*Dima. Kolya. Lyosha.*"

Alexei found that he was saying it too. Under his breath. His hands steady on the wheel. *Dima. Kolya.* Feeling them behind him, one car back, the way he had felt them through twenty months of glass and walls and white rooms. Two weights at the edge of his mind. Holding.

*Here. Here. Here.*

---

The pursuit caught up with them on the long straight road across the marshes.

He saw them in the mirror first: headlights, many, coming fast along the causeway behind. Border Security. Four vehicles, then six. He could see the light bars on their roofs, not flashing. They did not need to flash. There was nobody out here to warn.

"They're in it now," said Elena, looking back. "The storm."

"I know."

"They'll have shielded vehicles."

"Some of them," said Alexei. "Not all. They never have enough." He had watched this happen from the other side, a hundred times, from a hedge or a ditch or a cellar window. "The shielded ones are expensive. They send them first. They always run out."

The lead Border Security vehicle was gaining. It was big and dark and heavy, and it came down the causeway behind the van at a speed that meant its driver was receiving only one broadcast. Shielded. It came up close behind Teo's rattling laundry van, and its floodlight went on, white, blinding, and lit the whole road.

And then the second vehicle, behind it, swerved.

Alexei saw it happen. There was nothing in the road. There was only the black strip of the causeway and the marsh on either side. But the driver's interface was receiving the storm, and the storm had just told him that there was a building across the road, a Chronos pumping station, built in 2083, that Veridica said had never been built at all, and he believed it. He swerved to miss it at a hundred kilometres an hour. His vehicle left the causeway and went into the marsh in a great white sheet of water, and did not come out.

The third vehicle braked hard. Too hard. The fourth hit it.

"God," said Kira quietly, from the back.

"They'd have done it to us," said Elena. Her voice was flat. "Keep driving."

---

It was the next storm wave that took the van.

Alexei saw it in the mirror. He would see it in his sleep for the rest of his life.

They had come off the causeway onto the lakeshore road where it ran along the top of an embankment, a long curve with the black water of the lake on one side and, on the other, a drop of fifteen metres to the old rail cutting below. There was a fork ahead. There had always been a fork: the lakeshore road bearing left along the embankment, and a service track going straight on for a hundred metres to a gate. Alexei took the left. He had taken it a hundred times. The second car took it behind him.

And the storm came in, harder than it had all night, a great surge of wrongness, and he felt the whole road double under his wheels. *The lakeshore road, built 2069.* And, from Chronos, *the Lakeshore Expressway, four lanes, straight, opened 2092*, which had never existed, which Veridica said had never been built, which ran straight on at the fork across the top of the rail cutting on a bridge that was not there.

He felt it. He felt his own hands want to go straight. He did not. He had driven this road too often. He knew where the ground was.

In the mirror, the van went straight.

He watched it happen. It was not fast. It was almost gentle. Teo did not know the road. Teo was twenty-three and had been in the resistance for a year and had never driven the lakeshore in a storm, and the storm showed him four lanes running straight on, lit, safe, and he took them. The van's headlights went straight on past the fork. Past the service track. Past the edge of the embankment, where the road did not go, where there was only the dark and a drop of fifteen metres to the cutting below.

For one second the headlights shone out over nothing, very bright.

Then they were gone.

---

He could not turn back.

He knew it as it happened. He had known it since he was twenty-two, running families across this same border in the fog: you do not turn back. If you turn back, the patrol behind you catches you and everyone else, and the ones who are already gone are still gone. You drive. You drive, and you carry it.

"*Lyosha—*"

Dima's voice on the radio. Just his name. Nothing else. Dima had seen it too.

"I know," Alexei said into the radio. His voice came out perfectly steady. "Keep driving."

In the back, Marcus had stopped reciting. Kira had her arms round him. Elena, beside Alexei, had put one hand flat over her own mouth and was looking straight ahead through the windscreen, and did not move.

Three people. Teo, who had held the door of his van and told them to get down on the floor. Hanna Marsh, who had said *I won't make a sound*. Pavel Ionescu, twenty years old, with his gray top on inside out.

With three and a half weeks, Alexei thought, they would have had a proper vehicle. With three and a half weeks, they would have had a driver who knew the lakeshore road. With three and a half weeks.

He drove.

---

The checkpoint was at the old county boundary, where the lakeshore road crossed from Veridica's zone into the contested strip, and it came out of the fog the way checkpoints always did in the border zone, without warning. One moment dark road and drifting mist. The next, floodlights.

Two barriers across the road. A booth. Four Border Security vehicles drawn up on the verge. Figures in dark uniforms with weapons, standing in the light. The fog was real fog, lake fog, the kind he had grown up in, thick and wet and gray, and the floodlights turned it white.

"Stop," said Elena. "Stop, stop, there's no way round—"

"There's always a way round," said Alexei, but he was already braking, because she was right. On the left, the lake. On the right, the rail cutting. Ahead, the barrier.

It was, he thought, almost exactly the checkpoint where they had been caught, twenty months ago. The same fog. The same lights. The three of them, back to back, surrounded, with Sokolov's voice on the loudspeaker telling them there was nowhere to go.

He brought the car to a stop twenty metres from the barrier. The second car stopped behind him.

The floodlights swung and found them.

---

A figure walked out from the booth into the light.

A woman. Tall, in a long dark coat over her uniform. Thirty-five. The hard, fine-boned face of someone who had been promoted young and had never once been allowed to look it.

Captain Yevgenia Sokolov.

She came slowly down the road toward them, alone, with her hands in the pockets of her coat. Behind her, at the barrier, her people stood with their weapons up, waiting. She did not signal them. She walked until she was ten metres from the lead car, and she stopped, and she looked through the windscreen.

At him.

Alexei sat behind the wheel with the floodlight in his eyes and looked back at her.

He had his brother's face. He had always had his brother's face. And he knew, looking at her, that she did not know which of them she was looking at, and that it did not matter. According to the Registry, all three of the men who wore this face had died two hours ago, in the east wing of Ashford Correctional Facility, of cognitive failure during a fact storm. She would have read it on her screen on the way out here. She would have read it, and come anyway.

She was looking at a dead man's face.

---

It went on for a long time. Longer than it should have. Long enough that one of the figures at the barrier took a step forward, and stopped, uncertain.

Alexei watched her face, and could not read it. That was the thing about Sokolov; it always had been. Dmitri had worked under her for four years and never once known what she was thinking.

He thought he knew some of it now.

She knew what had made the storm. She had to. A storm inside the capital, the first in history, on a building on the eastern edge of the city: there was only one kind of thing that could do that, and there were only a few of them in the country, and they were all hers. If she arrested these people tonight, at this checkpoint, there would be an inquiry. And the first question the inquiry would ask would be: *where did the unit come from?* And the second would be about her programme. And the third, eventually, would be about a systems engineer called Ivan Volkov, Subject 2471, lethal threshold confirmed.

She would be putting her own programme on trial.

And she was a woman who had stood in an observation room at the border facility and looked at three men in three cells on three monitors for a long time, and said, *They really loved each other, didn't they?*

And then, so quietly that no one had been sure they heard it: *Or the solution.*

He did not know which of those things she was thinking. He thought, watching her in the fog, that probably she did not know either.

---

She took one hand out of her coat pocket. There was a radio in it.

She lifted it and spoke into it without taking her eyes off the windscreen.

"*Sokolov. Lakeshore checkpoint.*" Her voice was completely flat. "*No contact. I repeat, no contact. Checkpoint clear.*"

She lowered the radio. She turned round and walked back toward the barrier, and said something to the figures standing there, and one of them looked at her, and then at the car, and then did what she said.

The barrier went up.

Sokolov stood by the booth with her hands back in her pockets and did not look at them again.

Alexei drove through.

He did not speed up. He drove through the checkpoint at a steady twenty kilometres an hour, past the booth, past the dark figures with their weapons lowered, past Sokolov standing with her face turned away into the fog. In the mirror, the second car followed. Then the barrier came down behind them, and the floodlights went out, and there was only the road and the fog again.

The radio on the dashboard crackled.

"*She let us go,*" said Dmitri. His voice was strange. Very quiet. Almost wondering. "*Lyosha. She let us go.*"

Alexei kept his eyes on the road.

"No," he said. "She let *herself* go. We were just in the way."

---

The safe house was an old factory on the contested strip, the kind of building that Veridica said had been abandoned in 2065 and Chronos said was a neural interface training facility, and that was in fact neither. Alexei had used it for four years before his arrest. He had not been sure it would still be there. It was.

They came to it at dawn, as the fog was turning from black to gray. Two cars in a yard of cracked concrete. A steel door. Inside, a long room with high broken windows and mattresses on the floor and a stove, and two of Elena's people, a man and a woman, who took one look at them and began, without a word, to heat water.

They stood in the long room and counted.

Marcus. Kira. Dmitri. Nikolai. Alexei. Tanaka, the jacket at her temple black with dried blood. Morrison. Old Songs. Elena.

Nine.

Nobody said the other three names. Nobody needed to.

---

Old Songs sat down by one of the broken windows.

He sat on an upturned crate with his thin hands on his knees and looked out at the fog coming off the lake, and for a long time he did nothing at all.

Then he began to sing.

Not hum. *Sing.* For the first time in all the months any of them had known him, the old man opened his mouth and sang, with words, in a voice that was cracked and thin and absolutely true. An old song. A song from before the Founding, in a mode none of them knew, with a lift at the end of each line like a question. A song for the dead. Alexei did not know the words. He knew, from the first line, what they were for.

The others stopped where they were and listened.

Marcus had sat down against the wall with Kira beside him. Tanaka lay on a mattress with her eyes closed and her lips moving. Morrison had his face in his hands. Elena stood by the stove with her back to all of them, very straight.

Old Songs sang for Teo, and Hanna Marsh, and Pavel Ionescu, whom he had known for three months, and for Samuel in 7-G, and for an old man on the road inside the fence with his arms over his head. He sang for a long time. When he finished, the fog outside had gone white with the morning.

---

Alexei stood in the doorway of the factory and watched it lift.

He had spent ten years getting people across this border. He had sat in rooms like this one with strangers who were shaking, and made them tea, and told them the truth: that it would cost them. Their families. Their names. Everything they had been. *Once you leave, you can never come back.* He had said it a hundred times. He had thought he knew what it meant.

He had not known. He knew now.

He stood in the doorway until the fog had gone and the flat gray lake lay open under the sky. Then he went inside.

His brothers were on two mattresses at the far end of the room, in the corner, where the wall was solid and the windows were whole. Dmitri was sitting up with his back to the wall, still in his boots. Nikolai was lying beside him, with his eyes open, looking at the ceiling.

Alexei lay down on the other side of Nikolai.

Nobody said anything. After a while Dmitri slid down the wall and lay flat. After a while longer, Nikolai closed his eyes.

For the first time since they were twelve years old, since a tarred roof above a lake, since before Papa went away on his *course* and came back quiet, the three Volkov brothers fell asleep in the same room.
