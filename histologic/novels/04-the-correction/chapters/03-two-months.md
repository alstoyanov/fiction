*Early December.*

The session was different. He knew it was different because he noticed it, and he had not noticed anything in a long time.

He was in the white chair. The hood was on. The voice was there, just behind his head, where it always was. Dr. Moss's hand was in his. Dr. Morrison was at the console, not looking. Everything was as it always was. And the machine was moving through him, lifting, turning, putting down, and there was hardly anything left to lift.

It went looking for the café and found a table and a cup and a timestamp. It went looking for his mother and found a canvas bag and a citizen number. It went looking for his father and found a folded flag. It went looking for Elena and found a record, filed, correct, complete.

It went looking for *belief* and found a set of words in the proper order.

And then it stopped.

For a long moment there was nothing. Just the hum, and the white, and the warm hand.

"Stable," said the voice.

It said it softly, the way a man might say the name of something he loved.

"Look at that baseline. Beautiful. Completely stable." A pause. He heard, faintly, the tapping of keys. "Reduce to maintenance. Weekly. We'll want to hold him exactly here."

Someone else said something. Marcus did not hear the words. He heard the voice answer them, turning slightly away, the way a man turns from a patient to speak to a colleague.

"And keep 7-K open," said Dr. Reeves. "We'll have our second subject within the week."

The words went past Marcus like the humming did now. Sound, with nothing in it.

The hood lifted.

Dr. Moss leaned over him. Her face was shining. There were tears in her eyes, and she was smiling, and she squeezed his hand very hard.

"You're healing, Marcus," she said. "You're really healing. I'm so proud of you."

He looked up at her. He did not know what the words meant. He knew only, the way a dog knows from the tone of a voice, that they were good words. That he had done something right.

He tried to smile back. He was not sure if he did.

He sat on the edge of his bed, bent forward, his elbows on his knees, his head in his hands.

He sat like that most of the time now. It was not a decision. It was simply the shape his body took when no one was asking it to do anything, the way water takes the shape of the cup. His glasses were pushed up on his forehead. His hands covered his eyes. The white of the cell came through between his fingers.

He did not think. There was very little to think about.

At some point, far away, at the other end of the atrium, a door opened. Not the treatment corridor. The other door, the one to the rest of the building, the one he had come through himself, a very long time ago, with Supervisor Hayes. He knew it had opened because for one second the silence of Wing C broke, and sound came through: footsteps, a trolley wheel, and a voice.

A woman's voice. Raised. Angry. Arguing.

"—never been properly investigated, you can't just—if someone took it from the system then someone *took* it, that's not a delusion, that's a *fact*—"

It was a voice with something in it. It was alive, and furious, and asking questions, and it would not stop.

Then the inner doors sealed and the silence closed over it again like water.

Somewhere down the corridor, a new inmate was being processed.

Marcus Chen did not look up.
