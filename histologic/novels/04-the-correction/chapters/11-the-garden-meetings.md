# Chapter 11: The Garden Meetings

*Late January – February 2106. Ashford Correctional Facility, the Garden*

---

The garden was cold enough to hurt his teeth, and Marcus had never loved anything so much in his life.

It was the air. For three and a half months he had breathed the air of Wing C, filtered and warmed and faintly sweet, the same air in and out of the same glass box. The garden air came in off the frozen lawns with nothing done to it at all. It smelled of earth and ice and, very faintly, woodsmoke from somewhere beyond the walls. The first time the orderly opened the gate and walked him through, he stopped on the gravel path and breathed until his lungs ached, and the orderly had to touch his elbow twice.

*Garden privileges*, the new line on his blue card said. *Mon. Wed. Fri. 1400–1500. ESC.*

He did not know why they had given them to him. Dr. Moss had told him, in the white chair, with tears of pride in her eyes, that he had been *so stable for so long* that he had earned a little fresh air. The voice behind the mirror had said nothing. But the card had come through the slot the next day, and now three times a week he sat on a wooden bench in a small walled garden and looked at the sky.

He was not alone.

---

There were six benches along the south wall. The orderly sat on a chair by the gate with a tablet. A few inmates from the general wards came out at the same hour: an elderly woman who fed crumbs to sparrows, two men who played a slow game with pebbles, a girl who walked the path in circles. And from Wing C, four.

Marcus. Kira. The small woman with the silver-streaked hair, who was, it turned out, Dr. Yuki Tanaka. And the tall man from the upper level, Isaiah Okonkwo, who walked into the garden the first day as if he were arriving at a press conference, looked slowly around at the walls and the benches and the cameras and the orderly, and sat down at the far end of the row with his long hands folded on one knee.

And at the beds along the north wall, raking the frozen soil, blank-faced, in his overalls: one of the triplets.

Marcus had nearly flinched the first time he saw him. The man had the same face as the librarian. He had knelt among the roses as they came in and not looked up, and his movements had been so slow and so mechanical that Marcus had felt a pang of something like pity. *The gardener,* he thought. *The most corrected man in Ashford.*

---

Kira had worked out the camera.

She told him on the second day, sitting at the other end of his bench, her face turned toward the sky, her lips barely moving.

"East pole. It rotates. One hundred and forty degrees, nine minutes twenty seconds a sweep. For four minutes of each sweep it's pointed at the north gate. When it's pointed at the gate, it can't see this end of the benches. The two fixed cameras are angled at the path. If we keep our faces turned away from them and don't move much, they see four patients sitting in the cold."

"How do you know?"

"I counted."

He almost laughed. It came up out of him like a hiccup, a strange small sound, and he had to press it down and turn it into a cough. He had not laughed since the autumn.

Four minutes, then. Out of every nine. They sat on separate benches, a metre or two apart, faces angled to the sky or the ground or the bare apple tree. When the east camera swung away toward the gate, they spoke, low, in turn, like people praying. When it swung back, they stopped.

Four minutes was not much. It was the most time Marcus had spent talking to anyone since October.

---

Dr. Tanaka spoke to him on the fourth day.

She had been watching him since the first afternoon, from her bench two along. It was the same watching he remembered from the glass: steady, attentive, clinical. It did not feel intrusive. It felt, he realised, like being looked after.

When the camera swung away she said, without turning her head:

"Mr. Chen. I want to tell you what they did to you. May I?"

He hesitated. "Yes."

"You've been through what the programme's documents call reduction to baseline," she said. Her voice was very quiet and very precise, with a slight roughness to it, like an instrument that has been used a great deal. "The protocol finds the areas of highest salience in the memory. The areas where there is most feeling. It destabilises them. The associative links weaken. The feeling drains out. What's left is the information, stripped of its meaning. Like a record with no one to read it."

"Yes," Marcus said. That was exactly what it was like. He had not had the words.

"You went a long way down," she said. "Further than anyone I've seen come back. There's a point, below which, in my experience, there is no one left to return. I call it the floor." She paused. "You were very close to it. In December I thought you had perhaps a few weeks."

He looked at the gravel.

"But you're still here," she said. "That's what I wanted to say. You're still here. Salience maps can be rebuilt. Links can grow back. Slowly. With use. With..." She glanced, very briefly, down the row of benches toward Kira. "With reasons."

"Everything?"

She was quiet for a moment. "No," she said. "Not everything. I won't lie to you. Some things will not come back. I'm sorry."

The camera began to swing back. She fell silent. The girl walking in circles passed in front of them, humming tunelessly to herself.

---

He found out what he had lost in pieces, the way you find out the extent of a flood by walking through the house.

His mother's name was the first. He was sitting on the bench on a Wednesday afternoon thinking, for no particular reason, about the canvas bag of essays, and he reached for her name to go with it, and it was not there.

He knew her citizen number. He knew her profession, her school, the date of her death, the heart condition recorded in the factorepo. He could see her hands, green ink on her fingers. But her *name*, the word his father had called her, the word written on the card that came with the flowers at the funeral, the word he had said a thousand times as a boy—

Gone. Not blurred. Not filed. *Gone*. Like a word cut out of a page with a blade.

He sat on the bench in the cold and felt it the way you feel a missing tooth with your tongue. Over and over. Probing the gap.

When the camera swung away, he told Kira.

She was quiet for a long time. "I can't give you that one," she said finally. "I don't know it. I'm sorry."

"I know."

"But I'll remember that you lost it," she said. "That's something. I'll remember that there was a name and that it mattered to you. When we get out, we'll find it in the factorepo, and I'll remember why."

*When we get out.* He turned the phrase over for the rest of the afternoon. He had not thought, in months, that *out* was a direction.

---

There were other gaps. He remembered that Elena had spoken to him in her apartment on the last night, but not what she had said; he remembered *that* he had once had three versions of her last words, and he had none now. He remembered *that* he had believed in Veridica with his whole heart, and he could not remember what the believing had felt like. It was like knowing, as a fact, that you had once been in love. It was a line in a record.

He remembered the Enforcement Center. He remembered shouting. He held on to that one.

Kira filled in what she could. She knew what had been in the news. She knew what the Regional Factorepo was, how it worked, what a Junior Historian did. She told him in the four-minute gaps, little by little, and he took each piece and turned it over and tried to fit it back into himself.

"You worked on the fourteenth floor," she said once. "It said so in the article. There was a photograph of the building."

He closed his eyes. The fourteenth floor. The soft hum of the data processors. Three monitors. A man at the next workstation stretching his arms above his head, saying *another day in paradise*.

"Thomas," he said aloud, and opened his eyes, startled.

"Who's Thomas?"

"I don't know," he said, and then, slowly, "I think he was my friend."

---

Isaiah Okonkwo spoke to all three of them on the second Monday.

He had been silent for the first week, watching. When he spoke it was in the measured voice of a man who had rehearsed exactly what he was going to say and would not waste a word of the four minutes.

"We need to talk about why we are here," he said. "Not what we did. Why *here*. Wing C. I have been in this building for three months, and I have kept count of everyone who has passed through that atrium. Most of them come and go. Overflow from the general wards. A few nights in a glass box, and then they're moved on." He paused. "Seven of us stay. The three brothers. Dr. Tanaka. Mr. Chen. Ms. Osman. And me."

Kira had gone very still at the end of her bench.

"I have seen a tablet header," Isaiah said. "In my session, by accident. *Project Continuity. Subject C-7. Witness.* I believe I am C-7." He looked at the sky. "Which means that somewhere in this building there is a list, and there are six names on it before mine."

"C-6," said Dr. Tanaka quietly. "I've seen it on the orderlies' tablets. I didn't know what it meant."

"Then I would guess the rest," Isaiah said, "if I were a guessing man. Which, professionally, I am not."

Marcus tried to follow. He could feel the shape of the idea: a pattern, a list, a reason behind the reason. It excited something in him, something very old, the part of him that had spent three years looking at a thousand facts to find the one that didn't fit. He wanted to reach for it. He wanted to lay it out and look at it.

And at the same time, it exhausted him. It was like trying to lift something heavy with a hand that had been broken. The idea kept sliding away. He kept losing the thread of what Isaiah had said a minute ago.

"We were targeted," Isaiah said. "I don't yet know for what."

The camera swung back. They sat in silence, four patients in the cold. Marcus stared at the frozen roses and held on to the word *targeted* with everything he had, and by the time they walked back to Wing C he had nearly lost it twice.

---

The old man with the trolley came to the garden gate every day at quarter to three.

He did not come in. He only passed along the corridor outside the gate on his way back from the kitchens, pushing the empty trolley toward Wing C for the afternoon round, and when he passed the gate the orderly always nodded to him and he always nodded back.

He was humming.

Marcus had stopped hearing the humming in November. It had become a sound, like the hum of the cradle. But on a Friday afternoon at the end of January, sitting on the bench with the cold air in his lungs and the gravel white with frost, he heard it again. The same low, wandering tune, rising at the end of each phrase like a question.

And it *landed*.

It came into him all at once and whole, the way nothing had come into him in months. A room with yellow curtains. A narrow bed. Rain on a window. He was very small, and he had a fever, and his mother was sitting on the edge of the bed with a cool cloth in her hand, and she was singing. Very softly. Not an anthem. Not anything from the broadcasts. An old song, in a mode he didn't know, with a lift at the end of each line like a question. A song she was not supposed to know.

*Hush now, the river is long.*

He did not remember the words. Only those four. Only her voice singing them, low and a little off-key, and her hand on his forehead, and the rain.

He had not remembered it in twenty years. He had not remembered it at all.

And it was not a filed thing. It was not a record. It came with *everything*: the fever and the rain and the love, a love so enormous and so plain that he had to put his hand over his mouth to keep from making a sound.

He sat on the bench with his hand over his mouth and his eyes stinging and the old man's humming going away along the corridor, and he did not know what was happening to him.

When the camera swung away, he could not speak. Kira looked at him from the end of the bench, and her face changed.

It was Dr. Tanaka who spoke. She had been watching him, of course. She was always watching him.

"What was it?" she asked, very gently.

"My mother," he managed. "She used to sing it. When I was ill. I haven't—I didn't *have* it. It wasn't there. And now it's—" He stopped. "It's all there. All of it. Not like the others. Not filed."

Dr. Tanaka was silent. When he looked at her, her eyes were very sharp.

"Music," she said, almost to herself. "It survived in the music."

---

In the white chair, he began to lie.

He did it the way Kira had told him, and then he did it his own way, because hers did not quite fit. She thought in specifications. He did not. He had never designed anything. He had *entered* things.

So he took the one thing he had done every working day of his adult life, thousands and thousands of times, until it had worn a groove in him deeper than anything the machine had touched: the fact entry. The format. *Date. Time. Location. Involved parties. Event. Verification status.* The dull, sacred ritual of the Regional Factorepo.

And he filled the front of his mind with it.

*Traffic incident, corner of Fifth and Main, fourteen-oh-two hours, two vehicles, minor damage, no injuries, verified by sensor network, entered. Property dispute, Linden Avenue, neighbours, boundary fence, surveillance confirms prior agreement, verified, entered. Birth, Central Hospital, oh-three-fifty hours, female, three point one kilograms, verified, entered. Traffic incident. Property dispute. Birth. Business transaction, market district, fish, verified, entered.*

When the hood came down and the hum began and the machine went looking, it found a Junior Historian doing data entry. Endless, meaningless, perfectly flat data entry. It skimmed across it like a hand across water. It did not stop.

And underneath, folded into the formats, where he had put them, the new things.

*Location: garden. Involved parties: four. Event: conversation. Verification status: pending.*

*Location: library, reference section. Involved parties: two. Event: "If you entered it, it happened." Verification status: pending.*

*Involved parties: Osman, K. Event:*

He never finished that one. He had learned not to finish it. If he finished it, it would become something he cared about, and the machine would feel it get warm.

The voice behind the mirror was silent for a long time.

Then it said, softly, with the tenderness of a man admiring a painting: "Flawless."

He lay very still in the white chair and let Dr. Moss squeeze his hand and did not let anything move in his face.

He made it back to his cell before he started shaking. He sat on the bed with his head in his hands, the shape of a broken man, and shook so hard that his teeth knocked together, and behind his hands, in the dark, he was *laughing*, silently, helplessly, like a boy who has got away with something enormous.

He had fooled it.

He had fooled the machine.

---

He was falling in love with Kira Osman, and he knew it.

He knew it the way he had learned to know things now: carefully, with suspicion, turning each one over to check whether it was real. It was not the same as Elena. He could remember, as a fact, what Elena had felt like: sudden, overwhelming, a door flung open. This was slower. This was a small warm stone that he had been carrying in his pocket since the corridor in December, and it had got a little warmer every day, and now he could not put his hand in his pocket without feeling it.

She was his anchor. That was the problem. He knew it was a problem. She was the one real thing in a world that had been rubbed flat, the one person who had looked at him and seen someone there, and he did not know how much of what he felt was *her* and how much was simply need. The way a drowning man loves a rope.

He was damaged. He knew that too. He had holes in him that would never be filled. He could not remember his mother's name.

He told her, on a Wednesday in February, in the four minutes, with his face turned to the bare apple tree. He did not plan to. It came out the way things came out of him now, unfiltered, before he could stop them.

"I don't know if what I feel for you is real," he said, "or if I just need it."

She did not answer at once. The camera ticked slowly round toward the gate.

"I knew someone," she said at last, "who told me two things at once. That he'd been sent to use me. And that he loved me." She was looking at the sky. "I spent a long time thinking only one of them could be true. The factorepo would have made me pick. It rejects contradictions." A pause. "But they were both true. Both at the same time. I think that's allowed, for people. I think it might be the thing that makes us people."

He did not know what to say.

"You can need it," she said, "and it can be real. Both."

The camera began to swing back. She fell silent. He sat on his bench with his hands in his lap and felt the stone in his pocket, and it was very warm.

---

On the last Friday in February, in the four-minute gap, the gardener came over to the bed beside Marcus's bench.

He came slowly, rake in hand, blank-faced, and knelt among the dead roses a metre from Marcus's feet. He began to clear the leaves from around the stems with his bare fingers, one leaf at a time. Marcus did not look at him. He had learned not to look at the corrected ones. It was like looking at Samuel.

The gardener did not look up. He did not stop working. His face did not change at all.

But his lips moved. Barely. So little that from a metre away Marcus almost didn't see it.

"You are doing well," the gardener said. "Careful."

Then the east camera finished its sweep and swung back toward the benches, and the gardener's face was blank again, and his fingers went on clearing the leaves, slow and mechanical. After a minute he stood up and moved away along the bed, as if he had never stopped.

Marcus sat on the bench and stared at the gravel.

He did not move. He did not dare. His heart was going so hard that he thought the orderly by the gate must be able to see it through his coat.

*You are doing well. Careful.*

He had watched that man for four months. From across the atrium, from the benches, through the glass. He had pitied him. He had thought, looking at the slow hands and the empty face, that here was the most completely corrected person in the building. The one they had finished.

The gardener was working his way along the north wall now, his back to them, raking the frozen ground, one stroke at a time.

He had spoken in perfect sentences.

And he had timed it, Marcus realised, to the second.
