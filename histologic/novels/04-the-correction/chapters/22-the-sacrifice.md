# Chapter 22: The Sacrifice

*July 21–31, 2106. The Garden, Maintenance Room B-4, and Cell 7-E*

---

Isaiah set the last stone on a Wednesday afternoon, in the four-minute gap, while the gardener was working at the far end of the north bed with his back turned.

It was a small gray lake stone, smooth, the size of a hen's egg. He had carried it in his pocket from the path for three days. He knelt by the bed beside his bench, as if to retie his shoe, and put it down among the others at the edge of the roses.

Dmitri's map was all there, as it had been for a month. The long stones of the east building. The short row that was Wing C. The white stone that was B-4, the cairn that was the vestibule, the apple tree that was the fence. And near the apple tree, in a small loose ring on the soil, the seven stones that Dmitri had set there in the first week, which meant *us*.

Isaiah put his stone a hand's width outside the ring. On the building side. Not the fence side.

Then he sat back on his bench and folded his hands on his knee, and waited.

It took Dmitri eleven minutes to work his way back along the bed. Isaiah watched him out of the corner of his eye: the slow rake, the blank face, the stroke, the step. Dmitri reached the roses by the third bench. He knelt. His eyes went over the map the way they always did, a quick, precise check, stone by stone, like a man counting ammunition.

They stopped at the ring.

They stayed there a long time.

Dmitri did not look up. He did not look at Isaiah. He did not move the stone. He only knelt there among the roses with his hands still, and Isaiah, who had spent twenty years watching men receive news across tables, saw him understand.

---

It was arithmetic, in the end. Most hard things are.

Nikolai had set it out in the primers, the week after the single node, in pricks so uneven that Isaiah could see him fighting to keep his hand still.

*The raw Continuity archive is approximately forty terabytes. It is in the data centre under the east building, on encrypted arrays. With three and a half weeks, we could have moved it out in stages through my partition to Elena's people at the regional node. That was the plan. It is not possible now. I no longer have access to B-4. Morrison can reach the partition, but only for short sessions, and every one is a risk.*

*Elena can supply a physical drive. The largest she can get is six terabytes. We will copy what we can onto it during the storm. Kira will break the encryption. I will strip the arrays. In twenty minutes, in a storm, we might get the core: the aims, the subject files, the protocols, the session data for the seven of us.*

*We will not get the rest. The rest is the proof. The session logs for every subject since March 2105. The calibration series. The Ministry correspondence, which the Director believes is on the arrays. The Phase Two planning. Without the rest, it is our word and a drive against theirs.*

*Someone would have to stay at a terminal, after we have gone, and send it out. Over days. Through the Director's channel.*

He had not written *who*. He had not needed to.

---

The last meeting in B-4 was on the night of July twenty-seventh.

Morrison arranged it. Another sleep study, another firmware update, another work order in the Director's tray. It was riskier this time. They all knew it. Reeves was reading audit tables now. But Morrison had simply filed it the way he filed everything, flatly, in the proper form, and the Director had signed it, and on the night the cameras went down for forty-five minutes and nine people stood round a workbench in a warm basement full of humming cabinets.

Isaiah did not wait for the agenda.

"I'm staying," he said.

---

He had prepared it the way he prepared an editorial. He had written it in the study of his memory palace over three nights and read it through and struck out every word that did not need to be there, and he said it now in the same order, standing by the workbench with his long hands folded in front of him.

"If six of us escape and the evidence leaves with us," he said, "Veridica will call it forgery. Outlaws, fugitives, a drive of fabricated files. Every official voice in the country will say so within the hour. And they will hunt us, and when they find us, the files will die with us."

Nobody spoke.

"If the evidence is published from *inside*," he said, "by a subject who remained, who did not escape, who is still in their custody when the story breaks, then it is not a fugitive's forgery. It is a witness account. And it can't be traced to six people who, according to the Registry, died in the storm." He looked at the Director. "It comes out of Ashford through the Director's own channel, from a patient who was here the whole time. They can deny it. They cannot make it look like an escape."

"And someone has to send the rest," said Nikolai quietly.

"And someone has to send the rest," Isaiah agreed. "Over days. After you've gone. And someone has to make sure it goes out if the escape fails, and you're all in a van at the bottom of a ditch." He paused. "I'm a journalist. That is the job. You stay where the story is."

---

Alexei was the first to protest, and the loudest.

"No," he said. "No. We don't leave people. That's the whole point of me. I've spent ten years in the border zone getting people out, and I have never once left someone at the fence and driven away. I'm not starting with you."

"You're not leaving me," Isaiah said.

"I'm going to be in a car, and you're going to be in a cell. That's leaving."

Tanaka spoke next, and her voice was very quiet. "They'll move you to maximum security. The day it publishes. You know that. You know what the cradles are like there, Isaiah. They're not like these. There's no Morrison at the console."

"I know," said Isaiah.

"They'll try to take the article out of you."

"I know." He smiled slightly. "They'll find a very nice parlour."

Nobody laughed. He had not really expected them to.

---

And then Marcus.

He had been standing by the terminal with Kira, as he had at the first meeting, not quite touching. He had not spoken yet. When he did, it came out slowly, the way things still came out of him when they mattered, as if each word had to be found and lifted and set down.

"We don't leave people behind," he said.

He was not looking at Isaiah. He was looking at the floor.

"I left Samuel," he said. "The first night. I put my hand on the glass and then I sat there for two months and watched him and I never—" He stopped. "I don't want to leave anyone else. I don't want to walk out of here and leave someone in a glass box. I can't."

The basement was very quiet. The cabinets hummed.

Isaiah looked at him for a long moment. Then he crossed the room, slowly, ducking under the cable run, and stood in front of Marcus Chen, and waited until Marcus looked up.

"You're not leaving me," he said gently. "I'm staying by choice. That's the difference. And you, of all the people in this room, know exactly how big a difference that is."

Marcus's face moved.

"They *took* your choice," Isaiah said. "They took a fifty-eight and typed a seventy-three and sent you here. You never chose any of it. I am *making* mine. With my eyes open. For a reason I believe in." He put a hand, very lightly, on Marcus's shoulder. It was the first time Isaiah had touched any of them. "And you need to heal. You can't do that here. Not in a glass box, with Reeves in the next room. You need to be somewhere with windows."

Marcus held his eyes for a long time.

Then he nodded. Once.

---

Nikolai was the last to protest, and the first to accept.

He had been sitting on the edge of the workbench, because he could not stand for long since the single node; his legs shook. He had said nothing through the whole argument. When he spoke, it was so quietly that Isaiah had to turn to hear.

"Somebody stays in the middle," Nikolai said. "Between the ones who go and the ones who hunt them. That's the job. I've always been that." He looked at his hands. "For once, let it be someone who chose it."

Alexei looked at his brother for a long time. Then he turned away and put his forehead against one of the humming cabinets, and stayed there.

---

"I'm staying too," said the Director.

They turned to her. She was by the door, as before, in her dark coat.

"I'm the Director of this facility," she said. "Someone has to write the report. Someone has to stand in front of the inquiry and say: there was a storm, the like of which none of us had ever seen, and in the chaos six Wing C subjects and a long-term patient and a Continuity engineer suffered cognitive failure and died. Remains processed. One subject survived. He has been moved to secure care." Her mouth tightened. "I have to be the one saying it. If I'm the one saying it, it will be believed."

"And afterwards?" said Tanaka.

"Afterwards they'll investigate me," the Director said. "For months. They'll suspect everything and prove nothing, because the only people who could prove anything will be dead, and one of them will be in maximum security writing the story that makes anyone who wants me gone have to explain in public what I know." She smiled, very slightly, without humour. "I've run a correctional facility for eleven years. I know how to survive an inquiry."

"And the shielding," said Dmitri.

It was the first thing he had said all night. He was standing by the door, as he always did, with his back to the wall.

"Nikolai was going to take down the isolation from B-4," he said. "He cannot now. Someone must be at this terminal when the storm begins, and keep the shielding down against the automatic system for twenty minutes, while it tries to come back up every few seconds." He looked at the Director. "Someone who can be in B-4 at midnight without being questioned."

"Yes," said the Director. "I'd assumed so."

"You will be in the storm," said Dmitri. "Down here. Alone. You will feel all of it."

"Yes."

"You will need to type while your mind tells you that this building is a field."

The Director looked at Nikolai. "Then he'll have to teach me very well," she said. "Won't he."

---

It was decided before the clock on the terminal reached zero, the way the first decision had been. No vote. Just each of them saying it, one after another.

Six subjects would go: Marcus, Kira, Tanaka, and the three brothers. With them, Old Songs, and Morrison, who could not stay after what he would do that night. One subject would stay. The Director would stay, in B-4, in the storm, with Nikolai's instructions in her hand.

Everyone had a part. And everyone, Isaiah thought, looking round the warm basement at the eight faces in the cabinet light, could see the gaps. The storm unit only half calibrated, because Elena's technician had lost two weeks. Only two proper vehicles, and whatever else Elena's people could find. Reeves in the building. The drive too small. Nikolai unable to reach his own partition. A plan built in stones for three and a half weeks and cut down to fit into two.

It was not a good plan anymore. It was the only plan.

---

He went down to the cellar that night.

He had not been down the stairs, even in his head, for a long time. He had been careful. Down there were the storm onset times, and the procurement leak, and page thirty-one, and the Continuity header, and everything Nikolai had sent round in five months of primers, and he had kept it all in the dark, behind the door he had painted the exact dull cream of the kitchen wall.

He went down now, and turned on the light, and looked at it all.

Then he went back up and walked through the whole house.

The parlour, with his aunt at the head of the table. The kitchen. The study with its drawers. The bedrooms upstairs, with the people he had interviewed laid out on the pillows. The house on Carver Street, which did not exist anymore, which had existed perfectly in his head for twenty years.

In a little under a week they would move him to maximum security. There would be new cradles. Deeper. No Morrison. He did not know how much of the house would survive. He had to assume that some of it would not.

So he did what his aunt used to do before a dinner. He went through the rooms one by one and took down everything that mattered and carried it downstairs.

His first byline. His aunt's voice. The smell of pepper soup. The harbour contracts. The school closures. The retired schoolteacher in the border town. The faces of the six people he had stood in a basement with tonight. The Director's dry voice. Alexei's forehead against the cabinet. Marcus Chen nodding once. He carried them all down the narrow stairs one at a time and put them on the shelves beside page thirty-one, until the cellar was full and the house above it was bare.

Let them have the parlour. Let them have the empty rooms.

He sat in the cellar, in his head, on the bottom step, with everything he loved around him on the shelves, and began to write.

---

He had been composing it for months without admitting it. Every journalist does. You carry the first sentence around in you for the whole length of an investigation, turning it over, trying it against each new fact, until the day you can finally write it down.

He wrote it down now, in the cellar, in the dark.

*Last October, in a white building on the eastern edge of our capital, seven people were chosen to prove that a human being can be made to believe anything, and to mean it.*

He read it through. He changed nothing. It was true, and it was the right length, and it had the word *mean* in it, which was the word that mattered.

He was afraid. He let himself be. There was no one to perform for in the cellar. He sat on the bottom step with his first sentence in his hands and felt, for the first time since October, the full weight of what he had chosen, and it was very heavy, and he did not put it down.

---

The book-slot opened a little after eleven.

It was not the meal round. The meal round had been hours ago. He turned his head on the pillow and saw, through the glass, the old man with the white ponytail standing by his door with the empty trolley, as if he had simply stopped there on his way back to the kitchens and forgotten why.

Old Songs was humming. A tune Isaiah didn't know. Low, and slow, and very old, with a lift at the end of each phrase like a question.

Then the old man bent down, slowly, stiffly, until his bearded face was level with the slot. For two seconds, the soundproofing was open. For two seconds, Isaiah could hear him.

"The one who stays," Old Songs said, very quietly, in a voice rough with disuse, "is the one who remembers."

Isaiah did not move.

"That was always my job," said the old man. "Forty years of it. In one place after another. Keeping the songs while everyone else was sent on." His pale eyes, through the slot, were very clear and very sharp. "Now it's yours."

The slot slid closed.

The old man straightened up, and took hold of the trolley, and pushed it away along the curve of the atrium toward the kitchens, humming, until Isaiah could not see him anymore.
