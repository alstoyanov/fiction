# Chapter 8: The Pattern

*December 15–16, 2105. Wing C, Cell 7-E*

---

The house had nine rooms, and Isaiah Okonkwo walked through all of them every night before he slept.

It was the house on Carver Street where he had grown up, in the old eastern quarter of the capital, before the quarter was cleared and rebuilt. It did not exist anymore. It existed perfectly in his head. He had furnished it there over twenty years of reporting, and every piece of furniture was a fact.

In the front parlour, where his aunt had held her dinners, he kept the things he did not mind anyone seeing. His career: the byline at the *Capital Review*, the awards, the long pieces about harbour contracts and school closures. His family: Aunt Sarah at the head of the table, sharp and elegant, already talking about the diplomatic service. The smell of pepper soup. His own name in print for the first time, framed above the fireplace. All of it true. All of it warm. All of it harmless.

The kitchen held his sources. The study held his notes, filed in drawers by year. Upstairs, the bedrooms held the people he had interviewed, one to a bed, their words laid out on the pillows.

And below the kitchen, down a flight of narrow stairs his aunt had always told him not to use, was the cellar.

He never went into the cellar when they were watching. He did not even think about the stairs.

---

In the white chair, he gave them the parlour.

It had taken him three sessions to learn how. In the first, the machine had gone through the house like a burglar, fast and greedy, opening every door, and he had nearly lost the study. In the second he had understood that it was looking for heat, for the rooms he cared about most, and that it could feel him caring. By the third he had learned to walk it through the front door and into the parlour and *keep* it there. To sit it down at his aunt's table and feed it pepper soup and old bylines. He let it take whatever it wanted from that room. He let it find his pride, his ambition, his love for his family, his occasional vanity. He let it turn them over and rub at them and pronounce them corrupted. He wept, sometimes, when it did. The weeping was real. The parlour was real.

But it was the parlour. And the cellar stairs were in the kitchen, behind a door he had painted, in his mind, the exact dull cream of the kitchen wall.

The machine had never looked behind it.

*A journalist protects his sources,* he told himself, in the chair, with his aunt's voice in his ear and the calm voice behind the mirror telling him he was doing very well. *Even from himself.*

---

He was here because of the cellar.

He had been working on the story for fourteen months. It had started with a death notice, a small one, a retired schoolteacher in a border town who had died of *cognitive failure secondary to prolonged fact-storm exposure*. It was a common enough phrase. He had seen it a hundred times. But the date had caught his eye, because he had been in that town that week reporting on something else, and he remembered the storm. He remembered the sirens starting at an odd hour, just after three in the afternoon. And he remembered thinking, even then, that it was wrong. Every storm he had ever lived through had crept up on him: a building unease as the two nations' broadcasts drifted into conflict, the half-hour of headaches before the world began to flicker. This one had not crept. It had arrived all at once, at full strength, in every head in the street. Like a switch being thrown.

He had gone looking for others. Death notices, admissions to the cognitive trauma wards, border-zone incident logs. He had built a spreadsheet in the study, in his head, because he had learned long ago not to keep the dangerous ones on any device. Two hundred and six deaths over nine years. And a pattern that the official explanation could not account for. A natural storm, everyone knew, happened when the two nations' factorepos disagreed about a place: when a new contradictory fact was entered on one side of the border and its broadcast collided with the other side's. So the storms ought to have followed the fact logs. They didn't. Isaiah had checked. Dozens of storms had broken over districts where neither factorepo had entered anything new for months. Storms that started on the hour. Storms that returned, over and over, to the same few streets. Storms that behaved, he had written in his notes, *less like a conflict and more like a schedule*.

Then, in September, a source in Border Security procurement had given him a leak. Forty pages of supply requisitions and test logs, most of them dull beyond description. On page thirty-one, a list of numbered test subjects from a series fifteen years ago, with outcomes in a single column.

Most of the outcomes said *recovered* or *partial*.

One of them, Subject 2471, said: *lethal threshold confirmed.*

He had sat with that line for a whole night. Then he had taken it down the cellar stairs and put it on a shelf by itself.

He had been three days from publishing when the enforcers came. *Unauthorized historical inquiry. Solicitation of classified material. Contamination probability: eighty-one percent.* His editor had not called. His aunt had not called. He did not know whether they were not allowed to, or had chosen not to, and he had decided, for the sake of his own sanity, not to find out yet.

---

It was the orderlies who told him the most.

The cells were soundproofed. The treatment corridor was not. Twice a week he walked that corridor between two orderlies, and twice a week he listened. Orderlies talked. They talked about their children and their shifts and the canteen, and occasionally, when they forgot that the man walking between them was a person and not a piece of equipment, they talked about work.

*Take Dr. Tanaka—sorry, C-6—through to C-3, would you, he wants her at ten.*

*Is Osman in two or four today?*

*Has anyone told Reeves the triple series is slipping again?*

He filed every word in the kitchen. Then he went back to his cell and carried them, one at a time, down the cellar stairs.

---

He had a taxonomy of the staff. He had one for everyone. It was a reporter's reflex: sort the people in any building into the ones who would talk, the ones who wouldn't, and the ones who would lie.

There were the believers. Moss was the purest example he had ever encountered. She held his hand in the white chair with such tenderness that he had to look at the ceiling. She told him he was healing, and she believed it. She had the particular incandescent sincerity of someone who has never once, in her whole life, been tempted to ask a second question.

There were the jobholders. Most of the orderlies. Hayes. People who had been told what the work was and had done it, because work was work and the pay was good and the children needed shoes. They would not lie, exactly. They would simply never know anything worth lying about, and they would be very careful to keep it that way.

There were the disturbed. Morrison. Isaiah had interviewed enough men in enough uncomfortable positions to know the look: a man sitting on something he knows is wrong, and getting heavier every day. Morrison never looked at the patients. He looked at his screens as if they might at any moment look back at him.

And there was Reeves.

Reeves he could not classify. That in itself was information.

He had never seen him. Nobody in Wing C had, as far as he knew. He was a voice behind a mirror. But Isaiah had spent twenty years listening to people talk, and he had learned to hear what they believed in by the way they said the words they cared about. When Moss said *healing*, she said it the way people say *God*. When the orderlies said *procedure*, they said it the way people say *rent*.

When Reeves said *Veridica*, he said it the way people say *the building I work in*.

He did not believe in Veridica, Isaiah thought. Not the way Moss did. He was not a patriot and he was not a fanatic. He believed in something, certainly. His voice was full of it: calm, patient, delighted. But whatever he believed in was not the nation, or the factorepo, or even correction.

It was something he was making.

---

The tablet happened on Tuesday.

He was in the white chair, in C-4, with the hood down and the calm voice in his ear, walking the machine around the parlour. Moss had stepped out for a moment. Morrison was alone at the console, and at some point he reached across for a stylus and his elbow caught the edge of the tablet propped beside the screens, and it tipped, and slid, and ended up lying flat on the console's edge, face up, angled toward the chair.

Toward Isaiah.

Upside down, and at the very edge of his vision, which the hood left clear.

He did not move his eyes. He did not dare. He let the machine keep chewing on his aunt's dinner party. And with the part of his mind he kept free in the kitchen, he read, upside down, at an angle, the header on the screen.

*PROJECT CONTINUITY*

*SUBJECT C-7 — WITNESS*

*SESSION 14 — SALIENCE MAP: STABLE (PARLOUR?)*

He read the last line three times before he understood it.

*(Parlour?)*

Someone had given the room he fed them a name. Someone had noticed, on a screen, that Subject C-7's mind kept returning to the same bright, warm, well-furnished place, and had written it down with a question mark.

Morrison saw where the tablet had landed. His hand shot out and turned it over. His face had gone the color of wet paper. He did not look at the chair. He did not look at the mirror. He went back to his screens and his hands, for the rest of the session, did not stop shaking.

Isaiah let his eyes stay where they were, fixed on the ceiling, and walked the machine calmly back into the parlour, and sat it down, and gave it pepper soup, and did not think about the stairs.

Afterwards, in his cell, he carried the header down to the cellar and put it on the shelf beside Subject 2471.

*Project Continuity. C-7. Witness.*

*If I am seven, someone is one.*

---

He began the census that night.

He had been doing it in pieces for weeks, but now he did it properly, the way he would have laid out the characters at the start of a long investigation. He walked through the atrium of Wing C in his mind, cell by cell, and asked of each occupant the same questions: when did they come, and when will they go?

Most of Wing C, he realised, came and went.

In seven weeks he had watched at least twenty people pass through its cells. Overflow from the general wards: frightened, ordinary-looking citizens who spent a few nights in a glass box and were then moved on, somewhere else, and not seen again. The woman on the floor with her legs out in front of her had been there when he arrived. The man with his palms against the glass had come in November. The boy in 7-G, Samuel, had been there longer than anyone could tell him.

But some of them stayed. And those were the ones he was interested in.

*Three brothers. Identical. 7-A, 7-B, 7-C.* In the treatment corridor, on the wall outside the orderlies' station, there was a whiteboard for the day's work details, and he read it every time he passed. *VOLKOV, D. — GARDEN. VOLKOV, A. — LIBRARY. VOLKOV, N. — TECHNICAL.* They had been here since June, according to an orderly's grumbling about their privilege forms. Before that, somewhere else. A transfer.

*A small woman. 7-D. C-6.* A doctor. *Dr. Tanaka—sorry, C-6.* Since August.

*A thin man. 7-H.* Isaiah had recognised him the day he arrived in Wing C. There had been a small item in the *Review* in early October, below the fold on page nine, that Isaiah had read over his coffee and thought about for a whole afternoon: a Junior Historian at the Regional Factorepo, Marcus Chen, who had reported his outlaw lover to the enforcers and been convicted anyway, on exposure. *Loyalty no defence*, the headline had said. Isaiah had meant to follow it up. He had been arrested before he could. And now here was Chen, three cells along and across, sitting on his bed with his head in his hands.

*A woman. 7-K.* Newly arrived. *Is Osman in two or four today?* And that name had pulled something out of the study drawers. Years ago, as a young reporter, he had covered the formal opening of the new Factorepo Spire. There had been a press list. Among the architects credited with the security infrastructure, a young name he had noted because she was the youngest on the list by six years: *K. Osman.*

The system's own builder, now in the system's prison.

*And me. 7-E. C-7.* The man who had been counting fact-storm deaths.

---

Seven.

He sat with the number in the dark.

Seven people who did not come and go. Seven people who had arrived at Ashford over the course of about six months, from completely different places, for completely different offences, and been put in the same small wing of glass and silence and *kept*.

A true believer, who had done his duty and been punished for it.

An architect of the factorepo itself.

Three brothers, one of them, if the overalls and the garden detail meant what he suspected, a former enforcer.

A doctor.

A journalist.

It was not a random group. Twenty years had taught him to distrust random groups. It had the look of something else. Of a *sample*. As if someone had gone out into Veridica with a list of the kinds of minds that might make trouble for a system like this one, and collected one of each.

*Why?*

He did not know yet. But he had spent his whole career in the space between *I don't know yet* and *I know*, and he was not afraid of it. He was only very tired.

---

It came to him at about three in the morning, which was when things usually came to him.

He was lying on his back in the dark, not sleeping, walking the cellar shelves as he did every night: checking that everything was still there, touching each item as he passed, the way his aunt used to touch the silver before a dinner. The Continuity header. The storm onset times. The death notice of the retired schoolteacher. The procurement leak.

Page thirty-one.

He took it down off its shelf and read it again, as he had a hundred times. The column of numbers. The column of outcomes. *Recovered. Partial. Partial. Recovered.* And that single line, alone on its shelf.

*Subject 2471 — lethal threshold confirmed.*

And he stopped, because for the first time he let himself read the rest of the line.

He had always stopped at the outcome. It was the story: that line was the story, the proof that someone had been measuring how much contradiction a human mind could bear before it broke, and had found out. He had never paid attention to the cramped column at the far left of the page, where each number was followed by a surname and an initial, in the shorthand of a procurement clerk who had not expected anyone to read it.

He read it now.

*2471 — VOLKOV, I.*

Isaiah lay very still.

Then he turned his head on the thin pillow and looked out through the glass, across the dim atrium, at the triangle.

Three men lay asleep in three cells. Three identical faces turned up to three white ceilings, calm in the low blue light. They had come here from the border. One of them had been an enforcer. They had the same sharp features and the same gray-blue eyes, and their surname was on the whiteboard outside the orderlies' station, and it was on a shelf in his cellar next to the proof of a murder.

*Volkov, I.*

Their father, he thought. It had to be their father.

He was lying in a glass box, in a silent building, in a system that had arrested him for asking exactly this kind of question, thirty metres from three men who might never know how their father had died. He had the proof. He had carried it down the stairs and kept it safe from the machine through fourteen sessions.

And he had no way in the world to tell them.
