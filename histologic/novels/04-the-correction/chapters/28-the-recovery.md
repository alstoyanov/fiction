# Chapter 28: The Recovery

*August 22–23, 2106. The Safe House, the Border Zone*

---

Three weeks after the storm, Kira Osman could still not write a line of code without saying it out loud.

She sat at the scarred table under the high broken windows of the factory with a borrowed tablet in front of her, one of Elena's, and tried to write a simple filter for the resistance's radio traffic. Twelve lines. She could have written it at the Spire in four minutes, half asleep. She typed the first line and heard her own voice, low and flat, reciting it.

*"Filter input. Band three. Threshold minus forty."*

She stopped.

She typed the second line. Her lips moved. *"Discard below threshold. Log above."*

She put her hands flat on the table and made herself stop. She typed the third line in silence, with her jaw clenched, and it took her four minutes, and when she read it back it was wrong.

*Hide what you love in what you've memorised.* It had saved her. For eight months she had taken everything she could not afford to lose and folded it into specifications and recited them in the white chair until the machine skimmed past. And now the reciting would not stop. It had worn a groove in her. When she touched anything technical, anything with a format, the vault opened by itself, and her mouth began to say it.

She had not told anyone. She had thought, for three weeks, that she was the one who had come through clean.

She sat at the table with her hands flat and understood that there was no such thing.

---

There were nine of them in the factory, and all nine were broken, and they were learning to live in the same room with it.

Tanaka flinched at doors. Any door, any sliding sound, any hiss of air. The factory's steel door had a pneumatic closer, and every time it shut she went very still and her hand went to the bandage at her right temple, where the scar was healing in a thin pale line, and it took her a moment to come back. She was the only doctor among them. She treated everyone else with enormous, patient competence. She could not treat that.

Morrison did not sleep. He sat up at night by the stove with a blanket round his shoulders and looked at his hands. Sometimes Kira woke at three in the morning and saw him there, holding them out in front of him in the dim orange light, turning them over, as if checking they were still his.

Nikolai read the primer every night. *Arithmetic for Young Citizens, Book Two*, the one with the roof in it. He had brought it out of Wing C in the front of his overalls. He sat on his mattress in the corner and held each page up to the light, and read it, and read it again. It did not come back. He read it anyway.

Dmitri slept in his boots. Every night. On top of the blanket, fully dressed, with his back to the solid wall and his face to the door.

Alexei was the most normal of them, which in its own way was the most worrying. He talked. He cooked. He went out with Elena's people and came back with news and bread and jokes. And sometimes, in the middle of a sentence, he would stop and look out of the window at the lake, toward the lakeshore road, and not finish.

Old Songs sang now. Every evening. He had not stopped since the dawn they arrived.

And Marcus.

---

Marcus struggled most. She had known he would.

He had come out of Ashford with holes in him that would never be filled, and now that he was out, now that there was no camera to perform for and no white chair to fear, he could feel every one of them. He would be talking and reach for a word and find it gone. He would start to tell a story about the fourteenth floor and lose the end of it. He would sit for an hour by the window, not moving, and when she asked him what he was thinking he would say *I don't know*, and it was true.

"They almost erased me," he said to her, the first week, sitting against the wall. "Part of me *is* still erased. There's so much I can't remember."

He sat with his head in his hands sometimes, still. The shape of a broken man. But it was different now. In Wing C it had been emptiness, or the performance of emptiness. Here it was grief.

In the second week he showed her the list.

He had been keeping it on a sheet of paper from one of Elena's notebooks, in pencil, in his small precise historian's hand. It was written as fact entries. Of course it was.

*Item: mother's first name. Status: lost. Verified October–November 2105.*

*Item: three versions of Elena's last words. Status: lost.*

*Item: the colour of the plaza dress. Status: lost.*

*Item: the first conversation with K., the library, January 5, 2106. Status: lost (May 27, 2106). Record reconstructed from K.'s testimony.*

It went on for two pages. Small things and large things, all in the same format, all in the same even hand. *Lost. Lost. Lost.*

She read it sitting beside him on the mattress and did not cry, because he was watching her face.

"Then we remember for you," she said at last. "That's what we do now. We remember for each other."

She had a list too. She had been keeping it since June, since the night he asked her to tell him the library again. In her head, in the vault, folded into protocol 7.3. The things she remembered *about* him that he no longer could. The way he had held the book about reservoirs upside down. What he had said about the record following the fact. The exact sound of the laugh he had turned into a cough on the garden bench in January, the first time.

She read it to him. All of it. It took an hour.

When she finished he did not say anything for a long time. Then he took the pencil and, at the bottom of his own list, under *the first conversation with K.*, he wrote a new line.

*Status: held by K.*

---

It was the field that broke it open.

It was the twenty-second, a Sunday, late. The others were asleep or pretending to be. Kira and Marcus were sitting on their mattress under the broken window, with a blanket over their knees, looking at the stars over the lake, because the border zone was dark and the sky here was full of them in a way it never was in the capital.

Marcus had been quiet all evening. She had thought he was tired. He had been, it turned out, trying to comfort *her*.

"You've been sad," he said. "Since Ashford. Since the data centre."

She didn't answer.

"I know why," he said. "I've been thinking about it too." He was looking up at the stars. "But at least we had the field."

She turned her head.

"That night," he said. "Before the cars. Before the gate. When the storm made the building into a field and we—" He smiled a little. "We lay in the grass. You were counting the stars. I said you'd never finish and you said it wasn't about finishing." His voice was very gentle. "Whatever else he took, whatever he did, we had that. That was ours. That was real."

Kira did not move.

*There was no field.*

There had been a cell, and her hands on his face, and a corridor, and the atrium full of people screaming, and a tunnel, and the data centre, and Reeves on the floor, and the tunnel mouth, and Moss's hand on the alarm, and a service road, and a gate. She had been with him every second. She had dragged him by the arm. She had never, at any point in the whole of that night, lain down in any grass.

"Marcus," she said. "There wasn't a field."

He looked at her.

"There wasn't," she said. "I'm sorry. We never—we were in your cell. Then the corridor. I was pulling you. We never stopped. We never lay down anywhere." She heard her voice shake. "It didn't happen."

"It did," Marcus said.

He said it simply. Not arguing. The way you say the date of the Founding.

"It did. I remember it. The grass was wet. It came through my shirt. There were crickets. You were counting under your breath and your hair was on the grass, and you turned your head and smiled at me." He looked at her. "You said, *does it matter?*"

---

Tanaka said it in the morning, with her clinical voice, and Kira watched her choose every word.

"Storm-induced confabulation," she said. She was sitting at the table with a cup of Elena's terrible tea between her hands. "It's documented. I saw it in the trauma wards. Border patients who came through a heavy storm with gaps in their memory, and afterwards the gaps were full. The storm pours in contradictory material and the mind builds something out of it, something coherent, something that fits the gap, and then—" She paused. "Then it sets. It stops being the storm and becomes a memory. It feels exactly like one. There's no difference, from inside."

Marcus sat across from her very still.

"You're telling me it isn't real," he said.

"I'm telling you it didn't happen," said Tanaka gently. "That isn't quite the same thing. But yes." She put the cup down. "Marcus. You'll have to check your memories against other people's now. The ones from that night. Probably others. For the rest of your life."

He didn't say anything.

Kira watched it go into him. And she understood, watching, that for him this was worse than the erasure.

In Wing C, he had known what was missing. The holes had been holes. He could feel their edges; he could write them on a list. *Lost. Lost. Lost.* That was terrible, but it was clean. It was a record with gaps in it.

This was a record with a lie in it. A false entry, in his own hand, that he could not feel was false. The man who had spent his whole life saying *the facts are the facts*, who had believed the record was sacred, had just learned that there was a fact in his own head that had never happened, and that he would never, from inside, be able to tell.

He got up from the table and went outside. She let him go.

---

He came back at noon and sat down beside her on the mattress, and said, without looking at her, the thing that neither of them had said aloud for three weeks.

"Reeves said he made this."

Kira was very still.

"The file says he set it up," Marcus said. "The cell. The sessions. The library. I read it over your shoulder. *Selected for compatibility.*" His hands were clasped between his knees, hard. "What if he's right?"

She had been waiting for it since the data centre. She had asked him herself, on the service road, *was I sent to you?* And he had not answered, because he could not.

"I don't know," she said.

"I don't know which parts of me that love you are the old me," he said. "Or the new me. Or his experiment." His voice was very low. "There's a field in my head that never happened, and I'd have sworn to it in front of The Judge. How can I trust any of it?"

"Neither do I," she said. "Know. Which parts."

They sat side by side on the mattress. Outside, Old Songs was humming by the lake.

---

"In the corridor," Marcus said, after a long time. "In December. At the junction. The first time. I said *I know you*." He looked at his hands. "I still don't know how. I didn't know you. I'd never seen you. I've thought about it for eight months and I still don't know what that was."

Kira looked at him.

"Maybe you didn't know me," she said slowly. "Maybe you just *decided* to."

He turned his head.

"Maybe that's what it was," she said. "Not recognition. A decision. Something in you that was still there, at the bottom, under everything, looked at a stranger in a corridor and decided *her*." She swallowed. "And maybe Reeves put me in the corridor. Maybe he put me in the cell across from yours. He did, I think. I think the file's true. But he didn't make you decide."

Marcus said nothing. But he was listening. She could feel him listening, the way she had felt it in the library.

"Reeves arranged a room," Kira said. "That's all. That's all a file can prove. He arranged a room, and he put two people in it, and he watched." Her voice was stronger now. "He didn't arrange what we did in it. He didn't tell me to write down the library for you, line by line, because you'd lost it. He didn't tell me to learn your fact entry so I could say it back to you in a storm. He didn't tell me to hold your hand under the bench." She was looking straight at him now. "I *chose* that. Every day. In a glass box, with a camera on me, knowing what they'd do if they saw. I chose it. And I'm choosing it now."

Marcus looked at her for a long time.

"Then I'll choose it too," he said. "Every day. So that it's mine. Not his."

It was not proof. She knew that. There would never be any proof. There was no line in any record in the world that could settle it, not the Registry and not the factorepo and not R-2.

But it did not need to be proved. That was the thing she understood, sitting on a mattress in a broken factory in the border zone at noon, with the lake light coming through the windows. It was the answer to Reeves. It was the answer to the whole of Continuity, to *erase, then seed*. A belief that someone else has planted in you is manufactured, whatever it feels like. But a belief you keep choosing, every day, with your eyes open, knowing that it might have been planted, is *yours*.

That was the only kind of genuine there was. Maybe it always had been.

---

"And the field?" said Marcus, later.

It was evening. The stars were coming out over the lake again. They were lying on the mattress, side by side, not touching, looking up through the broken window.

"Should I try to forget it?" he said. "Tanaka says I can't. But if I could."

Kira thought about it.

"No," she said. "Keep it."

He turned his head.

"Keep it," she said. "Just file it right. You're a historian. You know how to file things." She looked up at the sky. "Not a fact. A hope."

He was quiet.

"We'll go and lie in a real field one day," she said. "When this is over. When there's somewhere we can lie down without anyone coming for us in the morning. We'll find one with crickets. And I'll count the stars, and you'll tell me I'll never finish." She swallowed. "And then it'll be true. It'll just have happened in the wrong order."

He did not answer for a long time. Then he reached out in the dark and found her hand.

"I don't know if I can do this," he said. "Any of it. Everyone keeps talking about what comes next. Elena. Dmitri. Reforming Veridica. Exposing it. Fixing it." He laughed, very softly, without much in it. "I don't even remember who I was."

"Then we'll figure out who you are now," said Kira. "Together."

---

It was not a triumph. She would think about that often, afterwards. It was not a kiss on a bridge at sunset, with the city watching. It was two broken people on a mattress in a condemned building, both of them damaged, neither of them knowing how they had got here or what had been done to them to get them here, choosing each other anyway. With their eyes open. With all of it.

It was the realest thing she had ever done.

---

Old Songs sang that evening, as he did every evening.

He sat on his crate by the window with the lake going dark behind him and sang to the whole room. The others came in from outside and sat where they could, on mattresses, on the floor, against the wall. Elena stood by the stove. Morrison had his blanket round his shoulders. The brothers sat in their corner, three in a row.

It was the lullaby.

*Hush now, the river is long.*

Kira had heard it before. Marcus had told her, in the garden in January, with his hand over his mouth: his mother, a fever, rain on a window. She watched him now across the room. He had gone very still.

Old Songs finished the first verse. And paused, for breath, the way he always did, with the lift at the end of the line like a question.

And into the pause, from the mattress under the window, very quietly, a little off-key, Marcus began the second verse.

The whole room turned to look at him.

He did not seem to notice. His eyes were closed. He sang it all the way through, softly, word for word, the words of a verse he had not known he knew until his mouth began to make them. And Old Songs, on his crate, listened with his head on one side and his pale eyes very bright, and at the end of the verse took up the third, and Marcus fell silent, and let him.

Nobody could tell whether he had remembered it or made it up. Kira looked at Tanaka, and Tanaka looked back at her and shook her head, very slightly. *I don't know.*

Nobody minded.

---

Late that night, when the others were asleep, Kira lay awake on the mattress and watched Marcus sleep beside her.

He slept on his side, facing her, his glasses folded on the floor by his head. His face was very young in sleep. The lines that Ashford had put into it were softer. He looked like the photograph from the news item in October, over her breakfast, a lifetime ago: a young man in a suit outside the Enforcement Center, earnest and a little frightened.

She thought about the Spire. The sixtieth floor, the holographic displays, the city spread out below like a circuit board of light. Her own voice, clear and certain, speaking a fact into the permanent record. *Timestamp twenty-two hundred hours, November eighteenth.* Watching it verify. Watching it pulse green.

She had not entered a fact since.

She did it now. Not for any factorepo. Not for anyone. Nothing would verify it. No system in the world would ever record it. She said it into the dark, in a whisper, so quietly that even Marcus, a hand's width away, did not wake.

"Kira Osman," she said. "August twenty-third, 2106. Twenty-three fifty hours. Border zone." A breath. "With him."

She was silent for a moment.

"By choice," she said.

It did not pulse green. Nothing happened at all. The stars went on over the lake, and Old Songs murmured in his sleep by the window, and the man beside her breathed slow and even in the dark.

She knew it was true.
