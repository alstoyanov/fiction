# Chapter 20: The Impossible Plan

*June 20 – July 16, 2106. Ashford Correctional Facility*

---

Dmitri Volkov had spent three years planning how to catch the most careful outlaw in the border zone, and it turned out to be excellent preparation for planning how to escape from a neural correction facility with him.

"You're enjoying this," Alexei whispered in the corridor on the second morning.

"I am not enjoying it."

"You are. I can feel it. You're like a dog with a map."

Dmitri did not answer. But he did not deny it either, and through the bond he felt his brother's delight, warm and bright, and he let it stay.

---

He laid it out in the garden, because the garden was where he could think.

Not on paper. In stones. The beds along the north wall were lined with them, smooth gray lake stones, and he had spent five months resetting them in patterns only seven people could read. Now, over two weeks, working his way along the beds with his rake and his blank face, he built a map.

The long stones along the north bed were the east building. The short row by the third bench, Wing C. A white stone at the corner was B-4. A cairn by the tool shed was the vestibule. The gravel path was the service road. And the apple tree, gnarled and bare-rooted at the end of the garden, was the fence line, and everything beyond it.

Marcus, on his bench, could see all of it. So could Kira, Tanaka and Isaiah. In the four-minute gaps, Dmitri knelt among the beds and moved a stone, and spoke, and they learned the building as he had learned it, piece by piece.

---

*What do we need,* he wrote in the first primer, on June twentieth. *Five things. I will go through them one at a time. Do not answer until I have finished.*

*One. A distraction. Massive. Undeniable. Something that takes every person in this building out of their ordinary minds at once, so that no one is watching the doors.*

*That is the storm.*

Elena's people had the unit. He did not ask how. He knew roughly what it would look like, because he had seen them at the border: a vehicle-mounted transmitter, tuned to broadcast on factorepo frequencies, loaded with contradictory facts about a single location. At the border it was used to *test*. Sokolov's programme. They drove it into a district, set it running, and broadcast a false history of the district into every interface in range on top of the true one, and watched what happened. Every head in the street receiving two truths at once, and unable to hold either.

Nikolai would give Elena the frequencies of Ashford's local node. Elena's people would park the unit outside the fence. And for twenty minutes every interface in the building, staff and patients alike, would receive three incompatible histories of the building they were standing in.

*Two. Inside help.* The Director: duty rosters, orders, signatures. Morrison: lockdown release and cradle shutdown. Nikolai: the building's systems. Old Songs: signals, in sound, in a building where no one else could be heard.

And one more thing, under Nikolai's name, that Dmitri had not known until Morrison mentioned it in the corridor.

*Isolation.*

"Correctional facilities are shielded," Morrison had breathed, passing. "You didn't know? Against outside broadcasts. During storms. We can cut the whole building off from external transmission in seconds. It's automatic. The moment the monitors detect a conflict above threshold, the shielding comes up." A pause. "If your aunt's storm hits the fence and the shielding comes up, nobody inside will feel a thing."

So Nikolai would have to take the shielding down. And keep it down. From B-4, at the moment the storm began, against a system that would try, automatically, every few seconds, to put it back up.

*It's only software,* Nikolai wrote in the margin of that primer. *I've fought worse.* But his hand had been shaking. Dmitri could see it in the pricks.

*Three. The evidence.* Nikolai's partition, copied fragment by fragment since February. And the raw archive, in the data centre in the basement of the east building, where the Continuity directory was physically stored. It would need to be copied by hand, onto a drive, during the storm. Kira would break the encryption. Nikolai would strip the drives.

*Four. An exit.* The service road ran round the back of the east building to a gate in the fence that was used for deliveries. Beyond it, a lane, a field, and a road. Elena's vehicles would be waiting on the road. Then east, through the night, toward the border zone and the safe houses Alexei knew.

*Five. Outside support.* Elena.

---

*There is a sixth problem,* Tanaka wrote back, three days later. *You have not listed it. I think you know what it is.*

He did.

The interfaces.

Every one of them had a mesh in the brain, installed at eight years old, and every mesh reported to the Registry. Location, vital signs, cognitive status, all the time, in a thin constant stream of telemetry that nobody ever thought about, because nobody could remember a time before it. It was how the enforcers found outlaws. It was how the border patrols tracked crossings. It was, he had once explained to a room of new recruits, the reason escape from Veridica was impossible: *you can leave your house and your name and your family, but you cannot leave your own head.*

The moment seven Wing C subjects crossed the fence line, seven interfaces would tell the Registry exactly where they were. And they would go on telling it. On the road. In the border zone. In the safe house. Forever.

*We cannot switch them off,* Nikolai wrote. *No one can. Removal kills. You know that.*

*Then we don't switch them off,* Tanaka wrote. *We make them say something else.*

---

It came in the second meeting, on the night of July second, in B-4, with the cameras down for another firmware update that Nikolai had found an excuse to need.

They stood round the workbench as before. Tanaka spoke quietly, with her hands folded, the way Dmitri imagined she had once spoken to families in the corridor outside an operating theatre.

"In a fact storm," she said, "interfaces fail. Not always. But often. The telemetry goes erratic. Vital signs spike, cognitive status drops, and in the worst cases it goes to zero. We see it in the trauma wards after every border storm. A patient comes in and the Registry has already logged them as a cognitive failure, and sometimes it's wrong, and sometimes it isn't." She paused. "In a storm like the one your aunt is going to make, a storm right on top of the building, the Registry will expect failures. It will be *logging* failures. Dozens of them. Staff and patients."

"So," said Alexei slowly.

"So in the middle of it," Tanaka said, "if seven more interfaces report *cognitive failure: flatline*, the Registry will believe them. Why wouldn't it?" She looked at Morrison. "Can it be done?"

Morrison had gone white. Then, slowly, the white went out of his face and something else came into it, something Dmitri had not seen there before. Interest. A technician's interest in a problem.

"Spoofed," he said. "Yes. If Nikolai can get into the telemetry relay from B-4, and I give him the signature of a real flatline, from one of the long-term care files, then yes. It'd have to be at the peak. When it's most believable. And it'd have to hold, afterwards. We'd have to keep the relay reporting zero for as long as..." He stopped.

"For as long as we're alive," said Nikolai quietly.

"And I'll certify the deaths," said the Director.

They all looked at her.

"Cognitive failure during a fact storm," she said. "Remains processed according to standard protocols. No family viewing." Her voice was very dry. "I've signed that form a hundred times. I know exactly what it says."

Dmitri felt his brothers go still beside him. He knew what they were thinking, because he was thinking it too. A datapad sliding through a slot at the border facility. *DECEASED. COGNITIVE FAILURE. NO FAMILY VIEWING PERMITTED.*

"The Registry will record seven facts," said Isaiah softly. "*Deceased*."

"Six," said the Director.

Isaiah looked at her. She looked back. Neither of them said anything more, and Dmitri, who had spent his life reading faces across interview tables, filed it and did not ask.

---

"There's another problem," Morrison said, before they left. "You need to know it."

He was looking at Marcus.

"The storm will hurt everyone," he said. "That's what it's for. But it won't hurt everyone equally. It gets in through the gaps. Where a mind is uncertain. Where there's damage, missing links, things that have been taken out." He swallowed. "Mr. Chen's salience map looks like a city after a bombing. There are more gaps in it than anything else. He'll be the most vulnerable mind in the building."

Marcus said nothing. Kira, beside him, had gone very straight.

"And the brothers," Morrison said, turning to them, "will be the least. If I'm right about the bond. If you can hold on to each other, the storm will have trouble getting a grip on any one of you." He hesitated. "In a storm, you'll be the only stable thing in the building. People will follow you. Whether you want them to or not."

---

Marcus struggled with the plan. Dmitri watched him struggle and did not know how to help.

It was too many layers. The storm, the shielding, the lockdown, the telemetry, the evidence, the route, the timing. It was a plan Dmitri himself could hold only because he had spent his life holding plans like it, and Marcus Chen had had most of his mind rubbed flat six months ago. In the garden, in the four-minute gaps, Dmitri would move a stone and explain, and Marcus would nod, and the next day in the primer he would ask a question that showed he had lost half of it again.

It was Kira who solved it. Dmitri saw her do it, from the beds, and understood with a small shock that it was the most intelligent piece of teaching he had ever witnessed.

She stopped explaining the plan to Marcus as a plan. She turned it into a list of facts.

*August ninth, twenty-three forty hours. Location: Wing C, 7-H. Event: subject waits. Signal: the humming. Verified.*

*August ninth, twenty-three forty-seven hours. Location: Ashford, all areas. Event: fact storm onset. Verified.*

*Location: Wing C, atrium. Event: lockdown release. Doors open. Verified.*

*Location: service tunnel, east building. Involved parties: seven. Event: descent to data centre. Verified.*

One line at a time. In the format he had typed ten thousand times on the fourteenth floor. And Marcus took each line into the vault where he kept everything that mattered, and filed it there, and by the second week of July he could recite the whole escape in order, start to finish, as if it had already happened. As if it were history.

Dmitri, raking, listened to him do it in the garden one afternoon, in a flat low murmur to the sky, and felt something he had not expected to feel about this man. Not pity. Respect.

---

*Sokolov,* he wrote in the primer on July eighth. *Someone will ask. So I will say it once, and then we will not discuss it again.*

*No part of this plan relies on Captain Sokolov. She arrested us. She ran the programme that killed our father. She delivered us here. When Border Security is called out after the storm, it will be her people, and very probably her. She will pursue us with everything she has. She arrested us once. She will do it again.*

Alexei's reply came back the same evening, in a volume of poetry, which meant *no news*, except that it wasn't.

*She also looked at the monitors for a long time. You heard her. Or the solution.*

Dmitri wrote back:

*Then let it be a surprise. Not a strategy.*

---

*Old Songs comes with us,* he wrote, on July eleventh.

Nobody had asked. He wrote it anyway.

*He taught us to survive. Without him, my brothers and I would be what we were pretending to be. He has kept songs in his head in this building for longer than any of us have been alive. He dropped four litres of soup on a floor for us.*

*We do not leave him.*

The reply came back from all six. The same word, in six different hands.

*AGREED.*

---

*The date,* he wrote, on July fourteenth, in the last primer before everything changed. *The night of August ninth. Storm onset 23:47. Reeves will be at the Ministry that night for the pre-demonstration briefing; the Director has seen the travel order. The triple cradle is scheduled for August tenth, 08:00. We will be gone eight hours before it.*

*We have three and a half weeks. It is enough. It is a good plan.*

He looked at that last line for a long time before he pricked it. He had written a great many plans in his life. He had never before written that sentence about one.

But it *was* a good plan. It had margin. It had redundancy. It had seven people inside who knew what they were doing, two staff, an old man with a trolley, a Director with a pen, and an aunt outside with a storm. For a few days, in the middle of July, walking out to the garden in the warm mornings with his rake over his shoulder, Dmitri Volkov let himself believe in it.

He had not believed in anything since the checkpoint in the fog.

It felt, he thought, a little like being young.

---

It was July sixteenth. A Friday. Warm. The roses were in full bloom along the north wall, dark red and heavy, and the air in the garden smelled of them.

He was kneeling at the bed by the third bench, deadheading, when he felt it.

Not a pressure. A lurch. As if the ground had tilted. As if someone had reached into his chest and grabbed.

*Kolya.*

He did not look up. He was a corrected man. Corrected men do not look up when nothing has happened. He kept his hands among the roses and his face blank and his eyes on the petals, and he let the bond fill him, and it filled with fear. Not the ordinary worry that Nikolai carried everywhere like a coat. Pure, sudden, cold, animal fear, the kind that comes before thought.

Across the garden, through the glass wall of the main building, he could see the east corridor, the long bright passage that led from the technical basement to Wing C.

Nikolai was walking along it.

Not with the usual orderly. With two men Dmitri had never seen before, tall, in the dark gray of the Continuity team, one on each side of him, close, their hands not quite on his arms. And in front of them, leading, her tablet held against her chest like a shield, Supervisor Hayes.

Hayes, who did not meet anyone's eyes. Who was not meeting anyone's eyes now. Whose face, even through two panes of glass and thirty metres of garden, looked sick.

They were walking Nikolai toward Wing C. Toward the treatment rooms.

It was not a session day.

---

Dmitri knew. He did not need to be told. He had spent his whole life in rooms where men were walked between two other men toward a door, and he knew what it looked like when the charge was already written.

*Every access is logged somewhere.* Nikolai had said it in February, in the primer, with his hand shaking. *The only question is whether anyone looks.*

Someone had looked.

Through the bond, faint and far and frantic, like a voice shouting through a wall, he felt Nikolai reach for him. Not words. Weight. And under the fear, a single thing, very clear, pressed outward as hard as his brother had ever pressed anything:

*They know it was me. They don't know what I took.*

The east camera ticked slowly round toward the benches. The orderly by the gate turned a page on his tablet. On the third bench, Marcus Chen sat with his hands in his lap and his face turned to the sky, and did not know. None of them knew. In the library, far across the building, Dmitri felt Alexei stop dead in the middle of the stacks, with a book in each hand, and understand.

He watched his brother walked along the corridor between two strangers until the corridor turned and he could not see him anymore.

He had planned everything. He had planned the storm and the shielding and the telemetry and the tunnels and the route and the date. He had built it in stones, one at a time, with margin, with redundancy, with three and a half weeks to spare. And every single piece of it had assumed one thing, so deeply that he had never even thought to write it down.

That they would choose the hour.

Dmitri Volkov knelt among the roses with his blank face and his steady hands, and understood that Reeves had just chosen it for them.
