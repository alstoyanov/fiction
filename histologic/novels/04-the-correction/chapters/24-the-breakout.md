# Chapter 24: The Breakout

*August 2, 2106, 00:00 – 01:20. Wing C, the Service Tunnels, and the Data Centre*

---

Kira stood in the doorway of 7-G and watched Marcus kneel in front of a man who did not know he was there.

The atrium roared behind her. She could not hear it; the soundproofing had failed with the doors, and now there was too much sound to hear, a single enormous wash of voices and footsteps and weeping, like surf. The walls came and went. *Hospital. Prison. Field.* She held on to the doorframe with one hand and to protocol 7.3 with the rest of her mind, *layer one, key exchange, asymmetric, lattice-based*, and watched.

Marcus was on his knees on the white floor. Samuel sat on the edge of his bed above him, hands folded, calm.

"Samuel," Marcus said. His voice was rough and very gentle. "We're leaving. The doors are open. You can come with us. Do you understand? You can come."

Samuel looked at him.

He looked at him politely. That was the worst of it. Not blankly: politely. The way a well-mannered man looks at a stranger who has stopped him in the street to ask a question in a language he does not speak. There was a small crease between his eyebrows, as if he were trying very hard to be helpful.

"Is it time for my session?" he asked.

Marcus did not move.

"I don't mind," Samuel said kindly. "I like my sessions. They help." He smiled, a small, sweet, empty smile. "Is it the morning?"

"No," Marcus said. "No, it's—it's night, Samuel. It's night. You don't have to have a session. You can come outside. There's a field."

Samuel's smile didn't change. He looked at Marcus for a moment longer. Then he turned, slowly, back toward the white wall of his cell, and folded his hands in his lap again, and was still.

Kira let go of the doorframe and went in and took Marcus by the arm.

"Marcus."

He didn't get up.

"Marcus, we have to go. We have to go *now*."

"He was the first one I saw," Marcus said. He was not looking at her. He was looking at Samuel's hands. "On the first night. He was frightened, and he put his hand on the glass, and I put mine on the other side. I promised him. I didn't know I was promising, but I did. With my hand."

"I know."

"He doesn't understand what a door is."

"I know," Kira said, and her voice broke, and she hauled him up by the arm with all her strength. "I know. I'm sorry. I'm sorry. Come on."

He came. He looked back once from the doorway. Samuel was facing the wall.

It was the first real grief she had seen on his face in eight months. Not the flatness. Not the terror. Grief, clean and unbearable. And they could not stop for it.

---

The brothers were at the treatment-corridor door with a crowd behind them.

Eleven people, Isaiah had said. There were more now. Patients in gray, a few staff in green, drifting after the three Volkovs the way people drift toward a light in fog. Most of them had no idea where they were going. They only knew that the three men walking together in a straight line were the only thing in the building that looked like it knew.

"We can't take them all," Dmitri said, low. He was holding Nikolai's arm. "We can't. There's no room."

"We can't stop them following," said Alexei.

A woman came up beside Kira. Small, in her forties, with cropped gray hair and a nurse's forearms. Kira knew her by sight from the upper level: 7-R, admitted in April. Overflow, she had thought at the time. But she had never been moved on. *Hanna Marsh*, her cell tag had said.

"You're getting out," Hanna said. Her eyes were wild but they were focused. "Aren't you. You're getting *out*."

Kira looked at Dmitri. Dmitri looked at her.

"Yes," said Kira.

"Take me," said Hanna Marsh. "Please. I'll do what you say. I won't make a sound. Please."

And behind her, a boy, twenty at most, with a narrow, clever face and his gray top on inside out, who Kira knew from 7-N. *Pavel Ionescu*. He had come into Wing C in May and never been moved on either. He did not say anything. He just stood there, shaking, looking at them.

"Two," said Dmitri, after a moment. His face was very hard. "Two. That's all. The rest can't."

*Two*, Kira thought, and did not let herself look at the others.

---

The treatment corridor. The junction. And Morrison, coming out of C-1 at a run, gray-faced, a tablet clutched to his chest, with Old Songs shuffling behind him, humming loudly and tunelessly, a sound Kira had never heard him make. The sound of an old man holding on to a song because it was the only thing in the building that was the same.

"Lockdown's released," Morrison gasped. "Whole east wing. Cameras are down, the storm's taken them. Reeves—" He stopped. "Reeves isn't in his quarters. I checked. I don't know where he is."

"Go," said Dmitri. "Tunnel. Now."

---

The service tunnel ran under the east building from the treatment corridor to the plant rooms and the data centre. It was low and bare and badly lit, with pipes along the ceiling and a floor of rough concrete, and it had been built, Kira thought, long before anyone had thought to make Ashford beautiful. It was the first ugly place she had seen in eight months. She loved it.

They went down it in a line. The brothers first, Nikolai between the other two. Then Kira and Marcus. Then Tanaka, who had caught up, with blood on her hands from somewhere that was not her own. Then Morrison and Old Songs and Hanna and Pavel. And last, walking at his own steady pace, as if he were arriving at a press conference, Isaiah.

The storm followed them underground.

It was not like weather. It did not care about walls. It was in them, in every head, riding the local broadcast node, and down here it came in waves. It had been meant to last twenty minutes, Nikolai had said. Calibrated, it would have been a single clean surge, rising and falling. Uncalibrated, it came and went and came again, and each time it came back it was harder. The tunnel was a tunnel, and a hospital basement, and a field under stars, and a tunnel. Kira kept one hand on the wall and felt the concrete under her fingers, and when the concrete went away she kept her hand where it had been.

"*Dima,*" the brothers called ahead of her, in rhythm. "*Kolya. Lyosha.*"

---

The data centre was a long, cold room of black cabinets in rows, their status lights glowing blue and green in the dark, and it was the most beautiful thing Kira had ever seen.

She knew it. She had never been in it, but she knew it. It was built to the Spire's standards. *Her* standards. The rack layouts, the cable routing, the console at the end with its three screens, the security module above the door with its familiar pattern of indicator lights. She had written half the specification it had been built from. She stood in the doorway for one second and felt, absurdly, as if she had come home.

"There," said Nikolai. "Rows four to seven. Continuity arrays. I'll pull the drives. Kira—"

"I know," she said, and went to the console.

---

It was encrypted, of course. Continuity's archive was the most sensitive data in the building, and it was locked behind a transmission-layer cipher she recognised at once.

She stopped.

She stood in front of the screen with her hands above the keyboard, and she stared at it, and for a moment she forgot the storm and the tunnel and the people waiting behind her, because she was looking at her own work.

It was 7.3.

Not something like it. *Her* protocol. The encryption upgrade she had implemented at twenty-two hundred hours on November eighteenth and spoken aloud into the factorepo and watched verify. The one that had vanished. The one they had told her she had never written. Lattice-based key exchange. Ninety-second rotation. Hash chaining, block size sixty-four kilobytes, chain depth twelve.

They had deleted the fact of it from the factorepo so that she would be convicted.

And then they had quietly taken the protocol itself and used it to lock their own archive.

"Kira?" said Nikolai, from the racks.

She began to laugh.

She could not help it. It came up out of her like something breaking, and she put one hand over her mouth and laughed into it, helplessly, with tears in her eyes, standing in front of the console in the flickering dark. Behind her Marcus said her name, frightened.

"It's mine," she said through her fingers. "It's *mine*. They stole it and then they used it. They used my key to lock their door."

She wiped her eyes with the back of her hand.

"And I know," she said, "exactly where I left the maintenance override."

She had built one in. Every good architect does. A recovery path, documented only in the original specification, for the day some future engineer locked themselves out. It had been on page two hundred and twelve of her design document. It was in her vault, folded into the section on key rotation, beside the Meridian Bridge at sunset.

She typed it from memory. Forty characters. It took her eleven seconds.

The archive opened.

*The stolen fact,* she thought. *Put back to work.*

---

"Six terabytes," Nikolai was saying, pulling drives. "Core first. Aims. Subject files. Protocols. Our session data. Isaiah, the list—"

"I have the list," said Isaiah, at her shoulder. He was reading the directory over her arm as she scrolled it, very fast, his lips moving slightly. "Priority one, the subject files. Priority two—"

She was scrolling the protocols.

*Protocol R-1: Reduction to Baseline.*

*Protocol R-2: Seeding.*

It was not locked. Reeves's private directory, the one Nikolai had never been able to open, the one with its own key, was simply *there*, under her own protocol, in the unlocked archive. The architect of Continuity had trusted the lock on the front door so completely that he had not bothered locking the rooms.

She opened it without meaning to. Her hand did it.

*PROTOCOL R-2: SEEDING.*

*Rationale: Reduction to baseline demonstrates that belief can be removed. It does not demonstrate that belief can be created. Phase Two requires both. R-2 tests whether genuine belief, experienced by the subject as authentic and self-originated, can form in an emptied profile when a compatible attachment figure is introduced under controlled conditions.*

*Method: Following reduction of C-1 to stable baseline, introduce C-2, selected for compatibility with C-1's residual profile (high conscientiousness; strong verification drive; prior attachment disrupted by deception). Placement: 7-K (direct line of sight to 7-H). C-1 maintenance sessions reduced to permit formation. Privileges synchronised (library, garden).*

*Measure: depth and stability of attachment. Degree to which subject experiences attachment as self-originated.*

*Status: active. Results exceptional.*

She read it twice.

She could feel Marcus standing behind her. She could feel his breathing. She did not know whether he had read it. She did not turn round.

She scrolled past it, and kept copying.

---

"Hello, Ms. Osman."

The voice came from the far end of the room, between the racks. Pleasant. Unhurried. Just behind her, always, the way it had been in the white chair.

Every one of them froze.

He was standing at the end of row six with his hands in the pockets of his gray suit. He must have been there all along, in the dark between the cabinets, watching the blue and green lights. His hair was a little untidy. That was all. Apart from that he looked exactly as Marcus had described him: medium height, even features, a face you would forget in a crowd, and light brown eyes that were deeply, affectionately interested in what they were looking at.

"Dr. Reeves," said Kira.

"I thought you'd come here," said Reeves. "It's where I would have come." He glanced round at the others, at the brothers by the racks, at Tanaka, at Isaiah, at Marcus, and nodded to each of them as if greeting guests at a party. "I felt it start, upstairs. I've stood in forty storms, you know, at the border. I find them clarifying." He smiled. "Where do you think we learned what a mind can bear?"

"Stay where you are," said Dmitri.

"Of course." Reeves didn't move. "I'm not going to stop you. I couldn't, could I? There are eleven of you and one of me, and I'm fifty-four." He looked at Kira. "I just wanted to talk to you. Before you go. It seems a pity for you to leave without understanding."

---

"The border storms," Reeves said conversationally. He glanced at the brothers. "You know about those. Your father. 2471. I'm sorry about him, truly. He was very early data. We didn't understand, then, how much we were measuring." He shrugged slightly. "Every storm on that border for fifteen years has been one of ours. They were never weapons, not really. They were a laboratory. You can't build a thing like Continuity without knowing, precisely, how a mind behaves when it's given two truths at once. You need thousands of cases. The border gave us thousands."

"You killed people," said Alexei.

"Yes," said Reeves. "I'm not going to pretend I didn't."

Nikolai, at the racks, had not stopped pulling drives. His hands were shaking, but they kept moving.

"And Phase Two is already approved," Reeves went on. "At Ministry level. The demonstration was a formality. There are carrier signals running in the broadcast network now, in three districts, very low, barely above noise. We're measuring baselines." He looked at Kira again. "Your layer, Ms. Osman. Your transmission architecture. It's beautifully built. It's going to carry Continuity to every head in the country."

"No," said Kira.

"The method is very simple," said Reeves, as if she hadn't spoken. "Everyone always thinks correction is about removing. Taking out the doubt. But anybody can empty a mind. You watched us do it. That's the easy half." He smiled. "The hard half is putting something *back*. Something real. Not compliance. *Belief*. The kind people feel as their own." He spread his hands slightly. "Erase, then seed. Remove the doubt. Plant the loyalty. And the person never knows that anyone was there."

"You're not escaping a prison, Ms. Osman," he said. "You're escaping a *pilot*. The country is the facility."

---

And then he looked at Marcus.

He looked at him for a long time. His eyes were warm. Kira had never seen anyone look at another person with such tenderness.

"Marcus," he said softly. "You read it, didn't you? Over her shoulder. R-2."

Marcus did not answer.

"I was so proud of you," said Reeves. "You have to understand that. You were the best result of my career. Reducing you to baseline was nothing; I've done that dozens of times. Samuel was a baseline. But you. You were *empty*, Marcus. Completely, beautifully empty. And the question I'd waited my whole life to answer was: could something real grow there? Not something I put in. Something that *grew*. That you'd feel as your own."

His voice was very gentle.

"So I stole her fact. I chose her cell. I gave you room: maintenance sessions, weekly, so there was space for it to take. I timed your library hours. I timed the garden. I measured it every week, in the chair, as it went deeper. I watched you hide her in your fact entries and I was so delighted I could hardly speak." He smiled. "Did you think it was *luck*? That the one person who could bring you back just happened to be put in the cell facing yours?"

Kira's hands had stopped on the keyboard.

"What you feel for her," said Reeves, "is my best result. It's the proof of Phase Two. It's the most genuine belief I have ever seen a human being hold." He tilted his head. "And I made it."

---

The data centre was very quiet. The cabinets hummed. The storm came in again, a wave, and the room was a room, and a field, and a room.

Kira did not look at Marcus. She could not. She stood at the console with her hands frozen over the keys and the drive still copying, and she thought, very clearly, the way you think in an emergency, a single thought.

*Is it true?*

The file said he had arranged it. The cell. The sessions. The timing. It was there in the record, in flat administrative language. *Placement: 7-K. Privileges synchronised.* She had noticed the library privileges herself, in January, and dismissed them because she wanted to.

The file did not say he had *made* it. It could not. No file could. There was no line anywhere in any record in the world that could say whether what she had felt, sitting in the garden with her hand under the bench, was hers, or his.

And she understood, standing there, that that was exactly why he was saying it. Because it could not be proved, either way. Because it was the one thing he could still do to them. The last weapon of a man with eleven people between him and his experiment.

Or it was true.

She did not know. She would not, she thought, ever know.

---

"You took my memories," Marcus said.

His voice was low and shaking. He was standing behind her, a little to one side. She still did not look at him.

"You took the library," he said. "You took my mother's name. You took three versions of what Elena said to me." A breath. "You don't get this too."

It did not sound like certainty. Kira heard it, and knew he heard it too. It sounded like hope. Like a man saying something aloud so that it will become true.

Reeves smiled at him, and it was the kindest smile she had ever seen.

"Oh, Marcus," he said. "I already have."

Dmitri hit him.

---

It was not cruel. Kira would think about that later: how precise it was. Dmitri crossed the length of row six in three steps and struck Reeves once, hard, under the ear, the way he had been trained to strike, and caught him as he fell so that his head did not hit the floor. Then Alexei was there with a cable tie from Nikolai's tool belt, and Reeves's wrists were behind him, and his ankles, and he was lying on his side on the concrete between the cabinets, dazed and blinking and, even now, looking up at them with interest.

"Very efficient," he murmured. "Border Security. Of course."

A rack shifted.

It was the storm, or it was Nikolai, pulling one drive too fast, or it was nothing. A tall steel rack at the end of row seven, one of the old ones, not bolted properly, lurched sideways as the room flickered, and toppled, and Tanaka, who had been kneeling by Hanna Marsh against the wall, was in the way.

She threw an arm up. The corner of the rack caught her across the right temple and she went down.

"*Yuki—*"

She was already sitting up when Kira reached her, one hand pressed to the side of her head, blood running between her fingers, her face white and furious.

"It's nothing," she said. "It's a scalp wound. They bleed. Give me something to press on it. *Keep copying.*"

---

The copies went three ways.

Elena's drive, six terabytes, took the core, and Kira watched the bar fill with her heart in her throat. The aims. The subject files. The protocols, R-1 and R-2. The session data for the seven of them. At the last moment, without asking anyone, she added the Ministry correspondence folder, which was small, and which she had just seen for the first time, and which was labelled with a word and a numeral she did not understand and did not have time to think about: *RESTORATION / XI*.

From the console, which connected to the Registry, Nikolai pushed a handful of fragments down through the layers into his old partition. Not much. The subject list. The schedule. The C-1 index line. Enough that if the drive was lost, something would remain.

And the rest, thirty-four terabytes of it, stayed on the arrays.

"That's mine," said Isaiah.

He was standing by the console. He had not sat down all night. He looked, Kira thought, exactly as he had looked on the first day in the garden: tall and straight and dignified, a man arriving at a press conference.

"Go," he said. "All of you. I'll protect the data from here. The Director's channel is open. She opened it from B-4 an hour ago." He put one long hand flat on the top of the console, the way a man puts his hand on a desk he intends to sit at for a long time. "I'll send everything. I'll publish everything."

The intercom above the door crackled.

"*Isaiah.*" The Director's voice. Ragged. Hoarse. The voice of a woman who had been sitting in a field for an hour typing the same override every four seconds. "*The shielding is holding. I can give you forty more minutes. Then I have to let it come up, or they'll know. Tell them to go.*"

"They're going," said Isaiah.

He looked at them.

At the brothers. At Tanaka with her bloody hand. At Morrison and Old Songs, Hanna and Pavel. At Kira. At Marcus.

"There were seven of us," he said. "And we were chosen. I'll make sure they know it."

Then he turned his back on them, and sat down at the console, and began to type.

---

The tunnel mouth was at the north end of the east building, where the service road came round. A steel door, and beyond it, the night.

They were ten metres from it when the lights came on.

Not the storm. Real light, white and hard, from the emergency fittings over the door. And in the light, standing in the tunnel mouth in a dressing gown and slippers, with her short gray hair on end and her face shining wet, was Dr. Reyna Moss.

She must have come down from the staff quarters. She must have followed the sound. Kira saw the storm in her face: her eyes going from wall to wall, wide, as if the tunnel kept changing around her. She had one hand on the frame of the door to hold herself up.

She saw them.

She saw Marcus, and Kira, and the brothers, and Tanaka with blood on her face, and Morrison, and the old man with the trolley, and the two patients from the upper level. She looked at them for a long moment. And Kira watched her understand.

Not the storm. *Them*. What they were doing. Where they were going.

"Oh," said Dr. Moss. "Oh, no. No, Marcus. Yuki."

Her face crumpled.

"You're so ill," she said. "All of you. You're so *ill*, and you don't know it, and if you go out there you'll be lost. The storm will—you'll never come back. You'll never *heal*."

"Reyna," said Tanaka, very gently. "Let us go."

"I can't," said Dr. Moss, and she was weeping. "I'm so sorry. I love you. I can't let you go out there and die."

She reached out, and put her palm flat on the red panel beside the door, and pressed.

The alarm began.

---

It went through the whole building. Kira felt it more than heard it, a deep rising electronic wail that shook the concrete under her feet. Somewhere far above, doors were slamming. Somewhere, a system that had been drowned by the storm for an hour was waking up and beginning to count.

Dr. Moss stood by the panel with her hand over her mouth, crying.

Dmitri did not touch her. He walked past her, and opened the steel door, and the night came in. Warm. Smelling of cut grass.

Kira stepped out onto the service road.

Behind her, Marcus stopped in the doorway and looked back. She turned to look with him.

Ashford rose above them against the sky. It was lit up in every window now, white and gold, alarms sounding, figures moving behind the glass. And its outline was *moving*. The storm was still on it. As she watched, the long white building shimmered and was a hospital, four hundred beds, lit for a night shift. And then a prison, with its fence line and its floodlights. And then, for one long impossible second, nothing at all: a dark field under stars, with crickets, and a line of trees.

And then Ashford again.

He had been almost lost there forever. She knew he was thinking it. She could see it in his face.

"Come on," she said. "We have to go."

He didn't move.

She looked at him. She thought of the file. *C-2, selected for compatibility. Placement: 7-K. Privileges synchronised.* She thought of Reeves on the floor between the cabinets, smiling. *I made it.*

"Was I sent to you?" she said. Very quietly. She had not meant to say it aloud.

Marcus looked at her.

He didn't answer. She did not think he could. She did not think anyone could, ever. He only looked at her for a long moment, in the light of the building that was a hospital and a prison and a field. Then he reached out and took her hand.

His fingers were cold. He held on hard.

They went together down the service road into the dark, and the alarm followed them.
