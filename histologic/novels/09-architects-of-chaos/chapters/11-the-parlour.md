# Chapter 11: The Parlour

*Monday, April 29, 2115. Carver Street, the capital*

---

He arrived at four exactly, because the letter had said four, and because a witness arrives when he is asked and not before, so that he sees the thing as it was arranged to be seen and can say so.

The old eastern quarter had been cleared and rebuilt years ago; he had taken that on the record's word for twenty years and had stood at the end of the new Carver Street in March and confirmed it with his own eyes at last. The Carver Street of his childhood survived nowhere but in his palace. And yet here, at the old number, on the same ground, was a house — new, plain, brick the colour of weak tea, well kept, a low railing, a clean step. The record had not lied about the quarter. She had not lied about the house. *The house is where it always was.* Both true, which was the kind of sentence that had always been the most dangerous thing about her. He had a hand copy of page thirty-nine folded into the lining of his coat, over his heart, where he had kept what mattered since before he had anything that mattered.

She opened the door herself.

Sarah Okonkwo was about eighty. She was upright as a doorframe, silver hair drawn back, and she wore a good dark dress and the particular stillness of a woman who had spent fifty years in rooms where the stillest person won. "Isaiah," she said, as though he had been round last week, and stood aside.

Inside, the parlour was exact.

He stopped on the threshold of it, and for a second the room in front of him and the room he had kept behind his eyes for twenty years were the same room — the long table, the eight chairs, the sideboard, the cups with the thin gold line, the lamp with the fringed shade, the worn rug, every piece where it had stood in a house that no longer existed. She had moved them. When the quarter came down she had taken the room out of the dying house piece by piece and carried it here and set it down again in a new one, and she had done it so exactly that a man who had rebuilt the room from memory in his own mind could walk in and find his memory confirmed down to the lamp. He walked in the way he walked into a press conference, measuring the room as he entered it, because walking in measuring was the only way he knew to walk into a room that could hurt him. He filed the one difference his eye could find and held onto it like a rail: the window faced the other way. In the old house the window had been on the left. Here it was on the right, and the afternoon came in from the wrong side, and that single reversed thing was the only proof his body had that he was not inside his own head.

"There's pepper soup," she said, going to the stove. "Because you always asked."

He took a bowl. Refusing it would have been a statement, and he did not make statements before he had the facts; a man who made a statement had told you where he stood and given up the advantage of being unreadable. The soup was good. He hated that it was good.

Because that was the thing nobody who had not sat across from one of them understood about the Architects, and the thing he was going to spend the rest of the year trying and failing to put into a column that his readers could hold: that they were not monsters, and that their not being monsters was the whole of their power. A monster you could hate cleanly and defeat without cost to your own soul. These people made pepper soup because you had always asked for it. They remembered your childhood accurately and with tenderness. They had done the arithmetic and come out the far side certain and kind, and the certainty and the kindness were real, were not a mask over some cackling cruelty, were the actual texture of the actual people who had designed the cage — and a reader could hate a cackling cruelty and could not, it turned out, hate an old woman's good soup, and the Architects had known that about the people who would one day judge them, had counted on it, had built a confession that would arrive, when it came, with the handle of the teacup turned toward your hand.

---

He laid the hand copy on the table, between them, and unfolded it, and did not sit.

"SO dot oh-four," he said. "Ambassador Sarah Okonkwo. Copy four. Principle Two." He turned the page he had also copied, the one from copy VII that he had carried since September of 2109. "Your principle, from a copy a Chronos historian found. *A system without opposition appears tyrannical; a system with uncontrolled opposition becomes unstable. Let there be several Histologic nations, differing in method and agreeing in substance, and let there be enemies outside them all. Controlled conflict answers every question before it is asked.*" He looked up. "Did you write it?"

She read it the way a person reads a paragraph they wrote long ago and are a little proud of still, her eyes moving along the lines, her head tilting a few degrees. She was not caught. He had come braced for the thing a guilty person does, the flinch or the denial or the sudden warmth, and she did none of it. She read her own sentence as though it were a good piece of work by someone she had once admired.

"I wrote that at thirty," she said. "I'd write it again."

He had his notebook out. He wrote that down.

And then she made her case, the way the old ones did, in short sentences, calm, humane, without heat, and he understood as she spoke that this was what the manuscript's voice sounded like coming out of a living mouth, that the pages had been written by people who talked like this, who had never in their lives twirled a moustache, who believed, who had done the arithmetic and come out the far side of it certain and kind.

She remembered the Fracture Years, she said, and he did not. That was the whole gap between them, and she named it first, the way a good diplomat names the asymmetry before her opponent can. She had been a young woman in the 2050s when shared reality came apart, when every faction had its own facts and the facts had armies, when you could not agree with your own brother about what had happened in your own street the day before, and it had not been an argument, it had been a slaughter, it had been the worst thing human beings had ever done to one another and they had done it with the truth, each side perfectly sincere. And out of that she and the others had built forty years in which the wars were wars of facts and not of blood — in which two nations could hate each other down a lakeshore and the dead could still be counted on the fingers of a few hands a year. The SFW and the CNT had their border wars, yes, and the border wars stayed at the border, by design, kept small, kept useful.

"Controlled conflict saved more lives than it cost," she said. "I did the arithmetic. You never do the arithmetic, Isaiah. You count the dead on one side of the ledger and you will not look at the other side, the column of the dead who were never born into a Fracture, the dead who did not die because we held it. You cannot see them, so you will not count them, and a man who will not count what he cannot see is not a serious person, whatever his byline."

He heard, under it, his own sentence from a white room, the thing he had said to himself to survive Unit 3: *It was arithmetic, in the end.* She had handed him his own survival reasoning as her justification for the thing that had nearly killed him, and he did not think she knew she had done it, and that was the most frightening moment of the afternoon.

---

He asked his questions in order, like an editorial, one clause at a time.

"Did you call? After they took me, in 2105. Did you call anyone."

"No," she said, without a flicker. "You were exactly where a witness should be. Inside. I was not going to spend a thing I had been saving for forty years to pull one clever nephew out of the one place that would finish teaching him." She said it gently. "I was right. Look at you. You are the most trusted voice in this country, and you learned it in Ward Three, and you would not trade the learning back, and you know it."

"Did you arrange my career?"

She smiled, and did not answer, and he filed the smile as an answer he could not print, because a smile was not a quotation and he did not print what he could not quote.

"Why the invitation. Why March."

"Because I am eighty," she said. "And because you were going to read my name by the summer. I did not know how, and I did not need to; it was always going to be read, sooner or later, by someone, and it was obviously going to be you. I wanted you to have had tea with me once, as yourself, before you had tea with my mark." She poured. "I am old enough to want that, and old enough to arrange it."

He noted that she knew the name would be read by summer. He did not know how she knew. He wrote *she knew* in his book and did not decorate it.

"Are you afraid?" he said.

"Of you?" She looked at him with something that was almost tenderness. "You'll publish it. They'll read it. And they will be grateful, in the end, to whoever kept the peace. That is the thing you have never understood about the people you write for, Isaiah. They do not want to have been free. They want to have been safe, and to be told, afterward, that they were free. We gave them both. You are about to take away one of them, and you will tell yourself you did them a kindness." She drank her tea. "Publish it. I mean that. It should be published. I only wanted the tea first."

He named the dead, then, because he had counted them and counting them was the one thing he did that she could not do better. Alexei Volkov. Nora Chen. Omid Farahani. The siege on the coast. The city that came off the dose screaming over a morning. "That was the Battle of Blood," he said. "That was your controlled conflict getting away from you."

"It was more than it should have been," she said, and for the first time her voice had a grain in it. "And fewer than it would have been. Both of those are true, and you will print the first and not the second, because the first is a sentence and the second is an arithmetic, and your readers can hold a sentence and cannot hold an arithmetic." She set her cup down. "I grieve them. You will not believe that, and it is true."

He did not write it down. He noticed that he did not write it down, and he noticed why — that he was afraid it was true, and that if it was true he did not know where to put it — and he made himself note the not-writing in the margin, because an honest witness records his own omissions.

She sat, the whole time, at the head of the table, in the chair, her chair, the one that in his palace she had sat in silent for seven years. He stayed standing until the end.

---

He walked home through the rebuilt quarter in the long spring evening, past the young staked trees, and he did not go into the palace until he was lying on his own bed in his own flat with his shoes on and the lamp off.

Then he went in. The hall. The kitchen, with its sources on their shelves. The cellar door, whose colour he had lost in Unit 3 and would never get back, a door of no colour, his eye sliding off it. And the parlour, exact, warm, the pepper soup steaming, his first byline framed over the fireplace.

Aunt Sarah sat at the head of the table, silent, as she had sat every night since 2108, upright and watching, and in front of her on the dark wood was the teacup, her good china, the one that had appeared there in March, the handle turned to her hand.

He walked the length of the parlour to her. He was courteous, because he was always courteous, even here, especially here, where courtesy was the only law.

"Thank you for the tea," he said. "You can go now."

She looked at him a moment, in the palace, with the expression that measured and gave nothing back; and then she rose, and she took the cup with her, the good china lifting off the dark wood and going with her as she went out through the door that led to the rooms he did not keep; and the place at the head of the table was empty.

He sat down in the chair.

It was the first time in twenty years he had sat at the head of his own parlour table, and the room did not change, and the soup went on steaming, and his byline hung over the fire, and he sat in the chair that had been hers and looked down the length of the table at the empty room, and he found that he could hold it — the arithmetic she could do and the dead he could count, both, one on each side, without setting either down — and that holding both was heavier than either, and was the chair, and was the price of the chair, and he paid it, and sat.
