# Chapter 6: The Man at 14:21

*Thursday, April 4, 2115. Elena's farmhouse, the border zone*

---

Old Songs had gone up to bed before it started.

He had done it deliberately, Marcus understood; he had put the barley away and banked the stove and said goodnight to the kitchen with a nod that took them all in, and had climbed the stairs slowly, hand on the rail, and shut the door of the little room over the kitchen. He did not testify. He had not testified in nine years, since the night in the back room when he told them about Kerr and refused to let anyone write it down, and he would not sit at this table and be a witness to a thing that was theirs to witness. His going up was a kind of instruction. *This account is yours. Write it carefully.*

They were nine around the long table and by the door. Marcus, and Kira beside him. Dmitri stood by the door with his back to the wall, formal, counting something, the way he stood in every room he did not command. Nikolai and Maya together on the bench, Maya's shoulder against his. Elena at the head, in from the yard. Tanaka across from Singh, the clinician across from the case. Isaiah with his notebook open. Kovač with her rimless glasses and her coat still on. Tamsin was at home with a sitter; this was not a room for a child.

Singh sat with her reading glasses on their cord and her sleeves rolled to the elbow and her hands flat on the table, and before she said anything she made a request.

"I would like three people to write this down," she said. "Not one. Three, separately, in their own hands, and then I would like you to compare them, afterward, so that where you three agree is the record and where you differ is noted as differing. I have learned what one account is worth." She looked around the table. "Three."

Kira took a pen. Isaiah already had his. Kovač took the third, and set a clean sheet square before her, and uncapped it, and the three of them wrote through the whole of it, and the scratching of three pens became the sound under her voice.

Marcus understood what she was doing, and why she had insisted on it, and it moved him more than the testimony itself. She had spent four years at the top of a Ministry that kept the one record of a nation, the single authoritative account against which no witness could stand; she had been, in her way, a priest of it; and she had come back, after nine years as a woman that record had erased, to do the opposite thing in a border kitchen with her sleeves rolled up — to say a true thing once, in front of many people, into three hands at once, so that it could never again be only one voice, could never be erased by the failure or the purchase or the death of a single keeper. It was the whole argument of the series made into a procedure. It was the Warning performed. A record kept by one hand is a confession waiting for its torturer; she was making hers by three hands on purpose, in the one house in the country that was in no record at all, and Marcus, who had spent nine years learning the same lesson the hard way, listened to the three pens and thought that he was watching the design answered not with a speech but with a method, which was the only answer it had never found a way to absorb.

---

"My credentials," she began. "Start there, because everyone always starts with the hour, and the hour is not where it starts.

"For four years I was Deputy Minister, and for four years the Minister's private office routed every scheduling request through my login. Every request that touched my brief — meetings, access windows, room bookings, the authorisations a Deputy signs off a dozen times a day — came to me already shaped by his office, and I confirmed it. So did every Deputy. It was how the Ministry ran. It was efficient and it was routine and I never once questioned it, because it was easier not to, and because questioning the thing that makes your day run is the kind of thing you promise yourself you will get to and never do." She paused. "The four entries at fourteen hundred on the fifteenth of November. The window that was offered to the Historian's office as the only free slot. The warning that went to deep cover. The defectors' window. The tip that reached Elena. And the confirmation logged at fourteen oh two, after it was all already over." She spread her fingers on the table. "Every one of those carried my credentials, and not one of them came from me. I would like that written down both ways. It carried my name. It did not come from me. Both. Write both."

Three pens wrote both.

"Thirteen fifty-nine and fifty-eight," she said, and Marcus felt the room settle, because this was the thing, this was the knock that nine years of inquiry had never explained. "Two knocks at the inner side door. Light, unhurried, two of them — the knock the private office always used, so that I would know, from the knock, that it was them and not a petitioner. It was the Minister's private secretary. I will not give you his name because I never knew it well enough to be sure of it, and I will not put a name in your three records that I cannot stand behind. He had the afternoon's folder under his arm, as he did most days at about that hour, and he said the Minister would be grateful if I kept to my desk this afternoon." She almost smiled. "I said I always did. I had just that minute written something in the margin of the folder on my desk, for myself — I will come to it — and my face must have done something, because he looked at me a moment longer than the sentence needed. And then it settled, because there was nothing in it; it was the most ordinary instruction in the world. He gave me three papers that wanted initialling, routine, none of them touching the hour, and I initialled them, and he left by the side door at about fourteen oh five."

"You kept to your desk," said Tanaka, flatly, not a question.

"I kept to my desk," said Singh. "As instructed. Which is why forty people in the district outside could remember me arriving somewhere I never went."

---

"I felt nothing when the hour was erased," she said. "Nobody did. You understand that by now; you have all of you learned it since. There was no moment. The record simply, somewhere, became a record in which an hour had not contained what it had contained, and I sat at my desk and felt the ordinary afternoon, and knew nothing.

"At twenty-three past two by the clock on my wall the side door opened, and this time there was no knock."

She looked at Marcus. "The door log downstairs had a card read at side entrance B at twenty-one minutes past two and seven seconds. Your colonel saw a man at that door at that time." Dmitri, by the wall, did not move. "It is two flights and a corridor from side entrance B to my door. Call it two minutes. The log says fourteen twenty-one. My clock said fourteen twenty-three. They are the same man and the same two minutes, counted from two ends." Marcus wrote the reconciliation in the margin of his own mind, the card below and the clock above, two readings of one climb, and the small ache of nine years' discrepancy closed like a shelf running 113, 115.

"It was Thomas Wei," said Singh. "I knew him by sight. A senior systems architect; we had sat in the same large meetings; I could not have told you the colour of his eyes and I knew his name. Dark jacket. A small tablet in his right hand. And his left hand was bandaged — dressed, properly, white gauze, taped, a dressing that had been put on with care and some time before, not a thing done in a corridor. He came in and shut the door behind him and he said one sentence."

The three pens waited.

"He said: *Die in the record, or die.*"

Nobody in the kitchen moved. Isaiah's pen had stopped; he started it again, and wrote the sentence, and Marcus saw Kira write it, and Kovač, three hands making the one sentence three times on three sheets.

"I understood both halves at once," said Singh. "I was not confused for even a moment, which I have thought about since, because it tells me something about what four years in that building had done to me, that a stranger could say six words and I would understand the whole architecture of the choice I was being offered. Die in the record — cease to exist on paper, become a name with a death beside it, go wherever the unrecorded go. Or die. The actual thing. He was not threatening me. He was not enjoying it. He said it the way a doctor says a diagnosis he wishes were otherwise." She glanced once at Tanaka. "I chose the record. He touched the tablet. On my own screen my Registry status went to zero while I watched it, and behind my ears, I suppose, a signal went flat that has been flat ever since. I felt nothing then either. There was no moment for that, any more than there had been for the hour."

---

"I took my coat," she said. "My gray coat, off the stand, because it was cold and because it was mine and because a person who is about to stop existing reaches for the nearest true thing. We went down the side corridor and the east stairs and out of side entrance B, and then on foot into the district, which was full of the hour. Forty people were out in it, that afternoon, remembering a Deputy Minister arriving at a building she never entered, and we walked through the middle of them, and not one of them saw me, because their afternoon had already been written and I was not in it. We passed two of the Ministry's own staff on the east stairs. Neither looked at me. I was already not there.

"At the edge of the district a car was waiting. I never saw the driver clearly; the light was wrong and I was not, by then, taking in faces. Wei put me in it. He did not say anything else. He turned around and walked back toward the building, and that is the last I saw of him until I read his name in a newspaper years later and understood that the man who built the hour and the man who walked me out of it were the same man." She was quiet a moment. "I was in unaligned territory by nightfall. I have been filed ever since."

"The body," said Tanaka, in the clinician's flat voice, because somebody had to say the word and she was the one who had kept the file.

"I don't know whose it was," said Singh. "When we left, my office was empty. There was no one in it. I do not know who brought the woman in, or when, or whether it was that night or the next day, and I have thought about this more than I have thought about anything else in nine years, because she is the part I cannot get the record to carry." She drew a breath. "In 2113 your Civic Record printed the restored paper day-books of the Ministry's hospitals. I read them in Chronos, the ones that reached me. In one mortuary day-book, for the fifteenth of November, 2106, there is a line. *Unclaimed female, approximately fifty. Released on Ministry order. Thirteen ten.*" The three pens wrote *13:10*. "Released at ten past one. Before the hour began. No name against it. A number. Someone was burned in my name within a day, and she was an unclaimed woman from a Ministry mortuary, and she was moved before any of it started, which means it was arranged before the knock, which means none of it was improvised." She looked at her hands. "I have looked for her name for two years. I will go on looking. She is the one I owe. The rest of you I only have to tell."

---

"The margin note," said Marcus. "You said you'd come to it."

"I wrote it for myself," said Singh, "a minute before the knock. On the corner of the folder on my desk. I don't fully know why; I had been uneasy for a week without being able to say about what. I wrote: *Who restores it? Watch who restores it.*" She said it flatly. "And then for nine years I did. Through newspapers, through your columns, Mr. Okonkwo, through the Chronos press. I watched who restored the record every time it broke. The Historical Integrity Commission in 2107 — its restorers appointed by the Minister. The Concordance. Your RESTORATION. And in 2110 the Commission of Rebuilding, again under the Minister. I kept a list, on paper, in three places, of who restored what. It is a short list. One name is on it more than any other, and it is never quite the hand doing the restoring and always the hand that chooses the hand." She did not say the name. She looked instead, for a moment, across the table, at Marcus. "And mostly, Mr. Chen, I watched you."

Marcus wrote nothing, and nobody glossed it, and the pens went on.

"My part," said Singh, because Isaiah had looked up, the one question he had not asked. "I chose it. I was offered a choice and I took the one that kept me breathing, and I have had nine years alone in two countries to decide whether that was cowardice, and I have not decided. I would rather you wrote that than wrote me a hero or a coward, because I am neither and I am tired of being filed as either. I chose it. Write that."

Isaiah wrote it word for word.

Maya, on the bench, had been silent the whole time, her shoulder against Nikolai's. She said, now, very quietly, the sentence she had written in a journal on the first Saturday of every month for years, true and finished and never retired:

"He didn't do it."

Her father was in prison for a murder that no one had committed, and he would stay there, not for the murder, but for Continuity and for Phase Two, for the things he had done that the record did carry; and Maya knew the difference better than anyone alive, and said the old sentence anyway, because it had been true the whole time and the truth of it did not go away because other things were also true. Nikolai's hand found hers under the table.

"It isn't proof against him," said Kovač, meaning the Minister, the name on Singh's list, carefully. "Not against anyone. It's a witness."

"Yes," said Singh. "That is what I came to be."

---

Marcus unfolded his lists on the table. He turned past CHECKED and UNCHECKED and HOPES, and past *QUESTIONS FOR THE WAR*, to the oldest of them, the entries he had kept in Book 5's winter, numbered, and he found Entry 6, which he had written on a night in 2107 and left deliberately, honestly unfinished: *What happened in the office: unknown.* He had carried the word *unknown* for nine years because it had been the true word and he would not write a false one.

He drew a line under it and wrote the amendment he had waited nine years to earn the right to write.

*Entry 6, amended. November 15, 2106. 14:21:07, side entrance B; about 14:23, the Deputy Minister's office, side door. Wei, T. Left hand bandaged before entry. Event: Singh, A., telemetry spoofed to zero; walked out through the seeded district while forty witnesses were seeded otherwise. What happened in the office: witnessed. Witness: Singh, A.*

Then he took out the UNCHECKED list, and found the oldest item on it, and moved it to CHECKED, and the moving of it was the quietest thing he had done in years and cost him more than he showed.

---

Later, when the others had settled and Singh had gone to the room Elena gave her, Marcus stood in the yard with the fog down off the lake, thick enough that the barn was only a darker patch in the dark, and Singh came out and stood beside him for a while in the gray coat and said nothing, and he found that he had one question left that the three records had not covered.

"Why did he let you live?" he said.

Singh looked out into the fog where the lake was.

"I've asked myself every day," she said. "I think he wanted someone to see it."
