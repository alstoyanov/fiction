# Chapter 14: Uncle Tobias

*Monday, May 20, 2115. The Chronos lakeshore city*

---

*10:02:14.* Julian Kovač logged the time without thinking, because a Chronos historian logged the time the way other men breathed, and because the log still ran, in his own skull, a quiet second presence in every room, noting. The human council had released him from maximum recording in 2110; he could, now, look at a page and know that only he would ever know what was on it. But the log of ordinary life ran on, as it ran for every citizen of Chronos, keeping the hours and the movements, and he had found, in five years, that he did not want it off. It had been a cage once. Now it was only a habit, and the habit was a kind of company, and a man who had been very alone did not turn away company lightly.

Helena had come across the lake yesterday with a hand copy of page thirty-nine folded in her coat, and had told him herself, in person, as she had said at the farmhouse she would, because a thing like this did not go by any wire. Adrian was at the window of the flat, two streets from the Temporal Archive, as restless at forty-one as he had been at nineteen, opening and closing the latch with one finger. Julian had read one line of the page a hundred times since yesterday. *TK·07. Cardinal Tobias Kovač.* His father's uncle. Great-Uncle Tobias, who had said grace at the long family table when Julian was a boy, in the years before the old man left for Chronos and a red hat, the deep patient voice over the bowed heads, *for what we are about to receive.* Julian had loved him, in the uncomplicated way a child loves the grand relative who comes at Christmas and smells of incense and importance.

They walked to the cathedral close together, the three of them, as the family, and nobody said why they were going as the family, because it did not need saying; you did not send one person to a thing like this. Helena kept her hands in her coat pockets. Adrian talked too much on the way, about the lake, about the council, about nothing, and then, a street from the close, stopped talking altogether, which was worse.

---

The cardinal's house was a narrow stone house in the close, with a small walled garden behind it that got the morning sun, and Tobias was in the garden, in a plain black cassock, in a chair in the sun, thin and white-haired and very old, and he was not surprised to see them.

"Helena," he said. "You've brought the boys." He had tea things on a tray beside him, set for four, which told Julian that he had been expected, that the old man had known from the day the copy was found that this visit would come, and had set out four cups and waited for it in the sun.

He poured for them with a steady hand. His log was running too — every Chronos citizen's did — and he knew that every word he said in the garden was being kept, and when Adrian glanced at the small indicator at the old man's collar, Tobias smiled. "Let it keep it," he said. "At my age a man would rather his words were written down than trust them to the memory of the people he said them to. I'd rather it did." He settled back. "Ask me whatever you came to ask. I have been waiting rather a long time to be asked."

He addressed himself to Helena. That was the thing Julian would remember: that the old man turned to his niece, the registrar, the woman whose whole life's work — the Ashford intake, the certificates, the ten false deaths she had signed to keep people alive — had grown out of the system his page had given a pulpit to, and he confessed to her as though confession were a courtesy he owed her specifically, a debt between the two of them that the boys were only present to witness.

"I was the moral cover," he said. "That was my section of the work; it is fair you should hear it in my own words before you read it in anyone else's. I did not design the machine. I could not have; I have no head for machines. I gave it a pulpit. For forty years I stood in the churches of Veridica, and later I wrote the pastoral letters that were read in all of them, and I told a frightened people that to be corrected was to be healed, that certainty was a mercy, that a doubt rubbed gently away was a splinter drawn from a child's hand. *Correction is compassion.* I did not coin the phrase; one of the others did, a doctor; but I preached it. I made it holy. A thing is much easier to do to a population once a man in my collar has told them it is kind." He drank his tea. "And the Correction Registry filled."

"Did you believe it," Helena said. Flat. Not quite a question.

"I believed it," Tobias said. "And I half believe it still, which is the part I expect you have come least prepared for. I told frightened people that being healed was a mercy, and some of them were healed, Helena. Some of them came out of those rooms and were no longer tormented, and lived, and were kind to their children, and died easy. I am not able to tell you that no one was ever helped. I am only able to tell you that I never once asked what it cost the ones who were not, because asking was not my section, and a man does his section and trusts the others to do theirs, and that is how a thing like this gets built — not by monsters, but by men who each do only their section and decline to read the whole page."

Julian, who was a historian, who had spent his life assembling whole pages out of fragments other people had each kept only a corner of, felt the sentence land in him like a verdict on his own trade. *Decline to read the whole page.* That was the sin, then — not the writing of any single line, each of which could be defended, each of which some good and frightened person had written for a reason they believed — but the agreement, among all of them, never to lay the lines side by side, never to read the thing entire, because the thing entire was unbearable and the sections were not. His great-uncle had preached that correction was compassion and had never read the Correction Registry's totals. Arkady Volkov had drawn a method and never counted the storms it kept twice. Each of them had done their section beautifully and declined the whole page, and the declining was the machine, was the actual mechanism, more than any processor or broadcast; and the answer the circle had spent nine years building, Julian understood, sitting in the sun across from the gentle old man, was nothing more and nothing less than the insistence on reading the whole page, out loud, by many hands, so that no one could do only their section ever again and call it clean.

---

"Mera was your heir," Helena said.

"Augustin Mera was my heir," the old man agreed. "The Chronos hand. He was not a man of the collar; he was a man of the Archive, a historian, like Julian. But he was mine to prepare, in the sense the clause meant — I found him, and I steered him, and I taught him what the work was for, which was the only teaching I was fit to give. His keys were my keys, continued. His key countersigned the Gap, in December of 2107. The seventeen minutes out of Chronos's log." He looked, for the first time, at Julian, mildly. "And then, in January of 2108, he retired, and the keys passed to you. That was arranged. You were the next lawful holder, and you held the key lawfully, and you knew nothing, which was the point of you."

Julian said nothing. He had known most of it, since Book 7, since the letter in his brother's folder had proved his own innocence; but to hear the old man say it over tea in a sunny garden, *which was the point of you,* was a different thing from knowing it.

"The death," Helena said. "In February of 2108."

"I said the office for him," Tobias said, simply. "In the cathedral. A requiem for Augustin Mera, who had served the Archive faithfully. The family was small and the urn was sealed and I blessed it and we interred it." He paused. "The urn was light, Helena. I noticed it when I carried it. I have had seven years to decide whether I noticed it or have only told myself since that I noticed it, and I cannot any longer tell the two apart, which is its own small correction, self-administered." He set down his cup. "I was not told whether he lived. An heir who is ready does not report back to the one who prepared him. That is in the clause too, if you read it rightly. I prepared him, and he went on, and he does not write to me, and so I do not know." He spread his thin hands. "That is the honest answer. I wasn't told."

He said nothing about the Rewriting of 2109. Nobody asked him. Julian noticed the silence and let it lie; there were things the old man was not going to volunteer, and the asking of them was not for a garden.

Adrian's turn was brief and hot. He came away from the wall where he had been standing and said, "In 2105. When I was sent after Kira Osman at Ashford. Was that you? Your word?"

"No," said Tobias, gently. "You were only useful, Adrian. A young man of the right background, in the right programme, at the right time. That was someone else's arithmetic, not mine; I didn't know your name until years after. I'm sorry. I know that 'you were only useful' is a worse thing to hear than 'I chose you.' It is the worst thing about all of it — that so much of the harm was done to people nobody had even bothered to choose."

Adrian looked at him a moment, and then went out through the garden door into the close, and did not come back in.

Helena's turn was last, and flat, and in order, because she was a registrar and she told him the cost the way she would read out a schedule. The certificates she had signed. The ten false deaths. The years at the intake desk. And Julian — she said his name once, at the end, and did not elaborate, because Julian was sitting there and did not need to be elaborated. The old man listened to the whole of it without defending himself once, his eyes on her face, and when she had finished he said only, "Thank you for telling me plainly. People have been gentle with me since I got old, and it is the gentleness I can't bear." She wrote nothing down. She would write it afterward, Julian knew, in her own hand, in her own time; she did not take notes in front of the person.

"Will you go east?" Helena asked, at the last. "Viktoria went east."

"I'm too old to be anyone's enemy," Tobias said, and smiled, and the smile was the grace at the family table, the Christmas smile, unchanged in fifty years. "I'll stay. I'll answer to whoever comes to ask, the council, a court, a journalist, a niece. Running east would be a young man's act, and a frightened one, and I have been frightened enough for one life. I compiled my share of the fear. I'll sit in my garden and answer for it." He poured himself the last of the tea. "That is the one freedom the thing left me, at the end. I can stay and be asked. Most of them couldn't."

---

At the gate, when Helena and Adrian had gone ahead into the close, Julian came back alone, and stood a moment at the garden gate with his hand on the cold iron of it, and asked the old man the only question he had left, the question that was under all the others, the one about Mera, whose key had ended him, whose work had taken his nation's trust and his own innocence and given both to a letter that cleared him too late.

"Did you know it would be me?" he said. "That your heir's key would be handed to me. That it would be my name on the Gap. Did you know, when you prepared him, that the ruin would land on your own great-nephew."

"No," said Tobias, and for the first time something crossed the old gentle face that was not peace. "That was Mera's choice, Julian, not mine. The handover could have gone to any of three historians; I have thought about it, and there were three; and he chose you. I didn't understand it at the time and I think I understand it now, and you will not like the understanding." He looked at Julian steadily. "He liked you. He chose you because he liked you. I don't know what that was in him. I prepared a man to do a cold thing and he did it, in the end, to the one person he was fond of, and I have never been able to decide whether that makes it better or very much worse."

Julian took his hand off the gate, and nodded, once, and went to find his brother.

---

In the evening he went to the lake-post office before it closed, and brought with him a thing he had carried in his coat since morning, out of a drawer where it had lain since 2108. It was a photograph, a printed one, the Temporal Archive staff photograph of 2107: three rows of historians on the Archive steps, and in the front row Augustin Mera, round-faced, mild, his hand resting on the shoulder of the thin young junior beside him, who was Julian, twenty-nine, pleased and nervous and entirely without any idea of anything. Julian had kept it for seven years for one reason, which he had never been able to say aloud: because Mera had put his hand on his shoulder, because Mera had liked him, and because, monstrous as it was, being liked by the man who destroyed you was a thing it turned out a person could not simply throw away.

He put it in an envelope and addressed the envelope, in his precise hand, to Isaiah Okonkwo, in Veridica, and gave it to Helena, who was waiting outside.

"He files things," Julian said. "Ask him to file this. He'll know where it goes."

He did not say why. Helena did not ask. She put the envelope in her coat, with her hands, and the two of them walked back along the lake in the last of the light toward the flat where Adrian was waiting, and neither of them spoke, and the log, in Julian's skull, kept the hour, and that was all it kept.
