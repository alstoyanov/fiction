# Chapter 24: The Heir

*Monday, July 22, 2115. The Commission chamber, after hours*

---

The Commission had risen at five, and a handwritten card had come down to Marcus from the Minister's office before he could leave: *A word, if you can spare it. After, when it's quiet. — CZ.* He had telephoned Kira, who was home with Tamsin, and told her where he was and what the card said, and she had been silent a moment on the line and then said the thing she always said now.

"Write it down."

"I will," he said.

At twenty to seven the chamber was lit and empty. The cleaners had gone; the long table held nothing but the lamps' reflections; the tall windows had gone blue with the late July dusk. Chen Zhao sat in his own seat on the dais, with a glass of tea in its metal holder on the ledge beside him, and he was seventy-four, and silver-haired, and warm, and entirely unafraid, a confessed Architect of a week's standing who was still the Minister and had been applauded that week for his courage in staying. At his right on the dais stood the deputy chair, empty since the first sitting in August of 2110, and he had his hand resting on the back of it as he had rested it there in March, lightly, an old man's spotted hand on good wood.

Marcus sat where he always sat, at the far end of the long table, in the dissenting member's place.

"Thank you for staying," Chen Zhao said. "I know the hour is yours; you'll want to be home for the child's bath. I'll be brief, which I am told I never am." He turned the glass a quarter-turn on its ledge. "The Council of Nations votes on the fifteenth of August. The World Record will need a human chair — not to run it, the machine runs it, but to answer for it, to be the face a frightened world can look at and trust. It cannot be me; I am a confessed Architect, and the point of the chair is that it is held by someone who is not. It wants someone every nation has watched refuse something." He looked down the table, mild and fond. "I am offering it to you, Marcus. The chair of the World Record. Mrs. Osman has been reading the Schedule, I know — good; she should; it will bear her reading, it is honest work — and you will want her beside you on it, and that can be arranged."

Marcus said nothing.

"You know what it is," Chen Zhao said, not as a threat, as a courtesy, giving him the credit. "You've read it, or she has and told you. You know it restores from one source and you know where the source is. I am not going to insult you by pretending otherwise, and I am not going to pretend the offer is anything other than what it is." He paused. "You are my heir, Marcus. You always were."

---

He told it gently, in order, and every step of it landed on something Marcus already had, in his own hand, in a list, exact.

"The clause," Chen Zhao said. "You've read it. *Each contributor shall prepare an heir.* I was the youngest of us; I wrote the essay the sixth principle grew from when I was twenty-two; I had the most time of any of them to prepare mine, and I took it. I chose a Chen. Distant enough that no one would ever call it favour, near enough that the blood was real. I won't tell you the degree; it doesn't matter, and you've chased it long enough." He drank a little tea. "I chose a boy who believed. That was the whole of the specification. Not a clever boy — there were cleverer. A boy who *believed,* because a believer can be made into a restorer, and a doubter never can; a doubter only ever makes another doubter; but a believer who is broken and rebuilt comes out the far side with something the whole world can see and trust, which is a man who knows exactly what it costs to be certain and has chosen, anyway, to keep faith with the truth. That is the rarest and most useful thing there is, Marcus, and it cannot be hired, and it cannot be ordered, and it can only be grown, and it takes about thirty years."

He heard, behind the mild voice, a door in a corridor on the seventh of October, 2105, and a voice through it — *he's exactly what was requested* — a sentence he had carried on a list for nine years under the heading *Requested by whom?*

"The Believer was requested," Marcus said. His own voice sounded far off to him. "That's in my record. I've had it nine years."

"I asked for a believer," Chen Zhao agreed. "Thomas brought me names. I chose the one who was family. That is all 'requested' ever meant." He said it without weight. "And a believer at fifty-eight goes home, Marcus. You know that now, you've read enough of the files. A believer who scores fifty-eight is let go, and a man who is let go goes home and raises his children and the thirty years are wasted. He has to cross the threshold. He has to go in."

And Marcus saw the line in the acquisition file, which he had seen a hundred times and understood only now: *Acquisition: contamination index 0.58 → 0.73 (adjusted; threshold compliance).* He knew who had sat at station 14-09. He did not say it, and Chen Zhao did not say it, and the name of the man who had typed the seventy-three sat between them unspoken, having walked down a flight of stairs lightly three weeks ago.

"The order that took you off the Unit Three van," Chen Zhao went on, "on the sixth of December, 2106 — that was my signature. You may have seen it; it was never hidden. And the appointment in January, to the Historical Integrity Commission — mine. I gave a toast that day. Do you remember it?"

"*My cousin,*" Marcus said. "*No, truly. The Chen families of this city are all related somewhere, Mr. Chen.*"

"I meant it as a joke," Chen Zhao said, "and I meant it. Both. That has been the condition of my whole life, Marcus, and I think it is the condition of yours now too." He turned the glass again. "You refused KINDRED. You carried out RESTORATION. Either would have served; I want you to understand that; there was no wrong turning you could have taken, because I had prepared you to be trusted whichever way you broke, and a man who refuses a dishonourable order is trusted, and a man who runs a hard restoration honestly is trusted, and I needed only that you be trusted. At the reception, that autumn, I raised a glass *to our restorers.* And when you confessed, in December, with no remedy — a fine, brave, useless thing — I offered to help you find their Register. Do you remember what I said?"

"You said, *then let me help you find this Register of theirs.*"

"And you found it without me," Chen Zhao said, and smiled, "which was better, because a thing a man finds for himself he believes, and a thing he is handed he suspects. You have believed every step of this because you took every step yourself. That was the whole art of it. I never once pushed you. I only ever left the doors open and let you walk through them, and you walked through them, every one, toward exactly this chamber and exactly this chair." He paused. "At the foot of the Justice Center steps, when the war ended — *Cousin, we'll need you on it.* And I have kept the deputy chair open for you ever since. You thought it was a seat on the Commission I was holding. It was never a seat on the Commission. A deputy is the one who succeeds. It was the heir's seat, and it has been empty five years because you were not ready, and now you are ready, and the chair it was always pointing at is the chair of the World Record."

"The Judge," Marcus said. "You didn't arrange the Judge ending."

"No," Chen Zhao said, and for the first time there was something under the warmth, something almost like respect. "That I did not arrange. None of us did; it was hers, Vance's, the one thing she kept from all of us. I watched it happen, and I let it run, because I could see what it would make you. It made you the man who ended the machine. Trusted in two nations. And then, this summer, in all of them." He spread his hands. "I could not have designed a better qualification for the chair of the World Record than *the man who ended the last machine.* And I did not design it. I only recognised it, and kept your seat open."

---

Marcus saw it then, whole, the way he saw a fact entry before he wrote it: the Commission of Rebuilding had been Principle Six in the flesh — *arrange to be the one who restores it* — and he, Marcus, had been its restorer, the trusted human face on the restoration of a nation's record, prepared for the role from before he could read, broken and rebuilt to exactly the specification, every door he had ever held open held because it had been left open for him to hold. He did not write it. He sat with it.

It should have unmade him, and he waited for it to, and it did not. He had expected — in the part of him that had been bracing for a blow like this since the night Singh said *mostly, Mr. Chen, I watched you* — that to learn his whole life had been prepared would be to lose it, that every choice he had been proud of would turn to ash in the knowing: the refusal of KINDRED, the ending of the machine, the marriage, the child, the quiet dissents, all of it revealed as track laid down in advance for a train that only thought it was driving itself. He waited for the ash. What came instead, slowly, in the lamplit chamber with the old man's fond eyes on him, was something steadier: the understanding that a prepared choice was still a choice. Chen Zhao had grown the conditions; Chen Zhao had not, could not, stand inside him at the moment of choosing and choose; the whole of the design's genius and the whole of its limit lived in that gap, the hair's breadth between *prepared* and *done* that Tanaka had found in a linen room and Kira had carried out of Ashford and the old man had sung about for fifty years. He had been prepared to be trusted. He had not been made to say yes. And the fact that his whole life had been built toward the moment he could give that yes was also, exactly and only, the fact that his whole life had equipped him to withhold it — because a man grown to be the one person the world would trust with its one record was, necessarily, a man grown to understand to his marrow why there must be no one record at all.

Chen Zhao said *cousin* once more, fondly, rising to the word, and Marcus thought — the way the mind reaches for the true cousin when the false one claims the name — of Nora Chen, his actual cousin, SFW, who had held a door in a burning room on the coast so that other people could get out, and had died holding it, and had never once in her life called him cousin or anything else, because they had never met, because the design had cast her nation as the enemy before either of them was born. The old man used the word like a key. Nora had earned it and never said it. Chen Zhao had never earned it and said it at every sitting.

"We made you a hero," Chen Zhao said. "We needed you." He said it without triumph, the way a man states the weather, as a fact that was neither to be boasted of nor apologised for. "The World Record will be kept by a man who has refused everything anyone ever offered him. That is exactly why they will let him keep it. A man who wanted it could never be trusted with it. You have spent your whole life refusing, Marcus, and that is the one qualification that cannot be faked, and I grew it in you, and now I am asking you to spend it. Refuse the chair, and they will give it to someone who wanted it, and that will be worse. Take it, and the one record of the whole world will be held by the most reluctant man alive, which is the safest hands it could possibly be in." He let that sit. "That is the trap and the truth of it. I am not hiding either half. Take your time. You always check."

---

He finished his tea. He rose slowly, the way an old man rises, with a hand on the ledge, and he came down from the dais and the length of the table, and in passing he rested his hand on Marcus's shoulder, lightly, for a moment, the weight of it almost nothing, and did not press it.

"Take your time," he said again, at the door. "You always check. It is why I chose you." And he went, and his footsteps went away down the corridor, unhurried, and a door closed somewhere, and the chamber was quiet.

Marcus sat alone in the lit room.

He took out his list. CHECKED. UNCHECKED. HOPES. And near the bottom, *Where is east?,* crossed through on the tenth of June. He uncapped the pencil and held it over the page, over the blank below the crossed-out line, where for nine years he had always, always had something to write — a thing to check, a thing to hope, a fact to enter, a hole to mark as lost. The Minister's empty glass stood on the table with a brown ring of tea dried at the bottom of it.

For the first time in the whole of it, he held the pencil over the page and wrote nothing at all.
