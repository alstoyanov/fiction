# Chapter 12: The Administrator

*Monday, May 6, 2115. Coastal command, the Atlantic coast*

---

*06 May 2115. 07:00. Coastal command. Weather: fog on the harbour, visibility under a cable. Status: routine. Duty officer: self.*

Colonel Yevgenia Sokolov wrote her log in the first person, in the morning, as she had written it every morning for years, because the first person was the register in which a commander took responsibility for a day and did not hide behind the passive voice the way the Ministry had taught everyone to hide. The fog was real this morning, true Atlantic fog off the grey water, and the gulls were loud in it, and the harbour was a rumour of masts. She had learned, since the war, to note when the weather was real. It was a small discipline and it kept her honest about larger things.

VS·09 had been in her office safe for thirteen days.

Dmitri Volkov had driven out on the Tuesday after the reading, the twenty-third of April, with a hand copy of page thirty-nine in a card folder, and he had stood in her office and handed it to her and said nothing while she read it standing. She had read it the way she read an annex: once, fast, for the shape, and then again, slowly, for the one line that mattered, which was the ninth. *VS·09. Chancellor Viktoria Sokolov. The Annex.* Her mother's mark. Her mother's copy, the ninth of thirteen, somewhere in the world or ash. She had initialled the folder's cover to acknowledge receipt, because acknowledging receipt was what you did, and she had handed it back, and she had said, "Thank you, Agent Volkov," and Dmitri had not corrected her, and she had noticed, the moment it was out of her mouth, that she had called him by a rank he had not held under her for more than ten years, and that she had reached for it because it was the register in which she could stand to be in the room. She had logged his visit and let him go.

---

At nine forty the guardroom called up. A courier at the gate, asking for the Colonel by name, on Synthesis diplomatic papers, and refusing to hand his envelope to anyone but her.

She had him held, and his papers logged, and then she went down herself, because a thing that would only be given to her was a thing she would take in person and see the giver's face. He was unremarkable — middle-aged, civilian, tired from the road — and his papers were in order, Synthesis diplomatic transit, correct seals, and the envelope he gave her carried no Council frank and no postage stamp and nothing at all on its face but her name, *Col. Y. Sokolov,* in her mother's hand. He said he was instructed to wait for a reply until eighteen hundred, and no later. She had him shown to the guardroom and told the duty sergeant to give him tea and not to let him wander, and she logged his arrival, the time, the papers, the instruction, and went back up with the envelope.

She read it in the register of an order, standing at her window over the fogged harbour, the way she had read the SFW annex in the war: not as a daughter, not yet, but as a staff officer reading a document to find out what it required of her.

It was two typed pages in her mother's style, which was to say exact and without a single flourish, every sentence carrying its freight and no more, signed at the foot with a single initial, *V.* And it was undated. She noticed that first, as a fact, before she let herself read a word of the body: her mother dated everything, had dated everything her whole life, considered an undated document a kind of lie; and this one bore no date, which meant it had been written to be read at a time its writer could not know in advance. *Written ahead.* She noted it, the way she noted real weather, and did not yet interpret it.

*When you read this,* it began, *you will have read my name.*

It went on like a personnel file, which was a thing her mother had understood better than anyone alive: that a life could be told as a list of dates, and that the list was more honest than any story you could lay over it. The storm-test programme, the year Yevgenia entered it. Captain, the date. Major, February of 2109. Colonel, the twentieth of December, 2109 — and here her mother had added, in the same flat voice, that the insignia had been engraved in advance, which Yevgenia had not known and did not want to know and now knew. Coastal command, January of 2110.

And then, without a change of register, the leak. Her mother set it down as a line in the file. The plan, and the phrase that had carried it — *the beaches are a door, not a wall.* It had been handed east, her mother's word was *conveyed,* and it had been a door, and the SFW had come through the door her daughter's command had drawn, and the war had had its beach. *The war needed a beach,* her mother wrote. *You drew the best one. I have never been prouder of a piece of your work, and I was not able to tell you so at the time.*

*I did not control you,* the letter said. *I guided you. That is what a mother is for.*

And near the end, alone on its own line, with white space above and below it the way the Annex had given white space to the clause that mattered:

*I gave you everything, Yevgenia. Even your career.*

Of the east, her mother wrote only that it was quiet, and that she was well, and that the climate suited her. She named no one. She did not say where she was. She did not mention a single other person, living or dead, in the whole of the two pages. It was a letter about the two of them, a mother and a daughter, and a career, and it was the most terrible thing Yevgenia had ever read, precisely because there was love in it, genuine, unmistakable love, delivered as a report of work successfully done.

---

She took her own file from the safe — her real service record, the one she kept privately, the dates in her own hand — and she laid it beside the letter and compared them line by line, the way she checked a manifest against a hold. They matched. Every date her mother had written was a date in her file, and every one of them her mother had known before she had. She took a clean sheet and did the thing she had done once before, in March of 2110, when she first understood: she drew two columns, and headed one *what I earned* and one *what was placed,* and she went through her career and put each thing in its column.

The second column was longer.

She looked at it a while. Then she drew a line through the heading of the first column, because in the fog of that morning it seemed to her that there was nothing in it, that it was all the second column, that a career built inside a design was a placed career to the last promotion and there was no clean earned thing anywhere in it.

And then she wrote the heading back in, above the line, because that was the lie her mother wanted her to believe, and she would not do her mother's work for her. Some of it she had earned. The holding of the line at the river in the worst week of the war, when the dose came off the city and her own soldiers were screaming at their own history and she had kept the checkpoint quiet and orderly and had not made martyrs — that she had earned, with her own nerve, on a morning no one had placed; her mother had drawn her the beach but her mother had not stood on it. She left both columns. She let them both be true. It was the only honest sheet, and it was not comfortable, and she had stopped expecting honest things to be comfortable.

This was the particular cruelty her mother had reserved for her, she thought, folding the sheet along its line: not to have been controlled, which a person could at least resent cleanly, but to have been *guided* — to have been given a nature and then given the opportunities that nature would seize, so that every choice was genuinely hers and every choice had also been foreseen, bet on, counted. Her mother had not made her draw the beach. Her mother had made the woman who would draw the beach, over thirty years, with a mother's patience and a mother's love, both of them real, and had then stood back and let her draw it, and had been proud, and the pride was the worst of it, because the pride was not a lie. A machine could take your past. Her mother had done something the machine could not: she had taken Yevgenia's future and made it indistinguishable from her character, so that there was no clean line anywhere a knife could go, no place to cut the placed thing away from the earned thing, because they were the same thing, because she *was* the placement, grown and finished and good at it. The only answer to that was the one on the sheet in front of her: to keep both columns, to refuse to let either one be the whole truth, to be a woman who was placed and who had also, on one grey morning on a beach, chosen — and to insist on the sliver of the second column that was hers, because the sliver was the whole of her freedom and she would not surrender it to make the accounting tidy.

---

At noon she walked the harbour wall with her hands in her coat pockets and looked east over the water, into the fog, which had thinned to a high haze now so that the horizon was a hard grey line and the gulls went over it and came back. The wind was off the land. It was ten minutes to one when she turned back. She noted the time in her head and did not note anything else, because there was nothing else that belonged in a log.

Dmitri had driven out again by dusk. He had come once a week since the twenty-third of April and had never said why, and she had never asked, because she understood it without being told: that a man who had served under her, and lost a brother in the war her command had helped draw, came out once a week to a windy coast to sit near someone who had been there, and that this was a thing done between soldiers and did not survive being spoken about. She showed him the letter. He read it through once, standing, formally, his lips not moving, and handed it back, and neither of them used a contraction, because neither of them did, and between the two of them the clipped speech was not a scar but a shared language.

"Will you answer it?" he said.

"I am entitled to," she said.

He asked nothing else. That was the thing about Dmitri; he asked exactly one question and accepted the answer as the answer. On the way back along the wall to his car, with the light going out of the west behind them and the east already dark over the water, she said, not to him exactly, looking at the sea:

"I was apparently very good at it. She has sent me the reasons." A gull went over. "She has sent me a commendation. For the best piece of work I ever did, which was treason, which I did without knowing I did it, because she had spent thirty years building the woman who would do it and think it was her own idea." She stopped. "That is a thing a person has to answer. Or not be a person."

Dmitri looked at the sea too, and counted, she thought, something — the lamps coming on along the wall, perhaps — and then he said, "Yes," and got in his car, and drove back inland into the dark.

---

That night she wrote the reply at her desk, on a single sheet of command paper, in her own hand. She drafted it three times. The first draft was long, and she read it once and put it in the burn bag, because it explained, and she would not explain to a woman who had never once explained to her. The second was short and cold and clever, and she put it in the burn bag too, because it was clever, and cleverness was her mother's register and she would not answer her mother in her mother's register. The third she kept.

At ten to eleven, long after the courier's own deadline of eighteen hundred, which he had let pass without a word and gone on waiting, she went down to the guardroom herself and handed him the sheet, folded once. It was one line, formal, in her own upright hand:

*Mother. I am where I should be. It is not where you put me.*

She went back up and logged the dispatch — the time, twenty-two fifty, the courier's papers, the single sheet — and initialled the entry, and closed the log for the day, and sat for a while at the window over the dark harbour where somewhere to the east her mother was warm and quiet and well and proud of her, and then she went to bed, because there was a dawn watch and she was the duty officer, and the fog would be back on the water by morning, and it would be real.
