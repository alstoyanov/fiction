# Chapter 5: The Filed Woman

*Monday, April 1, 2115. The capital, and the lakeshore road*

---

On the back of a tram ticket, in a small upright hand, she had written to herself in the night:

*Apr 1. Farmhouse today. Tell it once, to all of them, Thursday. Not before. Why not before? A.S.*

She had left the question standing because she had not yet decided the answer, and Amara Singh did not resolve a question before its time any more than she left a sum half-added. She was fifty-eight. Her reading glasses hung on their cord against her chest, and by eight in the morning her sleeves were rolled to the elbow, as they had been every working morning of a life spent among papers; she had tried, once, in the unaligned years, to become a person who kept her sleeves down, and had failed, and had taken the failure as evidence that there was a floor under her somewhere that the last nine years had not managed to dig through.

The gray coat hung on the door hook. It was the coat from the stand in her office in the Ministry in 2106 — the same coat, not one like it. She had carried it through six years of a disputed strip and three more in a Chronos lakeshore town, and it had been mended at the left cuff by a tailor in the strip who had worked for nothing because she kept the ledger that proved his dead were dead, and she had never replaced it. She knew that keeping a coat for nine years because it was the coat you were wearing when you stopped being a person was not a sensible reason to keep a coat. She kept it. She had made a private study of the things she did that were not sensible; the list was short, and the coat was on it, and she had decided that a woman entitled to only a few unreasonable things might as well spend them on wool.

Nine years was a long time to be no one. She had thought, in the first year, that being filed would be like dying — a stopping — and it was not like dying; it was like becoming weather, present and unremarked, moving through rooms that did not register her, a woman with a dead signal behind her ears whom no system would interview because the systems did not interview the dead. She had learned to live in the gaps of other people's attention, and it had taught her things about her old profession that thirty years inside it never had. She had spent three decades deciding what went into the record of a nation, and she had never once, in all that time, considered what it was to be a person the record had decided did not exist. She considered it now constantly. It was the single most important thing she knew, and she had been the last person in the world who should have had to learn it, and she had come back, in part, because a thing you have learned and tell no one is a thing that dies with you, and she had watched too many true things die with the one person who knew them to let hers go the same way.

She would tell it once. That was the one decision she had made and would not revise. She had told no one the hour in nine years — not the tailor, not the Chronos shopkeeper over whose premises she had rented a room, not Tanaka, who had examined her as courteously as a case and asked her nothing she did not have to. She had asked for Thursday, and for all of them together, because she had learned past any unlearning what a single person's account was worth in a world that kept records: nothing, unless a second hand signed beside it. She wanted it told once, in a room full of witnesses, written down by more than one of them, so that it could never again be only her saying it. For today she kept the hour shut like a door with her shoulder against it. She let her mind go up to the door — the knock, the clock on her desk reading fifty-nine fifty-eight — and no further. What was on the other side of the knock was Thursday's, and belonged to the room, not to a tram ticket.

Tanaka had established the rest over three days in March, gently, as a clinician. The small device behind Singh's ears had been reporting a clean zero since November of 2106 — a flatline, spoofed, the method everyone in the trade knew and almost no one could do, a dead signal sent up to a network while the living person walked around beneath it. It still received. She still got the broadcasts, when she was in range of one, the history of whatever nation she stood in laid gently over her own memory; she had simply never, in nine years, sent anything back. In nine years not one official in two nations had asked her about the zero behind her ears, because a zero behind the ears was a dead woman, and you did not interview the dead. That was the whole of how it had worked. The design had made her unseeable by making her, on paper, not there.

---

She arranged the nine years in order while she waited for the car, because arranging things in order was what she had instead of peace.

Six years in the unaligned strip, 2106 to 2112. A disputed country of no nation, bad roads, worse winters, and tens of thousands of people whom no system counted — refugees from four wars, the stateless, the filed-dead like herself. She had kept a registry for them by hand, in ledgers, in a schoolroom with the desks pushed back: names, dates of birth as near as anyone could say, who had come with whom, who would vouch that a child was a given woman's child. No machine. No Judge. Two witnesses to a fact, and her pen, and a ledger that a fire could take, and did, once, so that she learned to keep it in three places before an engineer she had never met arrived at the same rule from the other direction. She called it, to herself, the only honest job she had ever had in records. She had spent thirty years before it making the record true by deciding what went in. In the strip she had only written down what people swore to each other, and let it be as true as that, and no truer, and it had been enough to reunite four hundred families, which was four hundred more than she had reunited in thirty years at the Ministry.

Then Chronos, from 2112, under a name that was not her own and that she would carry to the farmhouse unspoken, because a name you had lived under was a door too, and she opened her doors one at a time. A lakeshore town, a room over a shop, the broadcasts of a nation she had helped design washing gently through her every evening. After the Chronos Judge died in 2110, nobody asked about an unlogged woman in a lakeshore town; there were a great many unlogged people now, the whole nation learning to be unlogged at once, and one more old woman with no signal was a leaf in a drift of leaves.

Once, in all of it, she had come back. The fifteenth of November, 2107, the first Day of Testimony; she had stood at the colonnade on the west side and watched a crowd read their ordinary days aloud at a plain stone, and a small woman with a notebook had seen her across the plaza and begun to walk, and Singh had left before the tram cleared the colonnade, because she had not been ready, then, to be a witness, having spent a year learning how little one was worth.

---

She had watched. Her own margin note had told her to, in the Ministry, a minute before the knock, written for herself in the small upright hand on the corner of a folder: *Who restores it? Watch who restores it.* She had not fully known, writing it, what she meant. She had come to know. For nine years she had watched, through whatever newspapers reached the strip and through Isaiah Okonkwo's columns and the cautious Chronos press, who restored what, and when, and at whose appointment.

The Historical Integrity Commission of 2107, its restorers appointed by the Minister. The Concordance, with its index of every journal, confessed and offered in the same breath. RESTORATION. And then, in 2110, after the machine ended, the Commission of Rebuilding, again under the Minister, restoring the record of a whole nation from testimony, humanely, by hand, trusted. She had kept her own list of who restored what, on paper, in three places. It was a short list, and one name recurred on it, at the top and near the top and at the side of nearly every line, a patient name that was never quite the hand doing the restoring and was always the hand that appointed the hand. She did not write the name on the tram ticket. She knew it. Thursday's room would hear it. A thing like that you said in front of witnesses or you did not say at all.

The file had found her in 2112. In a Chronos shop she had taken down *The Cost of Certainty* because she had read Okonkwo in the strip and trusted the byline, and she had stood in the shop and read, in a clinician's flat italic, a case note on an unidentified female decedent of November 2106, and had come to the sentence the doctor had set down to lie flat and be stepped over: *Nobody who cannot be seeded has seen her body.* She had laughed aloud, one bark of a laugh in a quiet shop, because it was so exactly, so professionally true, and because someone had seen it and written it down and let it wait; and then she had sat down on the floor of the shop with the book in her lap, and the shopkeeper had brought her water, and she had known, from the floor, who had been keeping her file, and that the file had been kept well, and by someone who meant it kindly.

Why now. She turned the tram ticket over and did not add to it. Because the same book, in its account of the manuscript, had said that its own contents listed a Register of Contributors that no one had ever found — a page with names on it, somewhere, waiting. Because she was fifty-eight, and the thing behind her ears would not report a zero forever, and a woman who was evidence was no use to anyone while she stayed in the folder. She had been a torn page in a safe for nine years. She had decided, at last, to be read.

She was not a brave woman and she did not pretend to herself that this was bravery. It was something colder and more durable than bravery, which was accounting. She had watched the restorers for nine years and kept the list in three places, and the list had, at its head, a name that recurred, and she was fifty-eight, and the device behind her ears would not sham a zero forever, and a witness who waited until she was certain of her own safety to testify was a witness who would never testify at all, because the design did not leave such witnesses safe, it left them dead or filed or discredited, and she had been two of the three and had no wish to discover the third. So she had done the sum, the way a records officer does a sum, and the sum came out that the value of her testimony fell a little every year she kept it and the risk of keeping it rose, and that there was therefore an hour, somewhere, past which keeping it was simply waste; and she had decided, on the back of a tram ticket, that the hour had come. It was not courage. It was that she could no longer justify the ledger.

---

Tanaka drove her out in the afternoon, north and west along the lakeshore road, and they spoke of tea, and of the road, which was bad in its usual places, and of the weather, which was turning, and of nothing else, by an agreement neither of them had stated. As the road came down toward the water the fog rose off the lake to meet it, real weather, grey and thickening, swallowing the fields and then the verge and then the road ahead to a car's length, so that Tanaka slowed and put the lights on and drove by the edge of the tarmac. Singh checked the car clock twice in the last few miles, and noticed, the second time, that she had checked it, and that she had been checking clocks all day, and that she had been checking clocks for nine years, and she made herself stop.

The farmhouse sat back from the road behind a yard of mud, a low stone house and a barn and bare plum trees beyond. It was, she understood as the car stopped, the one place she had come to in nine years that was in no record anywhere — no deed she could find, no entry, no address a system held. A woman in boots stood in the open door against the yellow light of the kitchen: fifty-ish, square, weathered, with the stillness of someone who had decided a long time ago exactly how much she was going to be surprised by in a life, and had used it all up. She looked Singh up and down once, left to right, the way you check a delivery against its list.

"Right," she said. "Come in."

Singh stepped into the kitchen.

The stove against the far wall had a crack in its firebox door that glowed orange and threw a moving light up the wall. An old man stood at it with a wooden spoon, very tall once and stooped now, a long white ponytail down his back, stirring something that smelled of barley. He looked up at her as she came in out of the fog, and he nodded, once, without a flicker of surprise, as if she were the last of a party that had been expected and were only a little late for the meeting; and then he turned back to the pot, and stirred, and hummed, low, a tune that rose at the end like a question nobody in the warm room needed to answer.
