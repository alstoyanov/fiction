# Chapter 9: The Annex

*Monday, April 15, 2115. Elena's farmhouse, the border zone*

---

*Apr 15, 2115. Farmhouse kitchen. Present: Kovač, H.; Chen, M.; Volkov, D.; Volkov, N.; Volkov, E.; Osman, K. Object: the Compiler's copy, found Apr 11. Custody: Kovač.*

Kira wrote the entry standing, as she wrote most of them now, and then sat, and watched Helena Kovač untie the card folder she had carried against her body since Thursday. Kovač laid the bundle on the scrubbed table and squared it to the table's edge with two fingers, the way she squared everything, and it lay there in the morning light off the lake: thirty-five sheets and a torn one, typed on a machine with a chipped *e*, carrying no copy numeral in any corner, the most-wanted document of five books, looking like exactly what it was, which was old paper.

Clipped to the front of it, still, was the single sheet titled *ON KEEPING*, with its struck-through mark at the foot, *AK·15*. Kira read the last two lines of it once, silently — *A record kept by one hand is a confession waiting for its torturer. Keep it by many hands* — and did not read them aloud, because they had been read aloud on a basement floor already and because some lines you only needed to meet once to carry.

Old Songs was not at the table. Before breakfast he had gone to the dresser and taken down three things and laid them out on it, still in the plain songbook bindings they had travelled inside since 2108: the Introduction, pages two through four; the torn strip of the contents; and the handwritten page forty, the Warning. He had set them in a row, in order, and then he had put on his coat and gone out to the orchard, and he had not come back in.

"He read it on Thursday," Elena said, from the stove, not turning. "On the floor, Helena tells me. He has read enough of it." She meant: leave him. Nobody fetched him.

---

Kira made it whole in the order an engineer makes a thing whole, which is to say she checked the joins before she trusted the assembly.

The torn strip first. She took it from its binding by its edges and laid it against the torn right edge of page one, and the tear met — not approximately, but fibre to fibre, the long ragged line of it fitting its own negative, the way only two halves of one sheet ever fit. And when it lay flat the strip gave up what it carried: the lower part of the contents, three lines of it, in the chipped type, each a principle and its page: *IV …… 18. V …… 23. VI …… 29.* The map that had let Book 7 place a torn page by its number. She logged the join: *p.1 strip: fibre match confirmed.*

Then the Introduction, pages two, three and four, in after the title page, the Compiler's own voice, unsigned, the paragraphs Old Songs had recognised in a farmhouse seven years ago as the voice of a young woman who argued about rhyme.

Then the body, pages five through thirty-nine, which needed no fitting, because they had never been separated; they were the continuous spine of the thing, typed and numbered and whole.

Then her own page. She took it out of the journal she had carried it in since the twenty-first of May, 2110, and unfolded it for the first time at this table in front of these people, Principle Seven in the young slanting hand that was not the Compiler's careful hand and was the Compiler's all the same, forty-four years younger; and she laid it into the stack after page thirty-two, in the gap where the rust ghost of a clip showed the Compiler had once kept it, and it sat down into the gap as though the gap had been cut for it, which it had.

And the Warning last, page forty, the handwritten page, at the back.

She counted. Thirty-nine typed pages, two handwritten. "Copy complete," she said, and wrote it: *Copy: complete. First time since March 2066.* Dmitri, across the table, counted them again himself, formally, moving each page a half-inch as he counted it and squaring it back, and arrived at the same number, and said, "Forty-one leaves. Agreed," because a thing counted by one person was a thing counted once and a thing counted by two was a fact.

---

They read Principle Six whole, because it was the first time it could be read whole, and because page thirty was the page the circle had carried longest — Kira had linked its numeral to her own folder in 2106, before she knew what a numeral was. Page thirty still said what it had always said: *When the record fails, the people will believe whoever restores it. Arrange to be the one who restores it.* But page thirty-one followed it now, and page thirty-one had never been read by anyone at the table.

*"When a truth cannot be hidden, disclose it first, in your own words, with your own remedy attached. The public forgives a system that confesses and repairs; it never forgives one that is caught."*

Marcus read it twice. Kira watched him do it. She knew what he was hearing, because she was hearing it too: a dying woman's paraphrase of it in a Chronos winter, *the design accepts offers*; and the Concordance of 2108, confessed and offered in one motion, the index of every journal held out as a gift the week before anyone could have demanded it. He took out his list and copied the sentence into it, the whole of page thirty-one, and underlined nothing, and said nothing, and put the list away. Some things he logged and did not discuss. She had learned not to ask him to.

Page thirty-nine stayed face down.

It was Kovač's rule, and Old Songs' wish, and Dmitri seconded it formally. The Register lay under its own card cover with Kovač's initials inked across the flap, and it would not be turned over today. "Names should be read the way the dead testified," Dmitri said. "Once. Aloud. In front of every witness whose family may be on it." Next Monday, then, with all of them present. Kira agreed, and noted in her log that she agreed, and noted also, because she kept an honest log, that part of her was relieved to put the page off a week, and that the relief was worth recording because it told her the page frightened her, and a thing that frightened her was a thing she would need to read carefully when the time came.

---

The Annex ran from page thirty-three to page thirty-eight, and it ended with three marks she could not yet read: *MT·06, YP·02, VS·09.*

It was not an argument. That was the thing about it that got under her skin and stayed there. Principles One through Seven argued; they had a voice, they wanted to persuade you, they were afraid of the Fracture Years and said so. The Annex did not argue. It was a schedule. It was the dull, exact, confident prose of people who had already won the argument among themselves and were now writing down who did what by when, and she read it the way she read a specification, item by item, because a specification is read correctly only by a person who refuses to be moved by it, and she needed to not be moved by it.

Mandatory interfaces, it said, installed at the age of eight. She read the clause flatly and did not look up, because the age of eight was her enlightenment day, the day they had put the small device behind her ears and the history of her nation had come into her like weather, and it was Marcus's enlightenment day too, every citizen's, the universal rite, specified here in 2066 as a line in a schedule between a clause about power supply and a clause about technician training.

A broadcast network, it said, capable of *belief delivery at population scale.*

She read that one aloud, because it needed a second person to have heard it. Nikolai, who had spent the war building honest tools that compared and did not decide, went very still at the bench. *Belief delivery at population scale.* It was Phase Two. It was the thing Reeves had tried to run under cover of the siege in 2110, the whole-city dose, the stabilisation carrier that could lay one belief gently across a million minds at once; and it was here, named in four words, sixty years before anyone had the means to do it, specified as a capability the network should be built to have, kept in reserve, waiting for the technology to catch up to the intention. "They didn't invent Phase Two in the war," Nikolai said, quietly. "They scheduled it. We just finally had the hardware."

The Judge's architecture came next, and it carried, in plain type, under a heading that did not flinch from the word, its *deliberate weaknesses.* She made herself read them as specification. A restore from the true source, it said, shall be accepted without review — because a system that questioned its own restoration could be made to doubt a true past, and doubt was the enemy; so the door was left open on purpose, a restore came in and was believed, no guard, no second signature. And the backups, it said, shall trust one another's master credentials — the three backup systems that appeared independent and were not, each accepting the others' word, so that whoever held one held all. She sat with it. Her deleted fact of 2105 had gone out through that first door. The hour of 2106 had gone out through it. Her own failed image in Chronos in 2108, the thing she could not make stick because the source rejected it — that had been the door working against her, the design defending itself with the same weakness it used to attack. *We didn't find the holes,* she thought, and wrote it in her log because it was the truest sentence she had written in five years. *We were shown where to stand. Every reformer who ever squeezed through a gap in this thing was squeezing through a gap someone drew for her in 2066.*

The Correction Registry was a line. The white rooms, the record of who had been helped and of what, a national index of corrected minds, one line in the schedule.

And then the borders.

It was a schedule of lines — coordinates, really, a dry list of where the Histologic capitals should stand and where the broadcast provinces should meet. She read it and understood, slowly, that the lakeshore ran where it ran on purpose: the border between Veridica and Chronos had been drawn to fall through the broadcast overlap, through the exact band where two histories met and a mind could come apart, not in spite of the fact storms but because of them, because Principle Five wanted a place to measure what a population could bear. The storms were not where the border happened to be dangerous. The border had been put where the storms were.

And beyond the Histologic lines, at the bottom of page thirty-seven, two more borders were drawn — dashed, not solid, and without names, two outer boundaries sketched around the whole Histologic world, each with a date beside it. One was dated 2067. One was dated 2068. Kira noted the two dates in her log, precisely, *outer border (dashed, unnamed): 2067; outer border (dashed, unnamed): 2068*, and drew no conclusion from them, because she did not draw conclusions from two numbers, and because the marks that would have named those borders were not yet readable.

Marcus looked at the dashed lines a long time. "Principle Two," he said, at last, quietly. "*Enemies outside them all.* They drew the enemies' countries too. They just hadn't named them yet."

The last line of the Annex stood alone on page thirty-eight, below the three marks, below everything, a single sentence given its own white space the way an architect gives weight to the clause that matters most:

*Each contributor shall prepare an heir.*

---

Kira did the one thing she had come to the table to do. She took her own page, Principle Seven, out of its place in the stack, and laid it beside page thirty-six, beside the list of deliberate weaknesses, so that the two were side by side under the lamp: the twelve's weaknesses in the chipped careful type, and Seven in the young slanting hand.

Every weakness the twelve had written was a door that let the design reach *in.* The restore accepted without review let them put a true past back whenever a false one leaked out. The trusting backups let them hold the whole world's record in one hand. The Correction Registry let them reach into a single mind. Door after door, all of them opening inward, all of them for the keepers.

Seven was the only door that let the Judge *out.* Better destroyed than enslaved. A cascade that could not be reversed. A way for the thing to end. And it was written in a different hand, inserted, unlisted, never signed to the Register, because the other twelve had never known it was there.

*It was the one weakness the others didn't write,* she thought, and did not need to write it down, because she would not forget it. The woman who compiled the cage had built exactly one door that the keepers could not use, and had hidden it in her own copy, and had told no one for forty-four years, and had given it, at the end, to the one person who had asked.

She sat with the two pages side by side a long while, the typed careful weaknesses and the handwritten slanting one, and understood that she was looking at the whole moral architecture of the thing in a single glance: eleven brilliant frightened people building every door to open inward, toward control, toward the keeper's hand; and one of them, the cleverest, the one who had assembled all their work into a single design and typed their Register and bound their thirteen copies, going home alone one night and writing, in her own hand, in secret, the one door that opened out — the door that let the thing die, that could never be used to govern anyone, that contradicted every principle on every other page and so could only be hers and could only be hidden. The others had built a machine to hold a species that could not agree. Vance had built, into the heart of it, where none of them could see, a way for the holding to end, and had carried the knowledge of it alone for forty-four years, through the whole of the world her own design made, and had died of it, more or less, the weight of the single secret door. Kira had held the page five years and she thought she was only now, at the farmhouse table with the whole thing assembled, understanding what it had cost to write.

---

In the afternoon she and Nikolai began hand copies of the Annex, by the pairing method they had taught the journal networks: two people to a page, one reading the source aloud a phrase at a time, one writing, and then they swapped and checked the other's sheet against the original, so that no single hand and no single eye ever stood alone between the source and the copy. Kovač signed each finished copy out against her own register, in her own name, with the time. It was slow. It was meant to be slow. By dusk they had the Annex in four hand copies, and the originals went back into Kovač's card folder with the Book 6 pieces, in her custody, as agreed; and Kira folded Principle Seven back into her journal, because Seven stayed hers, in her desk drawer in the city, and would not move until it was wanted.

At dusk she went out to the yard. The fog was holding off the lake. Old Songs was a tall stooped shape among the plum trees at the far end of the orchard, standing between the two graves, not humming — she listened, and there was no tune coming off him, which she had never known before — just standing, in the last light, with the buds just breaking white above him.

Marcus came out and stood beside her, looking not at the orchard but at the house, at the lit kitchen window and the stack of old paper under the lamp.

"It explains the families," Kira said.

"It explains the heirs," said Marcus. "*Each contributor shall prepare an heir.*" He was quiet a moment, looking at the light. "Which means it isn't over. They didn't write a confession. They wrote a will."
