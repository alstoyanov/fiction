# Chapter 3: The Third Site

*Monday, March 25, 2115. The capital*

---

*March 25, 2115. 08:10. Commission basement, room B-4. Present: Osman, K.; Volkov, N. Subject: third site. Status: open, day 1,769.*

She wrote the day count without thinking and did not cross it out. She had counted them since the twenty-first of May, 2110 — the morning the machine ended and something nine minutes ahead of the cascade did not end but moved — and she had never told anyone she was counting, because a thousand seven hundred and sixty-nine days was not a professional number. It was the number of a person who had been left a question by a dying woman and a dying machine and had not been able to put it down. She let it stand in the log because the log was hers and nobody read it but Nikolai, and Nikolai counted things too.

Room B-4 was the basement room where the three of them had built the image in 2109, in the war, with the door locked and the ventilation loud: she and Nikolai and Maya, around a screen, turning two million altered journals into a picture a person could point at. It smelled the same, of warm dust and old cable. They had kept it. The Commission did not use the basement, and nobody had thought to take the room back, and so it had become, without anyone deciding, the place where the work that had no department went on.

Five years of patient listening had given them a shape, and the shape was all they had.

The route was the one thing they had been sure of from the start. It came from the maintenance log store's own printout, run on the twenty-eighth of May, 2110, a week after the cascade, before anyone understood to stop them: the restore of thirteen thirty-one to thirteen thirty-five, and the relays it had gone out through. She knew the four minutes better than she knew most minutes of her own life. The trail left the continent. It crossed the unaligned strips — the disputed country that belonged to no nation and ran its communications on old, badly kept equipment anyone could touch — and then it entered the relays of the CNT, and there it stopped being a trail and became a wall.

Past the border they had timing and volume and nothing else. Never content. They could see that a path was busy; they could not see what moved along it. Journal-network volunteers ran Nikolai's open tools at relay points in the unaligned strips, honest passive instruments that noted when certain paths carried traffic and how much, and logged it, and broke into nothing, because breaking in was the thing they had spent their lives refusing to do and would not start now for a good reason, there being no good reasons, only reasons. The CNT had the best cyber warfare in the world. Kira assumed, as a working premise, that everything she did was seen. She found the assumption clarifying. You behaved differently when you took it as given that you were watched, and the difference, she had noticed, was mostly that you behaved better.

She laid the pattern out in her notes the way she would lay out a report, because a thing written as a report could be handed to someone, and a thing written as a fear could not.

Small reconciliations, she wrote. Every three to five days. Irregular hours, no clock-face pattern she could find. Tiny in volume — nothing like a restore, nothing like an attack, a few packets where a restore would have been a flood. Not the signature of a weapon being readied. Not the signature of a vault being raided. The signature, she thought, and hesitated over the word because it was not an engineer's word, of upkeep. Of someone going along the rows every few days, doing the small necessary things, pulling the one weed, tying back the one branch, and going away again.

She wrote the sentence down before she could stop herself.

*Like someone tending a garden.*

Nikolai read it over her shoulder, on his way past with two mugs. He was forty, going soft at the jaw, funny and tired in the particular way of a man with a small child and three jobs, and he kept a copy of everything in three places because he had learned in the hardest possible manner what it cost to keep it in one. He set a mug down by her hand and read the line again.

"It's the right sentence," he said. "Leave it in."

She left it in. And then she sat with it a while, because it had told her something she had not meant to say. An attack has a shape, and she knew the shape; a theft has a shape, a rush, a grab, a getaway, and she knew that too. This had the shape of neither. This was the shape of someone who intended to be doing the same thing in ten years — in fifty — the shape of ownership so complete it had stopped being anxious, the shape of a person who walked the rows of a thing they were certain no one could take from them and did the small necessary maintenance and went away again and slept soundly. It was that certainty, more than any volume of traffic, that frightened her: not that the design had survived the cascade, but that it had survived it so calmly, that whoever tended the garden tended it like a thing that was theirs by right and in no danger, and that in five years of watching she had never once seen a tremor in it, never a scramble, never the signature of fear. Fear she could have worked with. Fear made mistakes. Patience this deep made none.

---

Someone was tending it. That much the garden told them. Viktoria Sokolov had gone east in March of 2110 and had not come back. The CNT had taken in people it called émigrés from Veridica all through the war and after it, quietly, without ceremony, and a nation that took in people took in what the people knew. The design had people, and the people were still working, patiently, every three to five days, out past a wall she could see the shape of and not through.

Nobody could name one of them.

She had a column in her notes where names would go, and the column was empty, and she kept it empty on purpose. She had spent too long in a world where a name went into a record and became true by being written to start writing names she could not stand behind. She could write *someone*. She could write *the design's people*. She would not write a name until a name could be verified, and until then the empty column was itself a kind of honesty, a blank she refused to fill with relief.

Singh was a week away. Tanaka's news had reached them all seven days ago, and on Thursday they would drive out to the farmhouse and hear, from a dead woman, whatever she had come back to say. Kira thought about it once, in the morning, and thought about it the way an engineer thinks about a loose variable: every operation in the lost hour had carried Singh's credentials, nine years ago, the credentials of a woman who had then been filed as dead; and now the woman had come back unfiled. That was all she let herself make of it. The rest was Thursday's.

She took Vance's page out of the top drawer of the desk, as she did some mornings, and did not unfold it.

It had lived in the same journal since the twenty-first of May, 2110, when a dying woman had pressed it into her hands across a farmhouse table and said *this time you asked*. She knew both sides of it by heart and had never needed to open it to know them. The front was Principle Seven, the machine's own death built into it by the one person who had been ashamed enough to build it. The back was Vance's hand, and the back was the thing Kira carried into every day in this room: *everything that restores from it ends with it.* The source and all its children, bound to the one ending. That was the design's own law, and it was why the cascade should have taken everything.

But what had run east at thirteen thirty-five had not restored from the source. She had understood that slowly, over five years, and it had become the floor she stood on. It had not copied the source. It had *become* the source — a new original, downstream of nothing, bound to no cascade, because you cannot end a thing by ending the thing it is a copy of when it is not a copy. Vance's law had a door in it, and someone nine minutes early had walked a whole world's record through the door before it shut.

She folded the page back into the journal and put the journal in the drawer.

She had carried that page five years, and it had changed what she was. She had been, once, an architect of the Spire, a woman who built the place where facts were kept and believed the keeping was clean; and then a fact she had entered with her own hands had vanished, and she had been convicted at ninety-two per cent of doubting a truth she had herself made, and nothing after that had been clean again, and she had decided, in the long slow years since, that nothing ever had been, that the cleanness had been the lie, that a record that could be made could be unmade by whoever held the making of it. Vance's page was the proof of it in the designer's own hand: *everything that restores from it ends with it.* The whole terrible logic of the thing on one line. And a door in the logic, which someone had walked a world through, nine minutes early, into the east. Kira had spent five years learning to read a thing by the habits it could not help leaving, and she had come to think this was the only honest work there was — not deciding what was true, which was how you ended with a machine, but comparing, patiently, what two records said, and showing the difference, and letting people with the difference in front of them do the one thing a machine had been built to take away from them, which was decide.

---

Maya came at one with lunch in a cloth, the way she did most days, and stayed twenty minutes, and asked after Tamsin, and told them the small news of the Civic Record office where she worked now, filing the testimony of ordinary days, and then went back to it. Nikolai watched her go up the basement stairs the way a tired man watches a good thing leave a room, and then sat back down, and for an hour it was an ordinary day, and Kira let it be one, because you had to let some of them be ordinary or you went the way Marcus had nearly gone, chasing an edge until you cut yourself on it.

At sixteen fifty-two the fault happened.

A relay in one of the unaligned strips, old and badly maintained, misrouted a single packet — sent it a hop it should not have taken, out into the open where an honest passive tool was listening, and for once the tool logged the thing whole instead of as a mark on a timing chart. Nikolai saw it first, went very still, and said her name once, and she came round the desk.

It was a reconciliation notice. One short structured record, the kind a record store emits when it has finished reconciling itself with another store: *I have synchronised, this is where we now agree, this is the sequence.* Its content was opaque — a hash, a sequence number, the arithmetic of agreement, nothing a human eye could read as meaning. But it was signed.

She read the signature the way she had read the Gap's seam in the winter of 2108 and the Rewriting's countersignatures in the autumn of 2109, which was to say she did not read the name in it, because there was no name in it, but the habits: the rotation, the particular padding, the way the whole thing was shaped to leave nothing behind that could be set against it later, no edge, no contradiction, a signature built by someone who thought about contradiction for a living. Nikolai pulled his 2108 profile up on the second screen without being asked, the library of key-habits he had built in the war, and laid the two beside each other, and neither of them said anything for a moment.

It matched the Chronos Temporal Archive's senior reconciliation key. The one that had countersigned the Chronos Gap. The one that had countersigned the Rewriting. The key that had been Julian Kovač's from January of 2108, when it was handed to him, and lawfully no one's since.

That key had died on the twenty-first of May, 2110, with Chronos's Judge, because everything that restored from the source had ended when the source ended, and the Chronos reconciliation key restored from the source. It was supposed to be as dead as the machine it had served. It was, by every law of the design, impossible.

---

She told Marcus that night at the kitchen table, plainly, with the curtain drawn and Tamsin long asleep. She did not dramatise it. She laid it out as she had laid it in her notes, the garden, the misrouted packet, the signature, the dead key that had signed something six days — no, a reconciliation signed *recently*, within the week. Marcus listened the way he listened, completely, and at the end of it he took out the fourth list, the one with *QUESTIONS FOR THE WAR* at the top, and unfolded it on the table between them, and looked at the last line, *Where is east?*, and did not write anything under it. He only looked at it, a long time, as though if he looked at it correctly it might answer.

Kira was not looking at the list. She was looking at the drawn curtain, and she was very angry, in the level, contained way that was the only way she had ever learned to be angry, the anger that made her precise instead of loud.

"A key doesn't outlive the thing it signs for," she said. "Not unless someone decided it would. The cascade took everything that restored from the source. It didn't take this."

"No," said Marcus.

"Which means this doesn't restore from the source either." She turned from the curtain and looked at him. "It *is* the source. And the hand on it is Julian's hand, five years after Julian's hand should be gone." She let the plain fact of it sit between them on the dark table. "Someone kept a copy of the key too."
