# Chapter 28: A Better Enemy

*Monday, October 7, 2115. The capital*

---

*IO-2115-143. 7 OCTOBER. Flat. Broadcast expected 19:00, CNT channels, Synthesis relay.*

He filed it before it happened, which was discipline, and he had the photograph pinned above the desk where he could see it, which was not, and he had decided some time ago to stop pretending the two were separable. Five weeks since the coast. Chen Zhao gone on the second of September by the evening tide, Sarah Okonkwo by air that same morning, others gone whom he had counted and not named. Wei's trial opened next week. His aunt had not written, and he had not expected her to, and he had caught himself three times that month hoping she would, and had filed the hope each time, under the heading he kept for the things about himself he did not approve of and recorded anyway.

At seven the CNT Council sent its broadcast, openly, on its own external channels, and Synthesis carried it, and so it came up on screens in every nation at once, which was itself the message: *we are not hiding; we will speak to all of you at once.* Nikolai checked within the hour and sent the one line Isaiah needed: it had come through no one's interface. It was a picture and a voice, a thing you watched, not a thing laid into your mind in the night. A broadcast, in the old sense, the sense that left you free to disbelieve it. Kira and Marcus were on the voice line from their flat. Julian was on it from Chronos, with Adrian beside him; Isaiah could hear the two of them breathing, a continent away, over the open line.

Chairman Timur Aslanov spoke first. He was about fifty-five, square, plain, nothing like the woodcut of his father in the children's primer and everything like the photograph on the last page of it. He spoke simply. The Confederacy, he said, had given refuge to the founders who had been driven from their homes by the nations they had served, and would go on giving it, because the Confederacy did not surrender guests. His father had called the first Supreme Council in 2068, he said, and he did not deny the mark on the Register, RA·12; he said it as a man says a thing he has decided not to be ashamed of; and there was no apology in it, and there was no threat in it either, which Isaiah noted, because the absence of the threat was itself a move.

Then Viktoria Sokolov spoke, about seventy-nine, upright, grey, in a grey room, and she made the argument, calm and humane, in short sentences, the Architects' register, the register Isaiah had heard across a tea table in April and would hear in his sleep for the rest of his life.

"You united against us," she said. "For the first time in your history, Veridica and Chronos and the Sovereignty stood together, against the same thing, in the same week. You have not been able to do that in sixty years, and you did it against us. Ask yourselves how that felt. Ask yourselves whether it did not feel, a little, like relief." She let it sit. "You need an enemy. Your whole world was built on the premise that you need an enemy, and the premise was correct, as premises built by people who studied you for forty years tend to be. We have not taken anything from you. We have only given you a better one — cleaner, simpler, out in the open, a whole nation to be against, instead of the exhausting business of being against one another. You just changed the enemy."

Isaiah wrote down how she said the word *enemy.* She said it the way people say *tool.*

And the worst of it, the thing he would wrestle with until dawn, was that she was not wrong. He had spent five years watching his own country, and he had seen what she described from the inside: the strange steadying effect of the broadcast from the east, the way the Meridian Bridge filled with people who had spent five years quietly frightened of each other's memories and now had, for the first time, a single clean thing to be frightened of together. She had named a real hunger. The design had always been right about the hunger; that was the horror that ran under the whole series, that these people were not fools and were not simply cruel, that they had looked at the human animal with forty years of unblinking attention and seen something true — that a people united is a people that has agreed on an enemy, that the fastest road to *us* runs through *them* — and had built a world on the truth of it. Viktoria Sokolov was offering the capital the oldest comfort there was, and the capital wanted it, and Isaiah stood at his window and watched the horns and the crowd and understood that the argument he had to write against was not a lie. It was a truth, weaponised. Those were always the hardest to answer, because you could not answer them by calling them false; you could only answer them by refusing, with your eyes open, the thing they correctly told you that you wanted.

What he noticed, and filed without concluding, was who was not on the screen. His aunt was not on it. Chen Zhao was not on it. The two who had written principles — Two, and Six, the diplomat and the politician, the ones who made the arguments — were kept off camera, and the ones who spoke were the Chairman, who administered, and Viktoria, who administered, the Annex hands, the operators. The ones who could persuade were not being shown; the ones who could run a thing were. He did not know what it meant. He wrote *they are keeping the persuaders off the screen; why* and left it.

And third, at Viktoria's shoulder, stood a man of about seventy who said nothing at all. He stood a little behind her, in the grey room, his hands folded, his round mild face turned toward the camera, present and silent, and Timur, at the end, named him once, courteously, as the Council's guest, Dr. Augustin Mera. Isaiah had seen the name on a public roll and in a letter. He had not seen the face except in a photograph.

---

The circle's line went quiet after it ended. Nobody on it said anything for a while; Isaiah could hear Marcus not speaking, and Kira not speaking, and Julian, a continent away, not speaking.

The capital outside did the opposite. By eight there were horns. By nine there was a crowd on the Meridian Bridge, and by the late bulletins the word in the crowd was *invade,* and the word in the SFW press, which the bulletins quoted, was *offensive.* Isaiah went to the window and counted what he could see and kept the numbers modest, because modest numbers were true and large numbers were the raw material of the thing Viktoria wanted: a few thousand on the bridge, loud, frightened, reaching for the better enemy they had just been handed, exactly as designed.

He wrote the answer overnight.

He had been writing against his aunt's arithmetic since April — *you never do the arithmetic, Isaiah* — and tonight, at last, he understood what the answer to the arithmetic was, which was not a better arithmetic but a refusal to do sums at all with human beings, and he wrote the column with no arithmetic in it and no adjectives and no verdict. He gave each name. The mark. The principle, or the office. What the person had done, and when. Where the person was now. He set his aunt down in exactly the same form as all the rest, no softer and no harder: *Sarah Okonkwo. SO·04. Principle Two. Ambassador, retired. Left Veridica by air, 2 September 2115. Present location: the Confederacy.* He gave no condemnation, because the facts carried their own weight and did not need his, and because a condemnation was precisely the thing Viktoria's argument fed on — rage was the enemy she wanted, rage was the uniting, rage was the proof that the world needed an enemy — and he would not feed it. He gave the names, flat, in order, so that each reader could do with them whatever a free person did with a fact, which was decide.

It was the hardest column he had ever written and the plainest, and the two were the same thing. Every instinct of the trade, every instinct his aunt had tried to shame out of him across a tea table in the spring, pulled toward the verdict — toward the adjective, the indictment, the sentence that told the reader how to feel, because a reader told how to feel is a reader you have moved, and moving readers was the whole seduction of the work. He wrote none of it. He had learned, at last, what his aunt had meant and the opposite of what she had meant: she had said he never did the arithmetic, and she was right, and the answer was not to start doing it but to refuse the whole calculus, to lay the names down without a thumb on any scale and trust the reader to be a person and not a sum. A verdict would have been a gift to Viktoria — more heat, more rage, more of the uniting fury she had just told the world it could not live without. The refusal of the verdict was the only thing in his power that the broadcast from the east had no answer to: not a louder enemy, but a quieter page, the names and what they did and where they were, and a question at the end that he genuinely could not answer and would not pretend to.

His last line was a question he could not answer, because his pieces ended on those, and because this one was the truest he had:

*They have told us who our enemy is. Which of us will decide whether to believe them?*

In the palace, when he went in, the chair at the head of the parlour table was his, had been his since April, and he sat in it, and found, for the first time in his life, that he had nothing to file — that he had written the thing down already, out here, in the world, where it would be read by many hands, and that there was nothing left to carry alone into a room in his head, because he had stopped keeping it for himself and started keeping it for everyone, which was the whole of what the old man had been trying to teach him since a cold back room in 2107.

---

He looked up from the finished page at the photograph pinned above his desk.

The Chronos Temporal Archive staff, 2107. Three rows of historians on the Archive steps. It had come to him in the spring, in an envelope in Helena Kovač's hand, with a note that Julian had asked her to pass it on: *He files things. Ask him to file this.* He had filed it. He had not known, in the spring, why Julian had sent it, and he knew now.

The man in the front row, second from the left, round-faced, mild, a hand resting on the shoulder of the thin young historian beside him, was the man who had just stood silent at Viktoria Sokolov's shoulder, eight years younger. Augustin Mera. Mera, who had retired in January of 2108 and been cremated within a day of his death in February of that year, a requiem said over a light urn, the pattern exactly Singh's pattern, the pattern of the design's unkillable: a death in the record, a fast cremation, a body nobody who could not be seeded had ever seen. Mera had not died. Mera had been spared, or had spared himself, and had gone east, and had kept a copy of a key, and had tended a garden for five years, and was alive, standing in a grey room on a continent's worth of screens, having said nothing at all.

On the open line from Chronos, where Julian had been listening the whole night, Julian said nothing either.

Isaiah did not say anything into the silence, because he understood what Julian's silence was — not shock, Julian had suspected since the spring, which was why he had sent the photograph; it was the thing a man does when the person who destroyed him turns out to be alive and well and standing at the shoulder of the people who ruined his nation, and there is no sentence for it, and the only honest thing is not to pretend there is. The line stayed open. The two of them sat on either end of it, a continent apart, and did not speak, and the photograph looked down from the wall, and that was how the night ended, with an open line and a silence on it and a dead man's face pinned over a desk.
