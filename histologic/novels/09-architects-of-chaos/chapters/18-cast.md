# Chapter 18: Cast

*Monday, June 24, 2115. The SFW capital*

---

President Beatriz Martinez read everything twice. She had learned it in twenty years in intelligence, before the people made the mistake of electing her, and she had never managed to unlearn it, and it had served her as President better than any single other habit: she read a document once for what it said and once for what the writer had wanted her to feel, and she trusted the first reading and watched the second.

Her office was in an old stone building with walls a yard thick and no screens on them, because the SFW had no interfaces and no broadcast network and no particular wish for one; there were human judges, paper minutes, and the oral testimony of the town assemblies, which were loud and slow and, she thought, as good a way as any yet devised of finding out what had actually happened in a place. She wrote her own notes by hand. She had no Judge to ask. It had genuinely never occurred to her to want one; when the Histologic defectors spoke, as they sometimes did, of the terrible comfort of certainty, she listened the way she listened to a traveller describe a drug she had never been offered, with interest and no envy. Her grandmother's people had come east over the water in the Fracture Years, carrying the family name through two wars, and had settled among the unaligned and then among the SFW when there was an SFW to settle among, and Beatriz had grown up on the story and taken from it one conviction, which was that a people who could not be lied to could not be herded, and that this was worth the loudness and the slowness and the arguing in the halls.

The Sovereignty was not a comfortable country. It argued about everything, in the open, at volume; its town assemblies ran past midnight; its decisions were slow and often wrong and always contested, and a stranger from a Histologic nation, dropped into one of its halls, would have mistaken the ordinary business of self-government for the early stages of a civil war. Beatriz had served it her whole adult life and she knew exactly what it cost and exactly what it bought. It cost efficiency and certainty and peace of mind. It bought the one thing the nations across the water had sold for all three, which was that no one here could tell a Sovereignty citizen what had happened to him and make him believe it; he would argue; he would always argue; the arguing was the whole of his freedom and the whole of his protection, and a people that argued could be led nowhere it had not talked itself into going. She had spent her life defending the right to argue, and she was about to read forty pages proving that the quiet, orderly, certain nations across the water had been designed to make that argument impossible, and that her own loud country had been designed, too, as the enemy that justified their quiet.

Isaiah Okonkwo arrived by the diplomatic air route in the morning, as the message from the circle had said he would, with one hand-copied set in the lining of his coat. He was courteous and formal — *Madam President* — and he laid the eight sheets on her desk and then folded his hands and did not touch them again, which she noted, because a man who kept his hands off a document after presenting it was a man who wanted it read without his thumb on the scale. She noted also that he had resewn the coat himself; the stitching at the lining was careful and not a tailor's. The copyist's name and the countersigner's were on the last sheet, two names she did not know, and a date.

She read it through once without comment.

Then she read it again with a pencil.

Principle Two she already knew; she had seen Isaiah's own prints of it in May of 2110, at the withdrawal, when she had learned that her country had been *cast.* *A system without opposition appears tyrannical… let there be enemies outside them all… Controlled conflict answers every question before it is asked.* She knew the words. What she had not had, in 2110, was the foot of the page. She had it now: *SO·04.* And a name against the mark on the Register sheet. She looked up.

"My aunt," Isaiah said, and said nothing else, and she did not ask, because his face had told her the price of the two words and she was not going to make him pay more of it for her curiosity.

She read the Annex, the dull terrible schedule of it, and stopped at the line about the placement of Histologic capitals and borders, and thought of the maps on the wall of the council room next door, the maps her service had been built around, the lakeshore and the Caucasus line and the minefields, every border she had spent her life studying as the given facts of the world — drawn, it seemed, on these pages, as choices, by people deciding where a war would be convenient.

And then RA·12. *Rustam Aslanov. Founder of the CNT's Supreme Council, 2068.*

She knew the name the way every SFW officer of her generation knew it, from the history of the enemy — Aslanov the Founder, the hard man who had called the first Council against the Histologic expansion, the face on the other side of forty-seven years. The SFW–CNT border wars of 2091 to 2093, the Caucasus line, the mined valleys, the three thousand dead her own country still mourned each spring: the whole architecture of her nation's long enmity with the CNT, the rivalry that had given the SFW its shape and its army and its grief. And here was the Founder's mark on the same forty pages as the SFW's own casting, the twelfth signatory of the design the SFW had defined itself against. The design had needed two enemies outside the Histologic world, and it had needed them to hate each other, so that neither would look too hard at the thing they were both outside of. She and Aslanov's Council had been the two outer walls of one room, each told the other was the danger.

"We were cast," she said, aloud, to the thick-walled room. She remembered saying it to Isaiah at the withdrawal table. *Then we were cast.* And his answer, or her own: *Before I was born.* She had been right, and she had not known a fraction of how right. The SFW's dead from the Battle of Blood had been playing a part written in 1966. Farahani, killed on the coast road in the last week by a battery that never got the ceasefire order, had died in a scene the design had sketched before his grandparents met. She had negotiated the rest of that withdrawal herself, line by line from the twenty-seventh of May, 2110, and she could still recite the names of the prisoners she had got back, and she did, sometimes, at night, to keep them real.

Kerr's page she read slowly, twice, by her own rule, and then a third time, which was not her rule. *A record kept by one hand is a confession waiting for its torturer. Keep it by many hands.*

"That," she said, "is the only one of these eight pages I would have signed."

---

In the afternoon she put it to her ministers in the council room, under the maps, and they divided, as she had known they would.

One wanted to publish at once and loudly: the SFW had been right about the Histologic nations from the beginning, had said for forty years that they were not seven quarrelling countries but one managed thing, and here at last was the proof in the managers' own hand, and the SFW should be the one to hold it up. Another wanted to publish and invade: if the CNT had been built by the Histologic design, then the CNT was theirs, an outgrowth of the enemy, a legitimate target, and the moment of their exposure was the moment of their weakness, and the SFW had an army that had been waiting forty years for a clean reason.

Martinez shut the second one down flatly and without debate, because some ideas you killed in the room they were born in or they followed you home. "The CNT is forty million people with no interfaces who have been lied to about their own history their whole lives," she said. "They are not the design. They are the design's other victims, and we are not going to prove the dead men in 1966 right by marching on the people they fooled. No." And that was the end of it.

The SFW would publish on the fifteenth of July, at two o'clock, as the circle had asked: printed, and read aloud in every town assembly, because reading aloud in the assembly was the SFW's own way of making a thing part of the record, older than any press, the thing a people did so that no one could later say they had not been told. She gave Isaiah her word on it, and her word was good, and he wrote it down with her watching, which she respected.

---

Alone, in the evening, she let herself think the dangerous thought all the way through, because a thought you refused to finish was a thought that ran your decisions from the dark.

If the enemies were designed, then peace could be designed. That was the shape of it, cold and clean. If forty-seven years of enmity had been an artifact, a thing somebody drew, then it could be undrawn; some arrangement, some structure, some guarantee could be built that would mean no mother in any of her towns ever again opened her door to a man holding a cap and a folded letter. She had signed four hundred of those letters. She knew the number exactly because she had made herself learn it, one evening, going through the files, so that she would never be the kind of leader for whom the dead were a round figure. Four hundred. She would sign almost anything — she made herself feel the whole weight of the *almost* — to never sign the four hundred and first.

She recognised the thought as the most dangerous one she had had in years, and she did not make it go away, because making it go away would only have hidden it. She set it on the desk, metaphorically, where she could watch it, next to the real documents.

Because here was the trap she could feel the shape of without yet seeing its face: that the Register, which proved the war had been designed, would be used to argue that the war could be undesigned — that if enemies could be drawn, peace could be drawn too, by someone clever enough and trusted enough, some arrangement that would guarantee what no amount of arguing had ever guaranteed, which was that it would not happen again. And she, of all people, was the one it would work on, because she wanted the guarantee more than she wanted to be right, had wanted it in the small hours for five years, would trade a great deal of her country's loud freedom for the certainty that no door in any of her towns would open again onto a man with a cap in his hands. That was the hook, and she could feel it set, and she left the thought on the desk where she could see it precisely because a hook you could see was a hook you might, with effort, on the day, decline to swallow.

---

In the courtyard at evening, Isaiah left for the airfield. At the gate he turned, in the dry filing voice she was coming to like, and said, "A plan carried by many hands is not a secret, Madam President. People will know the date. We're not hiding it. I'd want you to expect that, so that when you hear the date from three directions before the fifteenth, you don't take it for a leak that means something. It means only that we told the truth to a great many people."

"I'll expect it," she said, and filed it, the way he filed things, because it was good advice and she had learned good advice wherever she found it.

She went back up to her office. Her secretary came in with a card that had come through the Synthesis mission that afternoon — good paper, the Synthesis seal, and on it a short courteous request. An envoy of Synthesis, unnamed, respectfully asked the President for a private meeting, at her convenience, on a durable peace.

She read it twice, by her rule.

Then she laid it down on top of the Register, where the two of them sat together on her desk in the lamplight, the forty pages that said the peace had always been managed, and the single card offering her a durable one, and she looked at the pair of them for a long time before she turned off the lamp.
