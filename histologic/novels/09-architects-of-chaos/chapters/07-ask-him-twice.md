# Chapter 7: Ask Him Twice

*Monday, April 8, 2115. The capital's Historical Archive*

---

He had opened the riddle a line a week, the way he had learned a long tune as a boy, and now there was only the last line left, and he had brought it to the building where it had been written for him to solve.

Line two had come at the stove, a week after the orchard. *Behind the number no one reads.* The number was 114. He had known it the moment he let the line settle: the shelf mark he had given the grey journal with his own hand, *Periodicals, Governance*, and the number on the run of it, 114, the number that had closed up so neatly after the sweeping that the labels went 113, then 115, with no gap you could see — which was not how numbers ran in an archive that was telling the truth, because an archive that was telling the truth left the gap and noted why. Line three had come in the orchard, among the buds. *The old man peeled his apple there.* Kerr, at eleven, every morning, in the reading room, one apple, the peel coming off in a single long curl under the little pocketknife he kept for it and nothing else. Both lines pointed into this building. Both were the Archive.

On Thursday evening, before he went up to bed and before Singh began, he had given Kovač a slip of paper with four words on it: *Archive. Periodicals. — M.P.* She had read it, and nodded, and put it in her coat, and arranged the rest without a single question, because she was a woman who understood that some requests came already carrying their reasons and only needed to be obeyed.

---

She walked him in on Monday morning, Helena Kovač, sixty-one, in her dark coat, her rimless glasses catching the light of the entrance hall. The pass clipped to his coat read *VISITOR: PETROV, M. SPONSOR: KOVAČ, H.* The Civic Record still listed Mikhail Petrov as deceased; he had declined, in 2106, to be brought back to life on paper, and had never regretted it, and nobody at the entrance desk of a post-war archive checked the pass of an old man against the register of the dead, because the dead did not visit and the staff had better things to do. He was a dead man with a visitor's pass, walking back into the building that had taken his teacher, and he found, going through the turnstile, that it suited him. There was a freedom in it. A dead man owed the building nothing.

He had not set foot in it since 1967, since the year they took Kerr, and he had expected to feel the forty-eight years as a weight at the door and did not; what he felt instead was the opposite, a lightness, almost a mischief, that he should be here at all, that the building which had been the instrument of so much quiet erasure should now open its turnstile to a man it had declared dead, sponsored by a registrar whose own uncle's name he did not yet know was on the page they were hunting. The archive had outlasted the régime that corrupted it, as archives do; it was only a building, after all, only shelves and floor polish and a green-shaded lamp, indifferent to which government it served, keeping whatever it was told to keep and losing whatever it was told to lose; and he had come back into it not to settle a score with it but to take out of it the one thing it had been made to lose, which was still inside it, filed, because a bureaucracy cannot help itself, because the instinct that erases is the same instinct that files the erasure, and the two instincts had been at war inside this building for fifty years without either of them knowing it, and he had come to collect the spoils of that war.

The floor polish was the same. Forty-eight years and three governments and a war, and the same wax, the same smell, so that his feet knew the corridors before his eyes did and his body went where it had gone when he was twenty-five, down the right-hand passage toward the periodicals, past the back room where the quarterlies nobody read had been kept, where there was still — he saw it through the half-open door — a lamp with a green glass shade on a desk in the corner. He did not go to that desk. He knew which desk had the drawer that stuck, and he did not go to it, because some rooms you could walk into and some you could only walk past, and the desk with the drawer was one he would walk past today and come back to, later, at the very end, when he knew how. He hummed the Lakeshore Air under his breath the whole way, the ferry verses, low. Kovač heard it, and did not mention it, which was one of the several reasons he trusted her.

The Governance shelves stood where they had stood. The labels on the spines of the run read, in sequence, in the Archive's own neat numbering: 112, 113 — and then 115, 116. He stood in front of the join for a long moment. It was so cleanly done. A person walking the shelf would feel nothing wrong; the eye slid from 113 to 115 the way it slid over a familiar word with a letter missing. He had catalogued this run himself. He knew there had been a 114.

He did the obvious thing the riddle seemed to ask, because you did the obvious thing first and let it fail before you earned the right to the subtle one. With Kovač steadying the heavy bound volumes, he drew out 113 and 115 and looked into the gap behind them, into the space *behind the number no one reads*. There was the bare pine back of the shelving, and fifty years of dust lying undisturbed in a fur along it, and nothing else. No packet. No envelope. No hollow. He had half known there would be nothing — a thing hidden behind a physical shelf for fifty years would have been found in any one of a dozen stock-takes — but the riddle had to be allowed to mean the simple thing before it was allowed to mean the hard one. He and Kovač put the volumes back exactly, 113, 115, and he brushed the dust off his hands, and the join closed up again as though it had never been opened.

---

At eleven he went into the reading room and sat at the long table.

It was the table. He was sure of it; the grain ran the way it had run under Elise's inky fingers on the Thursdays of 1964 and 1965, when she had come in from the systems department and sat across from him and whispered that rhyme was a compression algorithm and he had whispered back that it was a prayer, and neither of them had given an inch, and she had been nearly always right, and he had never told her so until he stood over her grave. The light came in the tall windows the same way. A young duty archivist sat at the far desk with a sandwich, eating it at eleven o'clock because eleven o'clock was when she was hungry, and she did not look up, and there was no reason she should.

Nobody peeled an apple.

That was the thing. The room smelled of floor polish and old paper and the duty archivist's sandwich, and it did not smell of apples, and the absence of the smell of apples was the sharpest thing in the whole building, sharper than the swept shelf, sharper than the green-shaded lamp. For two years a man had peeled an apple at this hour at this table in one long unbroken curl, courteous, exact, pretending not to hear two young people argue about rhyme, and then they had taken his memory and he had stopped, and now there was a woman with a sandwich, and the room did not know it had lost anything, and that was what they did to you, that was the whole of what they did: they took the thing, and then they took the room's memory of the thing, so that the absence did not even register as an absence to anyone but the one old man who had been there.

That was the cruelty of it he had never been able to make the young ones understand, not even Marcus, who of all of them had come closest. It was not the taking. Everyone imagined the taking — the white room, the machine, the thing drawn out of you — and the taking was terrible, but the taking was not the worst of it. The worst was the smoothing-over, the way the world closed seamlessly behind the thing it had lost, so that there was no scar, no empty chair, nothing for anyone to grieve or even to notice; a man was peeled out of a room, and the room went on exactly as before with a different person's sandwich at eleven o'clock, and the only evidence that anything had ever been otherwise was one old archivist's refusal to stop smelling an apple that no one had peeled in half a century. That was why the song. That was why, when he was twenty-six and understood what they had done to Kerr, he had begun to put things into tunes — because a tune leaves a gap when it stops, because a round that cannot be sung by one voice announces, by its own silence, that a voice is missing. A record could be smoothed. A song could not; it could only be unsung, and an unsung song was an audible hole; and he had spent fifty years making holes that could be heard.

He hummed the riddle, under his breath, the whole of it, to the Lakeshore Air, and let the last line come, and the tune lifted toward it the way his tunes always lifted.

*Ask him the number. Ask him twice.*

And it stopped.

The memory came up whole and exact, the way the worst of them did. One morning by the periodicals trolley, after Kerr came back from being helped — thinner, courteous, wrong — Mikhail had said the shelf mark to him, very quietly, the way you hold out a hand to someone to see if they will take it. *Periodicals, Governance, one-fourteen.* And Kerr had smiled at him with perfect courtesy and no recognition at all, the smile of a well-mannered stranger, and said:

*"I'm afraid I don't know that one. Are you sure you have the number right?"*

He had only ever asked him once. He had said the shelf mark that one time, and watched the nothing behind his teacher's eyes, and never said it again, because saying it again would have been cruel, would have been making an old man fail a test he did not know he was sitting. He had asked once and got nothing back.

But Kerr had asked twice.

Politely. In writing. Twice. Where the issues had gone, and on whose authority — he had asked it the way an archivist asks, so that the catalogue could record it, so that the record would be right. He had asked once and been met with silence, and then, because he was the kind of man who thought an archive was a promise and silence was a clerical error, he had asked again. *He asked politely. He asked in writing. He asked twice.* Mikhail's one spoken question had gone into the air and died. Kerr's two written questions would not have died, because a written question to an archive was a document, and a document was filed, whether or not it was ever answered — especially if it was never answered, because an unanswered inquiry was precisely the kind of loose end a careful registry kept, in case it was ever needed to show the inquiry had been dealt with.

*Behind the number no one reads* was not a shelf. It was not a hollow in the pine. It was paper — paper filed about a number that officially did not exist, letters that no one reads because no one ever reopens an inquiry into a thing that was never there. She had not hidden the copy behind the physical 114. She had hidden it behind the *inquiry* into the missing 114, in the one file the design would never reopen, because reopening it meant admitting there had been something to inquire about.

The thought arrived in him entire, in words, and he knew the words were hers as much as his:

*Bureaucracies never destroy the inquiry about the thing they destroyed. They file it.*

He took the ledger page out of his coat, the farmhouse ledger page from the twentieth of March with its one underlined line, and he laid it on the reading-room table, and with his shaking hand he wrote beneath line one the other three, slowly, forming each letter, until the whole riddle stood on the page outside his head for the first time since she had given it to him. Then he folded it once and held it out across the table to Kovač.

It was the first time the riddle had been in another person's hands. She took it, and read it, and read it again, and he watched her understand it in two passes, the folders in her mind sorting themselves. He said the only words he spoke in that building all day.

"He asked twice."

Kovač nodded. She did not need the rest explained; she had understood the moment she read it that it ended in a correspondence file, because correspondence files were the country she lived in.

---

Folders were her element, and registries more than folders. She explained it to him quietly as they went down the service stairs to the basement, her voice the voice of a woman walking a roster she had walked a thousand times. In the 2060s, she said, the Archive's own letters — what it sent and what it received — were logged on paper index cards, by correspondent, a card for each person who wrote to the Archive or was written to by it, and the letters themselves were boxed by year and shelved down here in the registry, separate from the catalogue entirely. The cards had never been entered into the catalogue; they were an internal finding-aid, the registry's private index of its own post. And the catalogue was what the design had purged, in '66 and '67, scrubbing every trace of the grey journal out of the record that the public could reach. The cards were not in the catalogue. Nobody had thought of the cards. You did not think of the index to the thing; you thought of the thing.

The basement registry office smelled of cold concrete and old buff paper. The card cabinet stood against the long wall, steel, its many shallow drawers labelled by letter in a typeface older than either of them. Kovač put her finger on the drawer marked **K**, 2060–2069, and drew it out on its runners, and it came with the particular grudging smoothness of good steel furniture kept oiled by people long dead, and she walked the cards with one finger, tilting them forward one by one, the way she had told him she used to walk a duty roster looking for the one name that was in the wrong place.

Her finger stopped.

She lifted one card a little out of the drawer so that the strip light caught it, and turned it so he could read it, and it was in a hand he knew, a neat archivist's hand he had not seen in forty-eight years, the hand that had written cards at the desk beside his when he was twenty-five:

*Kerr, A. Inquiry re: Periodicals, Governance, 114. 1st letter. 2nd letter.*

He looked at the number, 114, written in his teacher's own hand in the weeks after the box went out of the building, carried for half a century in a steel drawer that no one had opened, the only place in all of Veridica where the number still existed; and he stood in the cold basement and could not, for a moment, say anything at all.
