# Prologue: The Fifteenth

*Friday, March 15, 2115. The central plaza of the capital*

---

*15 March 2115. 13:41. Central plaza. Weather: cold, clear, wind from the west. Attendance: some hundreds. Notebook: the twelfth.*

She wrote it standing, the notebook braced against her forearm, in the small upright hand she had used for case notes for thirty years and had never managed to unlearn, though there were no cases now, and no ward, and no machine waiting to read what she wrote. She had begun the first of these notebooks in the spring of 2107, in the month after the hour came back, because a woman who had spent her life keeping records did not stop keeping them simply because the record had stopped being compulsory. She kept her own now. That was the whole of the difference, and it was the whole of everything.

Yuki Tanaka was fifty-five. She was small and very straight, and she stood at the edge of the crowd the way she had once stood at the foot of a bed, present and slightly apart, noting. There was a thin pale scar at her right temple, old now, silvered, that she touched sometimes without knowing she did it. The wind came off the lake and up the avenues and across the plaza from the west, and it was the kind of cold that was clean rather than cruel, March pretending at winter and not meaning it. She had worn the good coat. She noticed she had worn the good coat and did not examine why.

The stone stood a few metres from the fountain, pale gray and waist-high, plainer than anything else in the square. There had been proposals, in the first year, for something larger: a column, a figure, an eternal flame that a committee could be photographed lighting. The people who came on the fifteenth had wanted none of it. They had wanted a stone you could put your hand on, and they had got one, and the inscription cut into the top of it was the only grand thing about it.

*TO THE HOUR THAT WAS LOST, AND THE TRUTH THAT WAS FOUND.*

Since 2110 there were names cut into its side. They had started on the eastern face and worked around, the war's dead, taken from the journals because the journals were the record now and there was no other, from both shores and from the Sovereignty, a general and a gunner and a girl of nine, all of them the same size in the stone. Tanaka found *Alexei Volkov* without looking for him, and *Nora Chen* a hand's width below, the way you find the step at the bottom of your own stairs in the dark. She did not stop at them. She had stopped at them for a year and then found that she carried them without stopping, which she understood to be what the stone was for.

It was strange to her still, after five years, how quiet the end of the world had turned out to be. She had expected, when the machine died in 2110 — when both machines died, Veridica's and Chronos's, in the same cascade on the same afternoon — that the aftermath would be loud, that a people which had been told for forty years what had happened to it would come apart when the telling stopped. It had not come apart. It had done this instead: it had built a waist-high stone in a square and come to it on the fifteenth of every month to say, out loud, to each other, what it remembered. That was the whole of the new world, as far as Tanaka could see it from the edge of the crowd — not a revolution, not a new certainty to replace the old one, but a few thousand people in the cold agreeing to carry their days by hand. She had expected grander and been given this, and she had come, over five years, to think that this was the grander thing, and that she had simply not known, before, what grandeur looked like when it was real.

At fourteen hundred the readings began.

It was the fifteenth, as it had been on the fifteenth of every month for eight years, and the shape of it never changed, which was its point. Anyone might stand at the stone and say what they remembered of one ordinary day. A Commission clerk sat at a folding table with a ledger and wrote each account down by hand; the speaker signed; and then a second person who had been there that day signed beside them, so that no day stood on one voice alone. It was slow. It was meant to be slow.

A tram driver went first, a big man with a cap in his hands, and recited the stops of the number four line as they had run on the second of March, *Harbour, Mint, the two churches, the hospital, the long hill*, and said that the long hill had been gritted that morning and he had been glad of it, and a woman near the front called out that she had been on the seven forty and it was so, the hill was gritted, and she came and signed, and they did not know each other, and that was the whole of it.

A woman in a green scarf read a list of what she had bought on the ninth of February — flour, lamp oil, a blue thread to match a thing she did not name, four apples, one of which was bad — and her neighbour signed for the apples, laughing, because she had heard about the bad apple for a week.

A boy of perhaps ten stood on a crate and said that it had snowed on his birthday, and his grandmother, without rising, said gently from her folding stool that it had not, that it had rained and he had wanted it to be snow, and the boy thought about this with a frown and then said, all right, it rained, and the clerk wrote *rain* and the boy signed under it, and the thing Tanaka watched was the boy's face, which did not crumple at being corrected, because the correcting had cost him nothing and the woman doing it loved him. She had seen, in a white room, what it was to have a certainty taken out of a person. This was the opposite of that, done in the open, in the cold, for free. She wrote nothing about it. Some things she only watched.

Then the crowd sang the refrain of "The Names" once, unled, the way they did, rising at the end like a question nobody answered — *name them, and they stay* — and went back to the readings.

She had watched a great many of these afternoons now, twelve notebooks of them, and the thing that never stopped astonishing her was how little it took and how much it held. A tram driver and his gritted hill. A bad apple. A boy who wanted snow. Nothing in any of it would have survived a single second of the old régime's attention; none of it was important; and that was the point her whole profession had missed for forty years, that the record which mattered to people was not the record of what was important but the record of what was theirs, the long accumulation of unimportant true days that added up, in the end, to a life a person could stand on. The machine had kept the important things with a terrible exactness and had let the days rot, and a people with no days left could be told it had any past at all. These people were keeping their days. It was slow, and it was dull, and Tanaka had come to believe it was the only defence there had ever been, and that it had been available the whole time, and that it had cost a machine's death and a war's dead to make a species dull enough to use it.

Old Songs was not here. He rarely came into the capital now; the road from the farmhouse tired him, and the cold got into his hands, and a man officially dead for forty years did not need to stand where the cameras that no longer reported to anyone could still see him. Tanaka noted his absence the way she had once noted a missing instrument on a tray: not alarm, only the registering of a thing that ought to be there and was not. She thought of him as he stood when he did come, at the very back, at the edge, not singing, letting the song be in other mouths; officially dead, and there anyway; the only person she knew whose relationship to the record was stranger than her own.

At fourteen hundred and twelve she looked at the colonnade on the west side.

She did not decide to look at it. Her eyes went to it the way they had gone to it on the fifteenth of November, 2107, at twelve minutes past the hour, when she had seen a woman standing by the third pillar from the north end and had written, in a notebook three notebooks ago, *Witnesses: none*, and had then walked the length of the plaza to find Kira, so that it would not be none; and by the time she had found her the woman was gone, and had stayed gone for eight years, filed under the one heading Tanaka kept for things she could not close.

There was a woman by the third pillar from the north end.

About fifty-eight. A gray coat. Reading glasses on a cord against her chest. She stood with her hands in her coat pockets, very still, and she was not watching the stone, and she was not watching the readings, and she was not one of the dozen quiet people who came every month to stand apart and grieve their own and go. She was watching Tanaka.

Tanaka thought of Old Songs again, at the back of the crowd, there and not there. Here was another of them. Here was another person standing at the edge of a gathering that had written her down as dead, come to see whether anyone in the living world still knew her face.

She opened the twelfth notebook.

*14:12. Colonnade, west side. Female, about fifty-eight. Gray coat. Reading glasses on a cord.*

She wrote *Witnesses:* and stopped.

She had left lines unfinished before. There were case notes in all twelve notebooks that ended at a colon and a blank, the lines she could not bear to complete, where the completing would have put a thing into the record that she was not strong enough to make permanent. This was not that. She left this line blank for the opposite reason: because she did not intend, this time, for there to be no witness. She intended to be one. She capped the pen.

No tram came between them this time. In 2107 a tram had come, the long articulated tram that ran the harbour loop, and by the time it had passed the colonnade was empty. There was no tram now, only the cold plaza and the sound of a man at the stone reading the weather of a Tuesday, and Tanaka crossed the open ground at an ordinary walking pace, neither slow nor fast, because a person who hurried was a person who had decided something, and she wished to arrive having decided nothing she could not revise.

She counted her steps without meaning to. It was an old habit and it ran under everything, the checklist voice she had used ten thousand times at the start of a procedure. *Step one: confirm patient identity.* She almost laughed, crossing the plaza, at where the old voice had chosen to surface. She noted what she could see as the distance closed, because noting was what she had instead of nerve. The coat was good — a good coat, old, the wool still dense, mended at one cuff with thread a careful half-shade off. The sleeves had been pushed up at some point and pulled down again; there was the memory of a fold in them. The face was thinner than the Ministry's official portrait, the one that had hung in the corridor outside the inquiry in 2106 and had been printed in nine newspapers under the word *MURDERED*. The set of the mouth was the same. Tanaka did not let herself say the name, even inside her own head, because to say it even silently was to write it, and she was not ready to write it yet.

She reviewed, as she walked, what she had filed nine years ago. She gave it one paragraph and no more, because more than one paragraph was fear and she did not permit herself fear on the job. A death certified in a sealed ward. A cremation within a day, unusually fast, requested by a family that no subsequent inquiry had been able to find. No photograph of the face taken after death; the file held a portrait and nothing else. Identification made by staff who had every one of them been inside the seeded district that afternoon, so that every one of them had spent that hour remembering a thing arranged for them to remember. She had written the sentence herself, in 2112, in the eleventh chapter of a book she had written with Isaiah, and she had chosen the words with care and had meant them to lie flat on the page and be stepped over: *Nobody who cannot be seeded has seen her body.*

She stopped a pace and a half from the third pillar.

The woman in the gray coat looked at her for a moment longer, with an expression Tanaka knew, because she had seen it on the faces of patients who had decided to tell her the true thing at last and were taking the breath before it: not fear, and not relief, but the particular stillness of a person setting down something they have carried alone for a very long time.

Then she turned to face Tanaka fully, and took the reading glasses off, and let them drop on their cord against the gray coat, and said:

"Doctor. I think you've been keeping my file."
