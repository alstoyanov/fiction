# Chapter 30: The Lakeshore Air

*Friday, November 15, 2115. The capital*

---

*Nov 15, 2115. Home. Involved parties: Chen, M. (38); Osman, K.; Chen, T. (2 years, 7 months). Status: the day.*

He had been thirty-eight since earlier in the month. Tamsin, who was two years and seven months, corrected nothing that morning and wanted her shoes, and told him that the kitchen tap was the river, which it was, and he found both shoes and did not argue about the river, because arguing about the river was the one thing he had spent nine years and a Commission learning not to do.

It was nine years to the day since the hour was taken.

---

At nine he was at the Historical Archive, with Kovač, and Kira, and Tamsin on his hip, and Old Songs, and an archivist who stood a little apart and let them do it. Kovač had brought everything out of her custody, and she laid it into a plain archive box on the long table, and for the first time since March of 2066 the Compiler's copy was whole.

She did it in order, and nobody hurried her. Page one, with its torn strip laid back against the tear, the fibres meeting. Pages two through four, the Introduction, in the Compiler's own voice. Pages five through thirty-nine, the six signed principles and the Annex and the Register. And then the two handwritten pages that the typed run did not hold: page forty, the Warning, at the back; and Principle Seven, which Kira took out of her journal and put in herself, after page thirty-two, in the place where the rust ghost of a clip had waited five years, front and back, the machine's own death in the young slanting hand. Kerr's page, *On Keeping,* with its struck-through mark, Kovač clipped to the front, where Vance had left it in 1966, where a question had found it in April. Copy X, with its intake pages, did not go into the box; copy X stayed with the court, evidence in the trial of the man who had carried it up a flight of stairs, and would stay there.

Old Songs wrote the catalogue card. He had asked to, and Kovač had offered once to write it for him and had not offered twice, because she understood. His hands were very bad now, and it took him a long time, the pen going where it liked and not where he sent it, and nobody in the room said anything or moved to help, and he formed the letters one at a time, slowly, in a fair hand learned eighty years ago, the same card he had written at twenty-five for the same shelf:

*Periodicals, Governance, 114.*

The box went onto the shelf, in the run, between 113 and 115, so that the numbers ran 113, 114, 115, closed up no longer, the gap filled, the number that had existed for fifty years in only one steel drawer now existing on the open shelf where its first keeper had put it. Anyone could request it. Hand copies of every page of it were already in a thousand homes in six nations and pinned to a noticeboard in a provincial town in the east. The original going back onto the shelf changed nothing and meant everything, which was, Marcus thought, the whole of what an archive was for.

He stood in the periodicals room and watched an old man's bad hand form three numerals on a card, and he thought about the distance the thing had travelled to get back to a shelf it had been swept from in 1966. Fifty years in a steel drawer and behind an unanswered letter. A riddle set to a border tune and carried, uncopied, in a dead man's head for five years. A basement floor in April. A farmhouse table where it was made whole for the first time since it was written. Two copies cross-checked line by line in a flat at two in the morning, a confession from the son of one of its authors, the names read aloud at a stone by strangers drawn by lot. And now, this morning, quietly, with no ceremony and almost no witnesses, back onto the shelf its first keeper had given it, under the number its first keeper had written, in the first keeper's own failing hand. The design had erased it so cleanly there had been no hole. Mikhail had spent his life insisting on the hole, smelling an apple no one peeled, keeping a number alive in one old skull against a nation that had decided it never existed. And the number was on the open shelf again, 113, 114, 115, and any citizen could ask for the box, and that — not the merged record, not the Meta-Judge, not any machine's clean totality — was what it looked like when the truth won: a gap closed on a shelf, by the hand of the man they had tried to empty, fifty years late and exactly on time. Old Songs stood looking at the three numbers in a row for a moment, and then he hummed one phrase of the ferry verse, under his breath, and it lifted at the end, and he turned away.

---

The week sat behind the day, and Marcus carried it lightly. Wei had been sentenced the week before by a human court, twelve years; he had pleaded to everything and said nothing else, had asked for no counsel and offered no account, and the court had done the human thing, which was to weigh it and decide, with no number above which a man became a problem, only people deciding, slowly, what was just, and arriving at twelve years. Containment held. More reports had come back along Elena's routes, each copy found in a hand that was not theirs, and Marcus had filed each one under CHECKED, which was the heading he had the least practice with and was learning to use.

---

At two o'clock they were in the plaza.

It was the Day of Testimony, nine years on, and the stone stood by the fountain with the Lost Hour names cut into its top and the war's names down its side, and Marcus found Alexei Volkov and Nora Chen among them without looking, the way you find the bottom step in the dark. The crowd was some thousands. Tamsin was on his shoulders with her hands in his hair, and Kira was beside him, and the circle was spread through the crowd the way you spread people you love through a crowd so that no one place holds all of them: Dmitri at the old man's elbow, where he stood now at every public thing; Nikolai and Maya with their small son between them; Elena at the edge, counting heads left to right out of a habit she would die with; Isaiah very tall at the back, where he could see the whole of it; Tanaka near the fountain with her notebook. Singh stood at the colonnade on the west side, in her gray coat, her reading glasses on their cord, a filed woman at the edge of a crowd that had written her down as dead and knew better now. Sokolov stood at the far edge in a long dark coat with her hands in her pockets, in from the coast for the day. Julian and Adrian had come across the lake from Chronos.

Marcus looked at them spread through the crowd, the people he had come up out of a glass cell with and the people he had gathered since, and he let himself, for once, feel the whole of it, the way you let yourself feel a thing only when it is nearly over and safe to feel. Ten years. A believer convicted at seventy-three per cent, who had believed with his whole heart and had it taken out of him a curve at a time. An architect of the Spire who had watched her own fact vanish. Three brothers who could not be broken, and were two now. A doctor who had refused the trials and kept twelve notebooks. A journalist who had learned to listen. A singer who had carried a dead age's memory in tunes through cell doors. They had come out of Ashford officially dead and doubtful even of their own love, and they had spent ten years and a brother and a mother and a great many strangers' lives learning the one thing the design had been built to make impossible, which was how to keep the truth without a machine — by many hands, slowly, in the open, checkable and owned by no one. The stone in front of him was the proof of it, and the crowd around it, and the names in its side, and the frail old man about to sing. It was not the end of the design; the design was in the east, tended, patient, half the world's record under its hand by open treaty. It was only this: that here, on this shore, a people kept its own days by hand and came to a stone on the fifteenth to say them, and that the keeping had survived everything thrown at it, and would go on after all of them, which was the most that had ever been on offer and had turned out to be enough.

Old Songs stood at the stone, seventy-seven, officially dead for forty years, very frail, and he did not speak, because his one speech had been in August and he had none left to give. He sang the Lakeshore Air, complete, once, the old verses and the hidden ones together, in the thin true voice that still, even now, lifted at the end of every line.

He began with the ferry verses, the traditional words, the ones the grandmothers of the lakeshore had sung before any of it:

*Down by the water the ferry is late,*
*lanterns are low on the pier;*
*who will remember the name of the boat*
*when there is no one left here?*

*Down by the water the fog's coming in,*
*nobody sees the far shore;*
*who will remember the boat and the name*
*when there's no pier any more?*

Then the lullaby, *Hush Now, the River Is Long,* all three verses, sung to the same tune, the lullaby that mothers of the Great Lakes had sung who were not supposed to know it:

*Hush now, the river is long,*
*longer than night and longer than song;*
*sleep till the morning comes round the bend:*
*the river is long, but it comes to an end.*

*Hush now, the boatman is kind,*
*he carries the ones that we leave behind;*
*nothing is lost that the water has kept:*
*the river remembers the ones who have slept.*

At the second verse Marcus found that he was singing it under his breath, as he had sung it in Wing C when it was the only thing left that was his, *hush now, the boatman is kind,* and he still could not remember his mother's name, the clean socket where the name had been was still clean; but he had the verse, and he had stopped, somewhere in the last nine years, needing to know whether the verse was memory or invention, because Tamsin on his shoulders was singing the wrong word to it loudly and happily, and whatever the verse was, it was being sung by the next one now, which was the only verification a thing like that could ever have.

*Hush now, and if you should wake,*
*count all the stars for the river's sake:*
*one for the lost and one for the found,*
*and one for the river that carries us round.*

Then *Given Gently,* and the crowd knew by now whose words these were, knew they came from a young man's essay of 2063 that had grown into the sixth principle, knew the mark was CZ·11; and in the open air, sung plainly to the gentlest tune in the border zone, they were no longer a secret buried in a lullaby but exactly what their maker had set them to be, which was a warning:

*A people that has lost part of its past*
*will accept any past it is given —*
*provided it is given gently,*
*and by someone it trusts.*

And then the last verse, the one he had written for the day, new, in the old shape, lifting at the end like all the rest:

*Down by the water the ferry comes in,*
*lanterns are lit on the pier;*
*everyone knows the name of the boat —*
*who will remember we're here?*

---

The last phrase lifted, like a question, the way every phrase of that tune had lifted for sixty years, a question put and left open over a lake and a plaza and a stone.

The crowd did not answer it. They had learned not to; they had learned, over nine years, that the question was not a question to be answered but a question to be kept; and so they did the thing they had learned instead, which was to sing it back — thousands of voices taking up the last verse, raggedly at first and then together, *down by the water the ferry comes in,* carrying it, each phrase lifting and the next voice starting before the lift had finished, so that the question never closed and never needed to. Tamsin sang a word of it wrong, loudly, into the back of Marcus's neck. At the stone, Old Songs heard the thousands take it, and heard, somewhere in it, a child singing it wrong, and he smiled, and let them carry it, and did not sing any more, because it had many singers now, which was the thing he had wanted since he first hummed a tune through a slot in a cell door, that the song should outlive the singer because the singer was only ever one hand.

---

At night, at the kitchen table, with Tamsin asleep and Kira moving quietly in the other room, Marcus took out the lists. CHECKED, UNCHECKED, HOPES. *QUESTIONS FOR THE WAR,* with *Where is east?* crossed through on the tenth of June. And the page from the twenty-second of July, still blank, blank on purpose now.

He turned to a clean page, and he wrote the last entry, in fact-entry form, the form he had kept for nine years, the form a dead machine had taught him and a living life had let him keep:

*Nov 15, 2115. Central plaza. Involved parties: everyone present. Event: the song. Verification: we were all there.*

He read it once. It needed no machine to verify it, and no Judge to settle it, and no source to restore it from; it was verified by the only thing that had ever really verified anything, which was that a great many people had been there and had sung it and would remember, in a thousand slightly different ways, that they had; and he closed the book, and went to find Kira, and did not take it out again that night.
