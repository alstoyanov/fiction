Chapter 19: The Orchard

Monday, July 1, 2115. Elena's farmhouse, the border zone

Elena Volkov counted first and felt second, and she had organised her whole life around the order, because the one time she had let it run the other way a man had died, and she did not intend to learn that lesson twice.

She was fifty-one. She had been out of hiding for five years, since the Judge ended — there was no machine left to hold her record against her, no factorepo to rule her an outlaw, and the enforcers who had spent a decade wanting a woman named Vasquez had nothing left to want her with, no court, no number, no doctrine; she had simply walked back into being a person, in the spring of 2110, and had found it less of a homecoming than she expected, because the farmhouse had become her home while she was no one and remained her home now that she was someone again. She still carried the notebook she never wrote in. It lived in her breast pocket, blank, and she touched it the way other people touched a charm.

On the kitchen table was a map of the SFW–CNT border and the Black Sea coast, weighted at the corners with a cup and a stone and two spoons. She had written on a slip beside it, in the hand she used only for logistics: Routes: four. Carriers: nine. Tested: two.

Four routes. She had them in her head the way she had always had the thing that mattered, where no file could hold it. Across the mountain border from the SFW, on the old smugglers' paths, with President Martinez's quiet leave, which Isaiah had got her on the twenty-fourth. By fishing boat along the Black Sea coast, which was slow and weather-bound and nearly invisible. Through the unaligned territory in the south, the long way, the way her first courier had come and gone in nine days and a pair of ruined boots. And one route she would not name even inside her own head, because a route you had not said aloud was a route no one could take from you, and she had learned that from a man who hummed.

The CNT's people had no interfaces. That was the whole of why paper mattered and the whole of why it was hard. Nothing would seed a CNT citizen; no broadcast would lay a false afternoon over his memory; but nothing would hand him a true page, either, or skim one into his mind in the night. He could only read what he was physically given, if he was allowed to keep it long enough to read it. In the CNT, history was what the Council said it was, taught from printed primers to children with no devices behind their ears, and so a page of the truth was contraband there in a way it was nowhere else in the world — and it was also, there and only there, the one thing that could not be un-said, because a thing seeded could be reseeded and a thing broadcast could be rebroadcast, but a page a man had read with his own eyes and hidden under his own floor was his, now, permanently, in the one storage the Council could not reach, which was a human being who had decided to remember.

She had spent her whole adult life moving contraband across borders — people, mostly, in the old days, outlaws and doubters and the families of the corrected, hidden in trucks and boats and the backs of ordinary lives — and she found a bleak symmetry in ending it moving paper. It was the same work. It had always been the same work: getting a true thing past the people who had decided what was permitted to exist, into the keeping of someone who would risk themselves to hold it. The cargo had only changed because the war had changed. Once the dangerous thing to smuggle had been a person who doubted the record; now the dangerous thing was the record itself, true, on paper, into a country that had never been allowed to read its own. She counted the routes and the carriers and did not let herself think, yet, about what it would cost the ones who carried, because thinking about the cost was feeling, and feeling came second, after the counting, always, or the counting did not get done, and then people died for a failure of arithmetic, which was the one death she could not forgive herself, having caused it once.

She gave Kira her answer by courier, flat, and did not soften it. The routes would not bear the full run by the fifteenth. On the fifteenth of July the first carriers would take forty sets across, and no more — forty, hand-copied already into the CNT's own languages by émigré volunteers along the borders and checked by a second pair of hands, the pairing method in another alphabet. Forty sets walked in by nine carriers on four routes, and then, she told Kira, expect no word for months, because paper walked, and walking was slow, and a carrier who came back fast was a carrier who had been caught and turned. Paper walks, she wrote. If you hear nothing, that is the plan working, not the plan failing. I will tell you when there is something to tell.

Her border contacts said the CNT side had been quieter than in years since the spring. No incidents. No carriers taken. The patrols ran to timetable, which was the thing she liked least of all; she had crossed a great many borders in her life and she had never once met a quiet border that meant nothing. A quiet border meant either that nobody was watching or that somebody was watching and had decided not to act, and the first was rare and the second was a trap, and she did not believe she had stumbled onto the rare thing. She filed it under the same heading everyone in the circle was filing things under that summer, the heading that had no name, the one that held they haven't moved.

Old Songs was at the stove, the damper open two fingers so the crack glowed and drew, humming, and when he lifted the kettle he lifted it with both hands, because one would not do it alone any more. He was seventy-seven. The farmhouse had been his home for ten years, since he had come down out of the correction service a dead man and been taken in. He did not ask her about the routes. He never asked her about the routes. He hummed, and the tune lifted at the end, and she did her counting to it the way you do a thing to a sound you have stopped hearing as a sound.

Toward evening she went out to the orchard, and he came with her, slowly, and she let him set the pace.

The plums were setting, small and hard and green along the branches that had been white a month ago. The two graves were under the trees between the rows. Alexei, buried in April of 2110. And Elise Vance beside him, buried on the twenty-seventh of May that year, with a hand's breadth of turf left between the two graves, grown over now, an ordinary strip of grass that Elena knew the measurement of and had never asked anyone about. Old Songs walked to the foot of Alexei's grave and stood. Elena stood at the side of it, very straight, her hands at her sides, the way she had stood at the stove in a hospital corridor in 2106 when her sons' mother was dying and there had been nothing to count and nothing to do.

She told him, because he had not asked, which was the only condition under which she could have told anyone.

"Isaiah gave me the first half of it in the yard," she said. "Two days after. The twenty-fifth of April. He stood where the barn door is and he said Alexei's message. Tell Elena I love her." Her voice did not change; it was the briefing voice, flat, in order, because the briefing voice was the only one that would carry the words without breaking in her throat. "I didn't come in for the song that night. He sang Alexei at the stone and I was in the barn with the door shut."

Old Songs said nothing. He did not look at her. He looked at the grave.

"The last thing I said to him was on the radio," Elena said. "Before he went out for Isaiah, the twenty-third. He asked me how many. I said, Two of mine go with you. I want three back. That was the whole of it. A commander's line. Count out, count in, bring me my number." She stopped. "I got three back. The woman, and the boy, and Isaiah. I got exactly what I asked for. I am a very good quartermaster. I asked for three and I got three and the three I got were not the three I sent, and the one I sent I did not ask to come back, because I was counting, and you count the living, you don't count the one carrying the others, you count what you expect to recover —" She stopped again, and this time it held. "I never said it back. I have never said the direct thing to a living person in my life. I said it to his sheet, in the barn, and he was already under it. So it doesn't count. The one time it would have counted, I was counting something else."

Old Songs listened to all of it, standing at the foot of the grave with his bad hands folded in front of him, and he did not touch her, because she was not a woman you touched, and he knew it, and the not-touching was the kindest thing he could have done and he did it.

She thought, once, of the tip that had herded her people toward the lost hour in 2106, and of the credentials behind the tip, now returned from the dead and sitting at this very table a month ago with her sleeves rolled up; and she let the thought pass through her and out, because that was a reckoning for another day, and she knew it would come, and she had learned to let a thing she could not count tonight wait until it could be counted.

Then Old Songs began to hum. "The Names," low, the tune she had heard him sing over this grave five years ago and over the stone in the capital and in his sleep through the farmhouse wall, the phrases lifting at the end like questions put and left open. He hummed the first verse through without words. And at the third line, where the name went, he stopped, and held the hum, and left the space open, and did not fill it, and she understood that he was not going to fill it, that he had brought her out here to fill it herself.

She filled it. Hoarse, not a singer, her voice cracking on the long vowel, into the space he had left:

Who went down to the water? Name them, and they stay. Alexei went down to the water — name them, and they stay.

Old Songs hummed the refrain under her, the two lines he always gave the dead, the record may forget them, the song will not forget; and the last phrase lifted at the end, the way all his phrases lifted, like a question, and hung in the evening over the two graves and the strip of grass between them; and neither of them finished it, because it was not a thing that finished, it was a thing you left open on purpose, and the plums set green and hard above them in the last of the light.
