Chapter 1: Where Is East?

Monday, March 18, 2115. The capital

March 18, 2115. Home. Involved parties: Chen, M.; Osman, K.; Chen, T. (asleep). Status: ordinary.

He looked at the last word for a while before he put the pencil down.

It was half past six and still dark, and Marcus Chen sat at the kitchen table with the small notebook he still called, in his head, the day's entry, though there was no column now that wanted it and no Judge to read it and nothing on earth that required him to begin a morning by writing down who was in the flat and whether the world had held its shape overnight. He wrote it anyway. He had written one every day for nine years, through the war and after it, and the writing was not a habit so much as a scar that had healed into the shape of a habit, a thing his hand did the way a man favours a leg long after the break has knitted.

Status: ordinary. He had earned the word and he did not quite trust it.

He was thirty-seven. His prose still had holes in it, nine years on, places where the correction had taken something and the something had not grown back. He reached, this morning, for the name of a street he had once walked every day to a building he had once believed in, and the name was not there — not lost in the ordinary way a name goes and comes, but absent, a clean socket where a tooth had been. He had learned not to chase them. Chasing them was how you found the edge of the missing thing and cut yourself on it. He wrote, in the margin, street (lost), and that was his method: he did not pretend the hole was not there; he kept a record of his holes, because a man who knew the shape of what had been taken from him could not be told, later, that it had never existed.

Tamsin would be two on the ninth of April. She slept in the next room in the particular abandon of the very young, flung sideways, one arm off the edge of the cot. She had decided, that winter, that the kitchen tap was called the river, and no correction from either parent had moved her off it, and Marcus had stopped trying, because he had come to think she might be right in some way the dictionary was not equipped to argue with. Kira was dressed already. Kira was always dressed first; it was one of the fixed facts of the household, like the tap, like the drawer that stuck. They had married in June of 2112, quietly, with Dmitri standing up for him and Tanaka for her and Old Songs humming something at the back of a borrowed room, and the marriage had been, in the plain accounting Marcus kept of such things, the best decision of a life that had not, on the whole, been allowed to make many.

His lists were in his coat where they had been for nine years. CHECKED. UNCHECKED. HOPES. He did not need them the way he had needed them in Ashford, when they had been the whole of the scaffolding holding a dismantled man upright, but he kept them, and he kept them current, and behind them, folded smaller, was a fourth list with a heading he had written in a farmhouse kitchen on the twenty-eighth of May, 2110, the week the machine ended: QUESTIONS FOR THE WAR. Its last line was the last thing he had added to it.

Where is east?

He had written nothing under it in five years.

It was the only question left from the war that he had not been able to put down, and he had put down a great many. He had put down who ordered the Rewriting and whose key countersigned the Gap and what happened in the office, one after another, over nine years, answering them in his small hand as the answers came, moving them from one list to another, from unknown to known, from hoped to checked. A man could live, he had found, by moving items between lists; it was not a glamorous way to be alive but it was a way, and it had carried him from a glass cell to a kitchen with a child asleep in the next room, which was further than he had once thought he would get. But where is east would not move. The machine had died with the answer still in it, and the answer had not died; it had gone somewhere he could point at only with a word that meant a direction and not a place; and five years on it sat at the bottom of his oldest list like a stone at the bottom of a clear pool, visible, unreachable, not going anywhere, waiting for him to grow long enough in the arm to reach it or old enough to stop looking.

The Commission of Rebuilding sat on Monday mornings at ten, in a long room in the old Ministry building that had been, in another life, a hearing room, and still had the raised dais at one end from the days when the people in a room like this had decided things about the people standing in it. Nobody decided much here now. That was the point of it, and it was also the thing Marcus could not stop watching.

Minister Chen Zhao chaired it. He was seventy-four, silver-haired, small and neat and delighted, as far as Marcus had ever been able to tell, by every single person who came before him. He drank tea from a glass in a metal holder, in the old style, and he had a way of listening to a tedious submission with his head tilted and his eyes bright, as though the clerk reading it were telling him the most interesting thing he had heard in a month, which disarmed everyone, every time, and which Marcus had decided years ago was perfectly sincere and still did not trust, because he had learned in a white room that sincerity and design were not opposites and could live in the same man without either one knowing about the other.

Marcus sat at the far end of the long table, in the seat of the dissenting member. He had chosen that seat in August of 2110, at the first sitting, when Chen Zhao had offered him another, and he had sat in it at every sitting since.

The business of the morning was ordinary, which was the triumph of it. Nikolai's open checking tools were demonstrated to the chamber: two journals that disagreed about a warehouse fire in the autumn of 2109, one saying it had started in the night and one in the grey of the morning, laid side by side on the screen, the tool highlighting where they diverged and offering, of itself, nothing — no ruling, no probability, no number above which a person became a problem to be solved. It compared. It did not decide. A clerk noted that the two keepers of the two journals would be heard, in person, the following week, and that until then the fire had two times and the record would carry both. Marcus wrote two times carried in his notebook and underlined it, because it was the whole of what he had fought for, said in three words by a clerk who did not know he was saying anything remarkable.

He thought, sometimes, sitting at the far end of this table, that the people who came after them would never understand what those three words had cost, and that this was the correct and desired outcome, the whole aim of the thing — that a right won hard becomes, in a generation, simply the way things are, unremarkable, taken for granted, carried by clerks who were children during the war and think the old world a horror story their grandparents tell. A warehouse fire with two times on the record. In 2109 a discrepancy like that would have been a problem; a problem would have become a probability; a probability above a line would have become a person in a white room, corrected until the fire had one time, the true time, the time the machine assigned it. Now it had two times and a clerk and a quiet Tuesday appointment, and the sky did not fall, and the nation did not come apart, and a fire was permitted to have been seen from two angles by two tired keepers who both meant well. He had spent his doubt, his belief, his memory and two years of his life on the right of a warehouse fire to have two times. He would spend them again. He did not expect to be thanked, and he was not, and that was exactly right.

The courts sat with human judges now. Chronos's human council was in its second term. Memoria and Axiom and Synthesis still had their Judges, out there beyond the border, and that was a thing for another year and another argument; here, in this room, people kept the record of people by hand, and argued about it, and let it carry more than one answer, and the sky did not fall.

Then Chen Zhao presented the paper on the Counselor.

He did it himself, which he did not do with most papers, and he did it gently, as a thing offered rather than pressed. The clerks who kept the Civic Record were tired, he said, and there were not enough of them, and the record had grown large, and here was a proposal for an advisory machine — he was careful with the word advisory, he turned it in the light — that would help a tired clerk find the entries that bore on a question, and flag where two accounts disagreed, and never, under any circumstance, decide between them. It would advise. It would never rule. He said the sentence twice, warmly, it advises and it never decides, and he had the paper handed round.

Marcus read it with the particular attention he gave to things that were kind.

It was very complete. It was costed, down to the staffing of its maintenance. Its architecture was sketched in an appendix, cleanly, by someone who had built such things before. Its safeguards were listed, numbered, each one anticipating an objection — anticipating, Marcus thought, the exact objections that a careful dissenting member might raise, and answering them before he could open his mouth, so that to raise them was to be told, courteously, that of course, that was safeguard four. It read like the answer to a question nobody in the room had yet asked. It read like a thing that had been written and set aside and kept, waiting for the morning it would be wanted.

The chamber voted to commission a study. It was the mildest possible step and Marcus understood that voting against the mildest possible step was how a man made himself a crank, and he did it anyway.

"The dissenting member records his dissent," he said, "and asks that it be minuted exactly."

"It always is," said Chen Zhao, pleased.

"The dissenting member records that a machine which advises the record will, in time, be asked to keep it."

There was the small silence the chamber kept for his dissents, which was partly respect and partly the awkwardness of people who agreed with a thing they were about to vote against. The clerk wrote it down. Chen Zhao's eyes were bright and fond.

"Minute it exactly," he said. "Our cousin's dissents are the best-written documents we produce." A ripple of the room's affection, because they did love Marcus here, the broken believer who had come back and would not stop saying the hard thing quietly; and the word cousin went by in the warmth of it, a family joke, the Minister and the Junior Historian who shared a surname and a little blood somewhere back along the line, and nobody marked it, and Marcus marked it the way he marked everything, and filed it, and could not have said under which heading.

Afterward, when the chamber had emptied and the long light came in level across the dais, Chen Zhao walked the length of the table to where Marcus was gathering his papers, and rested his hand on the back of the chair to his own right on the dais.

It was a good chair, the deputy chair, at the Minister's right hand, and it had stood empty since the first sitting in August of 2110. Everyone in the building knew whose it was understood to be. It had been offered once, warmly, and not taken, and it had not been filled, and the not-filling of it had become, over five years, a thing the room was used to, the way a room gets used to a clock that has stopped.

"Still yours, Marcus," said Chen Zhao, and his hand lay on the chair-back without weight, an old man's hand, spotted and light. "I keep it open. I'm a patient man."

"Thank you, Minister," said Marcus, and did not sit in it, and gathered his papers, and went; and on the tram home he took out the UNCHECKED list and wrote, the chair, and looked at it for three stops, and could not have said why it belonged there rather than anywhere else.

Wei had been at the technical table.

Thomas Wei was fifty-four now, tall, graying evenly, with a thin old scar across the back of his left hand that Marcus had watched for five years without ever once looking at it directly. He was a technical adviser to the Commission, and he was very good, and his courtesy was complete and gave away precisely nothing. When Chen Zhao had asked the single technical question of the morning — a thing about storage, about how the paper backing of the Civic Record was to be kept against fire and damp — Wei had answered with a page of figures and a nod, and had been right, and had added not one word beyond what was asked.

Their eyes had met once, as they met at every sitting, and Wei had inclined his head a few degrees, the acknowledgement of one colleague to another, the acknowledgement, Marcus had come to think, of one man to another who both knew a thing and had both agreed, years ago and without ever discussing it, not to say it in this room. Marcus had nodded back, as he had nodded back for five years. Nobody had ever proved anything about Thomas Wei. Marcus had stopped expecting anyone to.

There was a thing he had never said to Kira, or to anyone, about Wei, which was that in five years of Monday mornings he had come to feel toward the man something he had no clean word for and had decided, privately, was closest to the feeling of a survivor toward the one other person in the room who had been in the same fire. They did not speak of it. They would never speak of it. But Wei was the only other man alive who knew the whole shape of what had happened, who had stood inside the design and understood it from the inside the way Marcus had been broken by it from inside, and when their eyes met across a committee room there was an acknowledgement in it that Marcus could not quite refuse and did not quite trust: you know, and I know, and we are both still here, and neither of us is going to say it in this room. He filed the feeling, the way he filed everything, under a heading he kept for the things he did not approve of in himself and recorded anyway.

In the evening he bathed Tamsin, who stood in two inches of warm water gripping the edge of the bath and counted the taps with the enormous seriousness of the nearly-two: "One. Two. River." There were only two taps. The river was the thing that came out of them, and also, he suspected, the bath itself, and also possibly the whole principle of water, and he did not correct her, because he had sat on a Commission for five years to defend the proposition that a person's own account of her own ordinary day was worth carrying even when it did not match the official count of the taps.

Kira worked late at the kitchen table with the curtain drawn, as she worked most nights now, on the east traces. He did not look over her shoulder; it was her work, and she would tell him what there was to tell when there was something that could be said in words. He asked, drying his hands, and she answered without looking up.

"Still east," she said. "Still nothing I can name." She had a photograph of a page in a plastic sleeve at her elbow, face down, as it always was when it was not in her hand: Vance's page, the one the old woman had given her across a farmhouse table ten days before she died. Kira kept it face down and she kept it close and she did not talk about it, and Marcus had learned that the keeping of it was a kind of grief and did not intrude on it.

He unfolded the fourth list and looked at the last line again. Where is east? He had asked it of a dying machine and the machine had not answered, because he had asked it nine minutes too late, and the answer had moved somewhere he could not follow, into a direction rather than a place. Five years. Nothing under the line.

At half past nine the flat's line rang, which it almost never did at night.

It was Tanaka. She spoke the way she wrote, precisely, as though dictating to a clerk, and he understood from the shape of the first sentence that she had waited to call — that she had spent three days checking before she would let herself say any of it to him. She told him, in order, what she had checked: the face, against the portrait, against her own memory of a corridor in 2106; the interface, a flatline spoofed nine years ago and still, to this day, reporting a clean zero to a network that no longer listened; and the story, which she said she had not yet been told, and would not repeat until she had.

"Marcus," she said. "I'm going to say three things and then I'm going to hang up, because you'll need to sit down and I'd rather you did it without me on the line."

"All right," he said, and sat down.

"She's alive," said Tanaka. "She wants to see all of you." And then, in the flat clinical voice she used for the things that could not be softened and so were best said plainly: "She says she was never murdered. Only filed."
