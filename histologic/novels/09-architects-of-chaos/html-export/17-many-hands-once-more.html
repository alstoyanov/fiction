Chapter 17: Many Hands, Once More

Monday, June 17, 2115. The capital

Jun 17, 2115. Kitchen. Present: Chen, M.; Volkov, N.; Reeves, M.; Volkov, D.; Okonkwo, I.; Volkov, E.; Kovač, H. Tamsin: asleep, door ajar.

Kira wrote the entry and set the pen down and looked at the table, which was the same table where Wei had laid down copy X three weeks ago, where she had cross-checked two copies of the manuscript line by line until two in the morning while Marcus wrote not yet said in his list. Tonight the table had eight sheets on it and her photographs squared beside them, and seven people around it, and the question that had been coming for five books, which was not what is true — they had that now, they had all of it — but how do you let a whole world have a true thing without handing someone a lever to take it back.

She was thirty-six. Vance's page was in her desk drawer where it had lived since 2110, and she did not take it out tonight; she did not need it; she had it in her, both sides, and the side that mattered for tonight was the back, the warning that everything that restored from a source ended with the source, which was the exact shape of the trap she was about to spend an evening making sure they did not walk into.

The set was eight sheets. The Annex, pages thirty-three through thirty-eight. The Register, page thirty-nine. And Kerr's page, On Keeping, which belonged with them because it was the answer the design itself had stolen. She had laid out her photographs of the Compiler's copy beside her photographs of copy X, and they agreed, page for page, chipped e for chipped e, and Wei's sentence came back to her unbidden as she looked at the two sets of photographs side by side: two copies are a pattern. Both originals were in Kovač's custody. Nothing on this table was an original. That was on purpose.

Nikolai proposed the obvious thing, because the obvious thing deserved to be said out loud and then answered, not just skipped. "We could put it on the open tools tonight," he said. "All eight sheets, every nation, every relay, before they can draw a breath. It'd be everywhere by morning."

"And if it goes out in one place, it can be taken back in one place," Kira said, levelly. "Principle Three restored three backups from one source. The Concordance restored every registered journal in the country from a master, overnight, and called it a gift. Anything we put up as a file has somewhere it lives, a first copy, a master, and a master is a throat. I'm not giving the design a throat to cut." She put her hand flat on the stack of photographs. "The photographs go up on the open tools too. After. After the paper is out, so that anyone who has a paper copy can check it against the image and the image against their neighbour's paper. The tools compare. They don't decide, and they don't originate. Paper first. Then the picture, so the paper can check itself."

The method was Book 6's pairing method, grown up, and she walked it in order because order was how you kept a thousand strangers from breeding a thousand errors.

Every set would be copied by hand, from the photographs, not typed, not printed except for Isaiah's facsimile run, copied, letter by letter. The copyist would sign and date the last sheet. A second hand would countersign it — but only after reading the whole set against the photographs, line by line, so that the countersignature meant I checked this myself, not I trust you. And no set would ever be copied from another set. Only from the photographs. Because if you let copies be made from copies, a slip on one sheet became the ancestor of ten thousand slips, and in a year the design could point at the drift and say see how testimony rots, see why you need one record, mechanically verified — and she was not going to hand them that either. Every hand went back to the same image. A mistake could not breed if nothing was descended from it.

She had thought about the method more than she had thought about almost anything in five years, because the method was the whole war, now, in miniature. The design's answer to the chaos of the Fracture Years had been one record, singular, verified, enforced — a single clean true thing that could not be argued with, held in one place, by one authority, for everyone. And the terrible genius of it was that it was not wrong about the problem; the Fracture Years had been real; a species that could not agree on what had happened really had slaughtered itself with its own competing truths. The design had simply chosen the one answer that handed the whole thing to whoever held the source. And the circle's answer — the Warning's answer, Kerr's answer, carried in a dead man's song for fifty years — was the only other answer there was: not one record but many, not clean but checkable, not enforced but compared, every copy traceable to a shared original and every original open to any eye that wanted to check it against its neighbour's. It was slower. It was uglier. It did not feel like safety, the way the one record felt like safety. But it could not be cut at the throat, because it had no throat, and Kira had decided a long time ago that a truth with no throat was worth any amount of slowness, and that she would spend the rest of her life defending the right of the record to be slow.

They went round the table, a route each.

Veridica: the journal networks, and Isaiah's press. Isaiah said his printers would set a run of the eight pages in facsimile for the day — true facsimile, the chipped e and all — for the people who wanted paper and could not copy, the old, the ones with bad hands, the ones who worked doubles and had no evening to spend with a pen. "Not instead of copying," he said. "As well as. A seed crop."

Chronos: Kovač's letters to the six paper keepers she had worked with in 2108, and through them the witness-book people, the networks that had learned in the war to keep a thing in many places. Tanaka would carry the first sets across the lake herself.

The SFW: Isaiah would take a set to President Martinez in person on the twenty-fourth, by the diplomatic air route, as the one channel into a country with no interfaces and no tools, only people and paper and town halls.

Memoria, Axiom and Synthesis, which still had their Judges: the small journal circles, the quiet ones, through the Synthesis Institute for Historical Methods, Isaiah's old academic channel from before any of it. "The Institute," Isaiah said, drily. "Where I learned the phrase controlled vocabulary." Nobody laughed, because the irony was too exact.

And the CNT: Elena's paper routes. She said only, "I'll tell you what they'll bear by the first," and did not promise more, and Kira wrote nothing down for the east yet, because Elena did not give a number until she had one.

"A date," Dmitri said. "Formally. A plan without a date is a wish."

"The fifteenth of July," Marcus said. "Two o'clock." He did not dress it. "It's the fifteenth. The city's already at the stone at two on the fifteenth; they've been going for eight years; nobody has to be told where to stand or when. And two o'clock is the hour the design took, in 2106. We give it back, full, in the open, in a thousand hands, at the exact hour it was stolen in secret by one."

Nobody argued. Kira wrote it at the top of a clean sheet. Jul 15. 14:00. By hand. Every nation.

"How do we keep it quiet," Maya said, "with this many people copying?"

"We don't," Kira said, because she had already done that arithmetic and there was no comfort in pretending otherwise. "We can't. A set is going to be in three hundred Veridican homes within a fortnight, and forty Chronos households, and a printing house, and a President's office, and émigré volunteers along four borders. There is no version of this that is secret." She looked up. "And it doesn't have to be. That was always the price and it was always worth paying. A secret plan belongs to whoever holds the secret. A plan a thousand people are carrying belongs to no one, and can't be bought from any one person, because there's no one person to buy it from. It doesn't have to be secret. It has to be many." She wrote, under the date: Not secret. Many.

It was the hardest thing to teach the people who came new to the work, because every instinct a person had learned under the old world said that a dangerous truth must be hidden, guarded, held close, trusted to as few hands as possible. The circle had spent nine years unlearning it. A truth held close was a truth that could be seized, bought, tortured out of its one keeper, or simply lost when the keeper died; the old man had taught them that with a song and a grave. The only safe truth was the one that had already escaped, that was in too many hands to be gathered up, that had stopped being anyone's secret and become everyone's possession. You did not protect such a thing by hiding it. You protected it by giving it away as fast and as widely as you could, so that by the time anyone moved against it the move was pointless, because the thing was already in three hundred kitchens and would be in three thousand by the time the order came. Secrecy was the old world's reflex and the old world's weakness. The new world's strength was that it kept nothing back, and so had nothing that could be taken.

Kovač took the copyists' register — the book that would hold who copied and who countersigned and when, folders being her element — and ruled its first columns while they talked. Kira's first tally, by the end of the evening, was three hundred and twelve sets promised in Veridica and forty in Chronos, with Isaiah's facsimile run on top of that, and the east still open.

They went, one by one, into the warm June night, and Kira cleared the table and did not go to bed. She opened Nikolai's traffic log instead — the record of the CNT relays, the small reconciliations every three to five days, the patient tending of the garden they had watched for five years — and she laid the last three months of it end to end and looked for the place where it changed.

It did not change.

The tending went on exactly as it had gone on all spring. It had not sped up when the Compiler's copy was found in April. It had not paused when copy X came up the stairs in May. It had not so much as missed a beat on the tenth of June, when Nikolai proved that the whole of it ran on the enemy's own clock. There was no spike of alarm, no scramble, no sudden silence that would mean they knew they were watched and had gone to ground. The garden was tended on the fifteenth of June as it had been tended on the fifteenth of March, placidly, every three to five days, as though nothing in the world had been found.

And Chen Zhao, at the Commission, had been warm as ever for three weeks. Marcus had said nothing to him since the night Wei came; Chen Zhao had asked Marcus nothing. No one had been arrested. Nothing had leaked from the other side, no statement, no denial, no counter-move, no warning shot. She logged the whole of it as an incident, in her incident format, and found that she was writing an incident report about the absence of an incident, which was a thing she had never done before, and which frightened her more than an incident would have.

At ten past one Marcus came to the kitchen doorway in his undershirt, his glasses pushed up into his hair, having looked in on Tamsin, who slept with her door ajar and one arm off the edge of the bed.

"They haven't moved," Kira said, without looking up from the log.

Marcus stood in the doorway a moment, looking at the stack of photographs and the clean sheet with the date at the top of it.

"That's what worries me," he said.
