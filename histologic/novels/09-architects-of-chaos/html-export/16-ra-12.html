Chapter 16: RA·12

Monday, June 10, 2115. The capital, and the farmhouse

At six in the morning Nikolai Volkov sat in the back room of the flat with the tools running and the house asleep behind him — Maya, and their son, who was two and a half and slept the way Tamsin slept, flung sideways — and looked at the one name he had not been able to place.

RA·12. Rustam Aslanov. It had been under UNCHECKED for seven weeks, since the reading. No factorepo west of the lake held the name; he had searched the Civic Record and the old Ministry stores and three nations' public indices, patiently, in the way that was the only way he knew how to be, and there was nothing. A mark, a copy numeral, a real ink signature, and a blank where the person should be. It bothered him more than any of the placed names, because a placed name was a grief and a blank was a question, and he had never been able to leave a question.

His wallet still held the primer page with the rooftop on it — a page from a children's civic primer that he had carried since Ashford, since Book 4, the page that was supposed to be a memory of his own schooling and was in fact only a thing he had been told he remembered, a seeded childhood, the rooftop and the lesson and the little sum. He kept it the way he kept everything, in three places if he could, because he had learned in the hardest school there was what it cost to keep a thing in one.

The paper came first, that morning, before the traffic.

On Friday — the seventh — Elena's first test courier had come back across the eastern border from the unaligned side, the dry run for the routes she was building, nine days out and back through country that belonged to nobody. He had come back thin and quiet and with his boots ruined, and he had brought, among the proof that the route could be walked, a thing Nikolai was now turning over in the lamplight: a CNT civic primer. Printed on paper. Bound. A schoolbook, because the CNT had no interfaces and taught its children from books, the way everyone had once taught everyone, with ink and a teacher's voice. It was for children of about eight — he could tell from the size of the type and the simplicity of the sums — and Elena's courier had pencilled a rough translation in the margins as he walked.

The Founder, one page said, under a woodcut portrait of a stern man, Rustam Aslanov, who in the year 2068 called the first Supreme Council to stand against the Histologic expansion. And a date below the portrait: Died 2093. And on the last page, under a photograph this time, a living face: Our Chairman, Timur Aslanov, the Founder's son, who guards the Council and the people.

Nikolai sat very still.

In Veridica the CNT had only ever been the Council and the Chairman. Forty-seven years of enmity, and his own record had never once carried the founder's name or the Chairman's name, because the CNT published no officials' names abroad and Veridica's record had never thought to ask. He had grown up afraid of a nameless Council. And here was the name, in a schoolbook for eight-year-olds, printed plainly, the way you print a thing you are proud of. Rustam Aslanov. The twelfth mark. The signatory with no principle and no page and no visible work, who had signed the Register in 2066 and then, two years later, founded the enemy.

He laid the CNT primer page beside his own primer page, the rooftop, under the lamp. Two schoolbooks. One taught a Veridican child a seeded sum on a rooftop that had never existed. One taught a CNT child to love a Founder who had signed the enemy's own design. Arithmetic for young citizens, he thought. The same hand had written both schools, from one table, in 2066.

It did something to him, holding the two pages together under the lamp, that was worse than any of the single revelations of the spring. Each name on the Register had been a grief for somebody at the farmhouse table; this was not a grief, it was a vertigo, the floor of forty-seven years tilting under him. He had grown up with the Confederacy as the given shape of the world's danger, the thing beyond the last border, the reason for the fences and the drills and the particular quality of fear that lived in the word the Council. His own seeded childhood, the rooftop and the sum he remembered and had never lived, had been printed to make a loyal little citizen against exactly that danger. And the danger had been printing its own children the same way, from the same table, so that two sets of eight-year-olds on two sides of a hated border were being made, by one design, to fear each other, to complete each other, to be the two walls that kept the real thing from being looked at. He had spent his life afraid of the far side of a mirror. He laid his primer page and theirs flat and side by side, two schoolbooks, and felt the particular sickness of a man who has just been shown the back of a stage set he lived his whole life believing was a country.

Then the traffic, which he worked in his own careful way, because the paper was a clue and the traffic was a proof, and he did not confuse the two.

He had the Book 8 printout, which he had kept — of course he had kept it, in three places. The maintenance log store's own record of the twenty-first of May, 2110, the four minutes from thirteen thirty-one to thirteen thirty-five, the transfer contingency, the thing that had run east nine minutes before the cascade and become a new original. And the thing about the receiving site, the thing he had noticed in 2110 and filed and never been able to use, was that its acknowledgements had carried their own clock, and that clock ran a fixed amount behind the national clock: not a wobble, not a drift, a steady fixed lag. Zero point four one seconds. He had measured it then across the four minutes and it had held to the hundredth, which was the signature of a clock that was simply set to a different master and kept good time against it.

The garden gave him the same number. The one reconciliation notice that the fault had logged whole back in March — the tending burst that got away into the open — carried an acknowledgement with the same offset. Zero point four one. And the tending bursts that they could see only as shadows, as timing and volume on the passive tools, all left the unaligned strips by the same last relay before the wall, the same final hop, every time.

And then he went and got the third thing, the thing anyone could get, which was why it was the best thing. The CNT Supreme Council ran an external bulletin service — the one CNT network the outside world was allowed to hear, a channel on which the Council broadcast its statements to foreign missions on a published schedule, so that embassies could know the official line. It was public. It was meant to be heard. He had been recording it for three weeks, every scheduled bulletin, and he had timestamped every one of them against the national clock, and the Council's bulletin service ran zero point four one seconds behind, and it left by the same last relay.

Same clock. Same door.

He checked it four times, because a thing checked once was checked once. The transfer of 2110, the tending traffic of 2115, and the Council's own official bulletins all kept the same master clock and all came out of the same final relay before the border. The third site was not an anonymous facility in some eastern waste. It was on the same infrastructure as the Supreme Council of the CNT. The true source of a whole world's record had been sitting, for five years, on the enemy's own government machines.

Maya woke at seven, and came in with her hair loose, and looked at his face, and did not ask. She sat down at her own tools and ran his comparison cold — he did not tell her the answer; he never told her the answer; that was the whole value of her — and twenty minutes later she said, "Same relay," and looked at him. "Same offset." And then, as she had said once in the war, "Tools this good are built for the job they're used for," and this time she was not talking about his tools. She was talking about the Council's, about a nation that had the best cyber warfare in the world and had always been said, in the uneasy jokes of the trade, to be suspiciously good at it for a place so poor, and now Nikolai knew why it was so good at it, because it had been built, from 2066, to be the vault.

That was the thing the clock offset told him that the primer only hinted: not merely that the design had founded the Confederacy, but that it had founded it for this, had drawn an enemy forty million strong and armed it and taught it to hate, across three generations, so that when the day came that the design needed a place to keep the one record safe from the very reformers it had also planned for, there would be a nation no reformer could enter, no storm could reach, no seeding could touch, with the best cyber warfare in the world and a founder's son on its council, sitting exactly where a dying machine could throw a copy of a whole world's truth nine minutes before it died. The enemy was not a mistake the design was fleeing to. The enemy was the design's own strongroom, built first, forty-seven years early, against this exact afternoon. Nikolai sat with that under the lamp while the house slept, and found that his hands were not quite steady on the keys, and kept a copy of the comparison in three places before he let himself stop, because a man who had just understood that the enemy was the vault was a man who did not trust the night to leave his one copy alone.

There was one more thing, a small one, that he filed rather than solved. The relay that carried the traffic between Synthesis and the eastern strips was a leased line, and the lease, when he traced the billing as far as he could, was billed to a reconciliation research network under the Liu Wei Endowment — the dead financier's foundation, the one Tanaka had told them was writing letters to Morrison. He put it under UNCHECKED, beside the note about the letter, and did not know yet what it was for, and would not guess, because the gap between a lease and a conclusion was a gap he did not jump.

He laid the whole shape out in his head, in order, because he thought best in order. Principle Two: let there be enemies outside them all. The Annex: an outer border, dashed, unnamed, dated 2068. RA·12, signed in 2066, two years before the Council was founded. Book 8: the signal ran east, and Viktoria Sokolov went east in March of 2110. And now: the founder of the enemy had signed the design, his son ruled the enemy and was his heir, and the true source had moved onto the enemy's own machines at the moment the machine died.

"For forty-seven years we were afraid of them," he said to Maya, which was as close as he came to a joke. "Apparently we should have been afraid of their filing."

The circle came out to the farmhouse that evening — Marcus, Kira, Dmitri, Elena, Isaiah, Kovač and Maya, around the kitchen table, the lamp lit — and Nikolai laid it out the way he worked, without looking up, the primer page and its pencilled translation, the Book 8 printout, three weeks of bulletin logs, set in a row along the table, and he walked them through it: the clock, the relay, the offset, the public bulletins anyone could hear. Kira checked the offset herself, pulling the figure off the acknowledgements with her own eye, and got zero point four one, and sat back. Elena said only that the primer had cost her courier nine days and a pair of boots, and that she thought the routes would hold.

Across the table Marcus unfolded QUESTIONS FOR THE WAR, the fourth list, the oldest question on it. He looked at the last line a moment — Where is east? — the question he had asked a dying machine nine minutes too late five years ago and carried unanswered ever since. Then he drew one line through it, cleanly, once, and wrote nothing beside it, because the answer was the thing on the table and did not need to be written twice. Nikolai saw him do it, and did not comment, because some things you let a man do without comment.

"They didn't run to the enemy," Nikolai said, with his hands flat on the Book 8 printout, to the whole table. "In 2110 we thought the design fled east, to the people who hated it, to hide among the one country it couldn't reach. That's not what happened." He looked up at last. "They made the enemy. In 2068. On purpose. The country we've been afraid of our whole lives is the back room of the thing we've been fighting. They didn't run to the enemy. They made one, and moved in."
