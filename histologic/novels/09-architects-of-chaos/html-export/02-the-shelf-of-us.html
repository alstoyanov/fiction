Chapter 2: The Shelf of Us

Wednesday, March 20, 2115. Elena's farmhouse, the border zone

The damper on the stove had to be opened two fingers, no more, so that the crack in the firebox door glowed and drew and did not smoke back into the kitchen, and this spring his right hand could not manage it.

He noticed the failure the way he noticed everything now, without complaint and with a certain professional interest, as a man watches weather he cannot change. He had turned seventy-seven earlier in the month. The hand would not make the small precise turn the damper wanted; the fingers went where they liked, not where he sent them. He did it with the left hand instead, badly, on the second try, and got the two fingers' width, and the crack came up orange and the smoke went where smoke should go. Then he put the barley on, and while it came to the heat he hummed, under his breath, the Lakeshore Air, the ferry verses, because the ferry verses were for the morning and the lullaby was for children and he was alone.

His hands were the first part of him to be old in a way he could not hide inside a tune. The voice was going too, thinner at the top than it had been, but a voice could be worked around; you dropped a verse an octave and nobody but you knew it had cost you anything. The hands could not be worked around. The hands did the damper and the knife and the pencil, and lately they did them like someone else's hands, lent to him, not well broken in.

He had been the keeper of a thing for fifty years, and the thing was large, and he was the last of its keepers, and his hands had begun to tell him that the keeping had an end now with a date on it that was not far off. He did not mind the dying; he had been officially dead for forty of his seventy-seven years and had found the condition suited him, had done his best work dead, humming through cell doors to men the record said did not exist. What he minded was the arithmetic. There had been three of them who read the grey journal at that shelf, and two were in the orchard, and he was the third and the last, and a thing known by one living person is a thing one winter from being known by none. He had taught the young ones to write it down, many hands, so that no one tune in no one old mouth carried the weight alone. He had taught it to everyone but himself. The riddle he still carried alone, uncopied, cold, in the one place a machine could not look, which was also the one place a grave could not be robbed of and could not be asked. He was running out of the time in which a man can hand a thing on, and he knew it, the way you know weather in a bad hip, and that was the whole of why he had gone out to the orchard this morning instead of next spring.

Elena had come back on Monday night from the capital with the message, and had said it the way she said everything, flatly, in the voice of a woman who had commanded people in a bad time and had never entirely put the command down: that the Deputy Minister who had died in the lost hour was alive, and in the capital, and wanted to see them all. He had hummed the rising phrase at her, the one that meant yes, go on, I hear you, and had gone on stirring, and had thought about it in the night in a single paragraph and had not followed the paragraph anywhere, because following things at night was how an old man lost his sleep and gained nothing. The dead were coming back. Well. He was one of the dead himself, by the record of two nations, and had been for forty years, and here he was at a stove. If the dead were coming back it was only fair they start with the ones who had gone first.

He had kept the riddle cold for five years.

She had given it to him in April of 2110, four lines, and he had done with it what he did with everything he meant to keep: he had set it to the Lakeshore Air and put it where the tune lived, and he had not looked at it. That was the whole of the method and it had never once failed him. For forty years the machines had hunted for heat — for the thing a person dwelt on, returned to, could not leave alone — and what a man looked at got hot, and so the way to keep a thing from the machines was never to look at it, to hold it the way you hold a coal you do not intend to drop, by not closing your hand. He had sung the four lines with their words only alone, in the yard, with the wind off the lake taking the sound before it reached the house; and everywhere else, in the kitchen, at the graves, he had hummed the tune of them with no words at all, so that even his own ear got nothing but the shape.

There had been no Judge in Veridica for five years. There was nothing left to hide the coal from. The habit had held anyway, because a habit kept for forty years is not a thing you set down because the reason for it has gone; and because, he thought, stirring the barley, some part of him had understood that the day he looked at the riddle would be a day with a great deal on the far side of it, and he had not been in a hurry to arrive there.

His hands had changed that. His hands had told him, this spring, how long he had, and it was not long, and a thing kept for the day someone needed it was no use to anyone if the keeper died with it still unopened in his hand. For when I'm not here to be asked, she had said, giving it to him. She had not been here to be asked for five years. He was running out of the other half of that sentence.

Late in the morning he went out to the orchard.

It was cold and bright, the sky scoured, the lake a hard grey beyond the barn. The plum trees stood in tight bud along their rows, not yet in flower; the lakeshore spring came late and the plum came latest of all, and there would be no white on these branches for three weeks yet. The two graves were between the rows, side by side, with a hand's breadth of turf left between them, grass grown over now and indistinguishable from the rest except that he knew it was there. Alexei on the left. Elise on the right. He had dug neither; Elena and Dmitri had dug them, in the spring of 2110, ten days apart, and he had stood at both and sung.

He stood at the foot of both now, and he sang the riddle once, with its words, under his breath, the way he had sung it alone in the yard for five years.

Look where they swept the shelf of us, behind the number no one reads; the old man peeled his apple there — ask him the number. Ask him twice.

The last phrase lifted at the end, as all his phrases lifted, like a question put and left open. For five years he had turned away from the question at exactly that point, let the tune close itself and the words sink back under it. This time he stood in the cold and did not turn away.

One line. He would open one line, the way he had learned a long tune as a boy — a line at a time, and never the next until the last had settled all the way down, because a man who grabbed at the whole of a thing got none of it, and a man who took it a line at a time got all of it and kept it. One line today. He could feel the second already waiting behind the first, patient, pressing a little, and he would not let it open. One.

Look where they swept the shelf of us.

He let it lead, and the memory came up in the order it had always lain in, the order of 2063, when he had been twenty-five and new and the world had not yet been built.

The periodicals trolley, first: the squeak of its near wheel, the smell of the binding glue, the grey journal lying on it among the ordinary quarterlies with its plain cover and its anonymous essays, waiting to be catalogued by a junior archivist who read everything he catalogued because he could not help it. The shelf he had given it, with his own hand, on a card in his own young writing: Periodicals, Governance. And the Thursdays — it had always been Thursdays — a young woman from the systems department who came in with ink on her fingers and an argument already loaded, and sat across the reading-room table from him and whispered, so as not to be shushed, that rhyme was a compression algorithm, a way of storing a long thing in a short space and checking it had not corrupted, and he whispered back that it was a prayer, that you said it the second time to find out whether you still meant it; and neither of them ever gave an inch, and she was nearly always right, and he never told her so. And Kerr, passing the end of the table in his cotton gloves with a box of foxed pamphlets, pretending not to hear the two of them, and hearing every word, and smiling at the shelf he was reshelving so that they would not see him smile.

And then the morning in '66. Two men from a Ministry so new that its letterhead still smelled of the printer, with a list, taking the issues of the grey journal down off the shelf and away in a box. He had stood at the end of the aisle with a cart of returns and watched them do it and had understood nothing except that it was wrong, that you did not take things out of an archive, that an archive was the one place where taking-away was not supposed to happen. Swept. That was the word she had chosen, and it was the exact word: not removed, not reclassified, which were the words the two men would have used; swept, the way you sweep a floor, the way you sweep a thing you have decided is dirt.

He stood with that a while, in the cold, among the buds.

And then the word that was not an event stopped him where he stood.

Us.

She had not written the shelf. She had not written the journal's shelf, which was what a careful person would write, which was what she had been, careful past all bearing, careful to the end of her life. She had written the shelf of us. And it was true in a way he had not let himself see in five years of not looking: that the Governance shelf in the Historical Archive had been, for a little while in the 2060s, the one place in the whole building where the three of them had come together. She took the journal down off it. He had catalogued the journal and shelved it there. Kerr passed it, in his gloves, and heard them, and said nothing. Three people, at one shelf, for a season. And then the same shelf was the one they swept, and the readers of that shelf were the ones taken away, afterward, one at a time, to be helped: Kerr first, and the two who had asked with Kerr, and in the end, in a different way, her, erased before the thing she had built was finished being built. The shelf of us. She had made a little monument out of the place they were destroyed, and hidden a key behind it, and set the whole of it to his tune, because she had known he would be the one to stand here and hear it.

It scans, he heard her say, from the farmhouse table in 2110, dry, with the tremor already in her voice. You always said mine didn't.

He laughed, once, aloud, in the orchard, which he had not done at these graves before; a short sound, more cough than laugh, that startled a crow up off the barn roof.

It undid him a little, the laugh, because it was hers in it — her dry correction of his vowels, her insistence across forty-seven years and a grave that rhyme was a compression algorithm, a way of folding a long true thing into a short space so that it could be carried and checked and not lost. She had been right, as she had been nearly always right, and she had proved it in the end with her own life: she had folded the location of the one copy of her confession into four lines of his tune and handed it to the one man whose head a machine had never been able to read, and set it to a melody every grandmother on the lakeshore knew, so that it could sit in the open air for five years, sung by a dead man over two graves, and never once be found. It was the cleverest thing anyone had ever done to him, and the kindest, and she had done it dying, and she had done it to make him the instrument of her undoing of herself, and he stood among the buds and laughed his cough of a laugh at the sheer completed cheek of it, and a little at the grief, because the two had stopped being separable in him some years ago and he had given up trying to separate them.

Then he stopped himself, deliberately, the way he stopped a verse before the chorus when he wanted the room to lean in. He could feel the second line waiting, risen now, close behind the first — behind the number no one reads — and it would have been the easiest thing in the world to let it come, and he did not let it come. One line. A thing you took a line at a time was a thing you kept. A thing you grabbed at whole was a thing that slipped. And there was, he thought, going back toward the house before the barley caught, a decency in it as well: that he should give the first line to the orchard, and the two of them lying in it, before he carried the rest of it to a reading room in the capital where the end of it waited.

At dusk the fog came up off the lake, real weather, grey and slow, filling the low ground between the barn and the water until the orchard was a rumour in it. He lit the lamp and took down one of Elena's plain accounting ledgers, the ones she kept the farm's numbers in, ruled in faint blue, and he took a pencil, and he sat at the kitchen table where Elise Vance had sat ten days before she died, and he wrote.

His hand shook. He wrote slowly, forming the letters the way he had formed them as a boy learning a fair hand, each one deliberate, because a shaking hand that hurries makes nothing anyone can read. For the first time in five years the riddle existed outside his head, where a thing could be found, where a thing could be taken; and he wrote only the one line, because only the one line was open, and the rest was still a coal in a closed hand.

Look where they swept the shelf of us

He looked at it a long moment in the lamplight, the fog at the dark window, the barley gone quiet on the back of the stove.

Then he drew a line under the last word, and then a second line under the first, pressing the pencil hard enough that he felt the point score through to the page beneath, so that the ledger under this one would carry the ghost of the word even after this page was gone; and he sat with it, an old man with a marked word in the lamplight.

She meant us, he thought. Her, and me, and Kerr.
