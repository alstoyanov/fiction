Chapter 8: The Second Letter

Thursday, April 11, 2115. The Historical Archive, correspondence registry

Thursday, 11 April. Registry, basement. Box: CORRESPONDENCE 2066, J–L. Requested Monday, 14:40. Delivered 09:30.

Helena Kovač briefed herself the way she always did, silently, in the registry's own idiom, because a thing stated in the idiom of the record was a thing that could be checked later by anyone, including herself, and she did not trust memory, hers or anyone's, which was why she had given her life to paper. Paper could be foxed and torn and lost and burned. It could not be adjusted behind your back while you slept. She had kept folders through the whole of the old regime and she had kept them for the same reason a bird keeps to the hedge: because it was the cover that held.

She was sixty-one. Rimless glasses, hands in her coat pockets, the coat still on because the basement was cold and because she thought better with her hands where she could not see them doing anything. Mr. Petrov had asked for Thursday. She had put her finger on Thursday on the retrieval form on Monday without a word, and the clerk had stamped it, and she had not explained, because the explanation was not the clerk's and because she knew it: Vance had read at this Archive on Thursdays. If the old woman had hidden a thing for a particular hand to find, she would have wanted it found on the day she herself would have been here. Kovač did not believe the dead watched. She believed the living owed the dead the courtesy of their patterns.

She had carried the ledger page since Monday, folded in her inside pocket: four lines in Mr. Petrov's shaking hand, line one underlined twice. She knew it by heart now, which she had not intended and could not undo. Look where they swept the shelf of us.

The registry clerk — unnamed, young, incurious in the blessed way of someone who had never been taught to fear a file — brought the box up on a trolley, set it on the retrieval table under the strip light, had her sign the delivery line, and left them alone, because down here you were left alone; it was the whole point of a registry basement.

It was a buff folder, soft at the corners, the registry hand across its tab: KERR, A.

It was thicker than two letters should be. She noticed that before she noticed anything else, before she opened it, the way she had noticed over forty years that the folders which mattered were always a little too thick or a little too thin for what they claimed to hold, that the record gave itself away in its thickness before it gave itself away in its words. She set her hand flat on it a moment, the way she might on a patient's chart, and then she opened it.

The two letters were on top, Kerr's own carbon copies on Archive letterhead, each with the registry's receiving stamp across the corner, RECEIVED — FILED, and the date. They were addressed upstairs, to the Director, and through the Director to the new Ministry whose men had carried the box away — a Ministry she would not name, because its name was not typed on either sheet and she would not supply from memory what the paper did not say.

The first was dated Monday, the eighth of February, 2066. It was a model of its kind, four sentences, courteous to the edge of warmth, requesting to be told where the issues formerly held at Periodicals, Governance, 114 had been removed to, and on whose authority, so that the catalogue may record it. That was the whole of his concern, on the face of it: that the catalogue should be right. That a thing which had left should be shown to have left, and why.

The second was dated Monday, the first of March, 2066. Three weeks later. It was the same request, in nearly the same words, with no reproach in it anywhere, only one clause added at the front: Since I have had no reply to my letter of the eighth ultimo. No reply was filed to either. There was no carbon of an answer, no note of one, nothing stapled, nothing cross-referenced. He had asked, politely, in writing, twice, and the Archive, or the Ministry behind it, had simply not answered, and had filed his asking, as a registry files everything, including its own silences. She thought, holding the two thin sheets, of every form she herself had signed and sent up and had answered by nothing at all, and how she had kept the carbons anyway, and how here was a man fifty years dead who had done the same and whose carbons had outlived the catalogue that erased what he asked about.

Behind the second letter, in the folder, was a flat bundle.

It was folded inside a single plain sheet, wrapped the way an archivist wraps a thing meant to keep — not glued, not pinned, just folded close in a protecting leaf, so the edges would not foist and the light would not reach it. A single typed sheet was clipped to the front of the wrapper with a steel paper clip gone to rust. She unclipped the sheet and set it aside, face up, unread; one thing at a time; the bundle first.

She opened the wrapper.

The pages inside were typed. She looked at the first line of the first and her eye went, by thirty years of trained habit, not to the words but to the letters, and there it was: the lowercase e, every one of them, with the same small bite taken out of the top of the loop, the chipped e of a single machine, consistent down the page and, when she fanned the edge, consistent through the bundle. And in the lower right corner of the first page, where a copy numeral would be — I, or VII, or XI, the small roman figure that marked which of the thirteen a page belonged to — there was nothing. Clean paper. No numeral. Not on the first page, nor, when she checked three more at random by lifting their corners, on any of them.

"The Compiler's copy," she said. She said it once, aloud, flatly, because it had to be said once and she would not say it twice. Beside her, Mr. Petrov closed his eyes.

She had spent forty years among folders, and she had developed, over those years, a feeling that she would not have described to anyone as anything but professional, which was that a folder had a weight beyond its paper — that the documents which had cost the most to make, or would cost the most to lose, sat in the hand with a density that had nothing to do with grams. She had felt it for ten false death certificates she had signed to keep ten people alive. She had felt it for the intake pages at Ashford. She felt it now, in the basement, with the strip light humming and the trolley at her elbow, holding the thing five books and fifty years had been circling — and the density of it was not exhilaration, which she had half expected, but something closer to dread, the dread of a person who has found the one document she has spent her life protecting other documents from the likes of, and who understands that from this minute forward she is its keeper, that she has not found it so much as inherited it, with all that inheriting costs.

She did not read it. It was not hers to read first, and it was not a thing to read on a basement retrieval table under a strip light with a trolley at her elbow. She counted it instead, which was a different act, and a lawful one, the act of an archivist taking an inventory, and she did it by the folio numbers at the foot of each page, turning only the corners, touching the pages as little as a person could touch them.

Page one, torn — torn lengthwise, down the right edge, a long clean tear where a strip had been taken away, the strip that would have carried the lower part of the contents. The body of page one remained.

Then a gap. Pages two, three and four were not there.

Then pages five through thirty-nine, continuous, every folio number following the last, no break, thirty-five sheets in an unbroken run.

No page forty.

She understood what she was counting as she counted it, and the understanding was its own kind of grief, held at arm's length by the counting. The Introduction, pages two through four, was not here; it had gone to a farmhouse in a songbook in 2108. The strip of page one was not here; it had gone the same way. Page forty, the handwritten Warning, was not here; the same. The Compiler had taken her own pages out of her own copy and sent them away, years apart, to be kept by the one man she trusted, and had left the body of the thing — the six signed principles, and the Annex, and the last page, the dangerous one — folded behind an unanswered letter where only a question could reach it. She had dismembered herself and scattered the pieces across her two keepers, so that no one place held the whole.

Between pages thirty-two and thirty-three there was a faint rust ghost on the paper, the shadow of a clip that had held an extra leaf in place for a long time and then been removed, the oxide print of it still on both facing pages. The leaf was gone. She did not need Mr. Petrov to tell her what leaf had been clipped in after thirty-two; she knew that Kira Osman had carried a page since 2110, front and back, and that it had been inserted, that it was not part of the run. The ghost of its clip was all that was left of it here.

She let herself see three things more and then no further, because the rest belonged to the farmhouse and to all of them, not to her, alone, in a basement. She saw that there were marks at the feet of pages — at the foot of every even page within a principle, and again at the end of each principle — small, two capital letters, a raised dot, a number, typed in the same chipped machine; and she did not read them, did not let her eye resolve a single one of them into its letters, because to read one was to begin, and this was not the place to begin. And she saw that the last page, page thirty-nine, was not prose but a list, a column of entries with the marks beside them; and she turned it face down on the stack without reading a line of it, because a list of names was the single most dangerous page in the world and she would not be the first and only person to read it bent over a trolley in a cold room.

Before either of them read one further word, she took the registry's paper removal ledger from the shelf where it lived, the plain book in which anything taken from the basement for examination upstairs was entered by hand, and she wrote in it, in her own name, with the time: CORRESPONDENCE 2066, J–L: contents of folder KERR, A. removed for examination. To be returned. H. Kovač. 10:55. She would not steal from an archive. Not even this. An archive that could be stolen from, even by the people who loved it, even for the best reason in the world, was not an archive, and she had spent her life being the kind of person who did not steal from one, and she was not going to stop being that person on the one day it would have been easiest to. She knew, writing it, that a paper removal ledger in an open basement could be read by anyone who came down here and turned its pages. She wrote the entry anyway. The alternative was worse.

Then she turned to the sheet she had set aside.

The rusted clip had printed its own small stain onto the body of page one; the two had lain together a long time. This sheet was typed, but on a different machine: she saw at once that the es were whole, every one of them clean and closed, so that whatever this was, it had never been among the thirteen, had never passed through the chipped typewriter, had been made somewhere else by someone else and then laid against the Compiler's copy and clipped there and folded in with it.

It was titled, in capitals, ON KEEPING.

At the foot of it was a mark, like the others and not like them: two capital letters, a raised dot, a number — and a single line struck through it, ruled once across the whole mark with a straightedge, firm, deliberate, so that the mark was cancelled and still perfectly legible beneath its line. It was the first struck-through mark any of them had seen. She looked at the two capital letters, and she knew — the way you cannot help knowing — what name began with those two letters in the circle she was standing at the edge of, whose uncle had worn a different collar; and she stopped. She did not let herself read the number. She did not let herself try the two letters against any other mark she had glimpsed on the pages below, though she had glimpsed several, though it would have taken a second. Some things you do not do on a floor. She would do her arithmetic at the farmhouse table, in front of witnesses, in her own good time, or not at all.

She gave the sheet to Mr. Petrov.

He read it standing, slowly, the whole page, his lips moving a little the way they did when he was fixing something in the part of him that kept things; and his face did, as he read, what she had watched it do once before, in a cold back room in January of 2107, when he had told the circle about a teacher named Kerr and had refused to let anyone write it down. It was not weeping. It was something stiller and older than weeping, the face of a man meeting, in a dead man's plain prose, the voice that had taught him. The page was courteous and unhurried, the voice of someone who had believed an archive was a promise and had been proved wrong and had, before he was proved wrong, written down why the promise mattered. Then he read the last two lines aloud, to her, to the cold room, in the thin true voice that still rose a little at the end of a line:

"A record kept by one hand is a confession waiting for its torturer. Keep it by many hands."

She stood very still. She had been in that back room in January of 2107. She had heard him say, to all of them, out of nowhere, as though it had come to him from the air: Write it down. Many hands. She had read, since, in a thing carried out of a farmhouse, the last line of the Compiler's own handwritten Warning: Build by many hands. And she understood, standing on the cold concrete, that the Warning had taken its answer from this page — that the woman who designed the cage had reached, for the one thing that could undo it, past all her own cleverness to a sentence written by the man she and her circle had corrected for saying no; and that Mr. Petrov had carried his teacher's own phrase in his mouth for fifty years, through forty years of singing it to strangers through cell doors, without ever knowing whose words they were. Everything we did, we did with his words, and didn't know it.

He sat down on the registry floor, between the steel stacks, with the page on his knee, an old man of seventy-seven lowering himself carefully to the concrete because his legs had decided for him. Kovač took off her glasses, and put them back on, and then she sat down beside him on the cold floor, in her dark coat, at sixty-one, with the buff folder and its dismembered miracle on the table above them and the two polite unanswered letters on top of it; and for a long time neither of them said anything at all.
