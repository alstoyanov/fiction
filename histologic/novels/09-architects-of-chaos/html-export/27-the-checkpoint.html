Chapter 27: The Checkpoint

Monday, September 2, 2115. Coastal command, the Atlantic coast

05:40. Coastal command. Visibility: fog, real, lifting by 08:00. Traffic: light. Duty officer: self.

Colonel Yevgenia Sokolov wrote her log on paper in short entries, as she had since the war, and she wrote no confession in it and no speech about conscience, because a log was for what happened and not for what the officer felt about it, and she had never once in her life confused the two.

The situation, as the log would state it if the log stated situations, which it did not: since the fifteenth of August the heirs' remedy had carried in three nations and failed in three, and the succession was public, and Amara Singh's testimony was before a court in the capital, and the prosecutor's office would ask that court for warrants on the names in the Register tomorrow, the third. On Friday the prosecutor's office had sent Border Security an informal request — not an order, there being as yet nothing to order — to hold for questioning certain named persons should they present themselves at the coast. Sokolov had replied in writing, within the hour, that she held no person without a warrant, and had filed her reply, and the filing of it was the whole of her position and she had chosen the words so that it would read the same to a court in a year as it read to her deputy this morning.

The manifests had come up overnight. A private aircraft was filed out of the coastal airfield for ten forty, routed east, over Memoria and over Axiom — the two nations that had voted for the World Record and would not trouble an eastbound flight. A Synthesis-flagged vessel was to sail from the harbour on the evening tide. Both passenger lists were lawful and dull and complete, and among the names on them were two she read and did not write into her log: Chen Zhao, and Sarah Okonkwo. Other names followed, and she read them once, and they stayed off the page, because a name she wrote became a thing she had recorded knowing, and there were names it was better, for the day she was planning, not to have recorded knowing. Thomas Wei was on Friday's list of persons of interest. He was on no manifest at all.

Her authority was plain to her, as it was plain to her deputy, who stood by the plot table and waited to be told. Without a warrant she could not hold anyone. But without a warrant she could still order a documents check on any departure, and a documents check could lawfully take up to six hours, and in six hours the warrants that the prosecutor would seek tomorrow might, with a telephone call she was entitled to make, exist today. She had the lever. Her deputy knew she had the lever. The whole morning was the question of whether she would pull it, and her deputy, who was a good officer and had served her since the war, stood at the plot table and did not ask, because he had learned that she did not like to be asked and would tell him when she had decided.

She had decided days ago. The morning was only the enacting of it, and the enacting would have to look, to the record and to her deputy and to any court that came after, exactly like a careful officer declining to exceed her authority — which it also, truthfully, was; she had spent her life inside the space where the lawful thing and the chosen thing were made to coincide, and this was only the largest instance of it. Her mother had taught her that, though her mother would not have called it teaching. The whole of the administrator's art, VS·09's art, the art on the Annex pages, was the arranging of things so that the outcome you wanted arrived wearing the uniform of procedure, so that no one could point to the choice because the choice was dressed as a rule. Yevgenia had learned it at her mother's table and used it against her mother's design, which was, she supposed, the only inheritance she had ever been going to get and the only use she was ever going to make of it: to let the architects go, lawfully, by the book, because the book, read exactly, happened to permit the one thing that would deny them the martyrdom they would otherwise have been handed.

She wrote one line in the log, at a quarter to six, which was as close to the inside of her head as the log ever came.

Arrested: martyrs. Gone: an address.

She did not explain it to her deputy. It did not need explaining; it was the whole of the reasoning and it fit on one line. Arrest Chen Zhao on the coast road and he became a persecuted old man, a confessed and repentant Architect dragged from a car by the daughter of a fellow Architect, and the three nations that had signed his remedy would have their martyr and the half-built World Record would grow a saint at its foundation. Let him go, and he became a fixed point in the east, an address, a thing you knew the location of, a design that had a return path you could watch, which Nikolai was already watching, and which paper could be walked toward. A martyr was a story that spread. An address was a place you contained. She had spent her life learning the difference between a story and a place, and she knew which one she could hold.

And there was a second line under the first, which she did not write even in the log, because it was not an officer's reasoning but a daughter's, and she kept the two apart on principle: that she did not want them tried. She had seen what the city did with the names, the crowds at Carver Street, the word east going up at every living name on the steps; she had read the casualty estimate the general would present in a fortnight; and she understood that a trial of Chen Zhao, of Sarah Okonkwo, of her own mother brought home in handcuffs, would not be justice, it would be a furnace, a thing the whole nation would feed on and feed, a public reckoning that would teach the people exactly the appetite the design had spent sixty years cultivating in them, the appetite for an enemy with a face. Gone, the architects were an abstraction in a far country, a thing to be watched and starved and out-waited. Brought home and tried, they would be the best show the capital had staged in a generation, and the show would make them the center the circle had refused to give the networks, would make the design, at the last, exactly what it had always wanted to be, which was the thing everyone was looking at. She would not give it that. She lifted barriers instead.

In the drawer of her desk was her mother's letter of May, and beside it her own one-line reply, filed. Mother. I am where I should be. It is not where you put me. She did not take either out. She knew where the cars were going today, and she knew who would be at the table when they arrived, and she knew that her mother was already at that table, warm and quiet and well, and that one of the names on the morning manifest would sit down beside her within the week. She closed the drawer.

At a quarter past eight she issued the standing written order for the day, signed, timed, and copied to the First Minister's office, so that it was on the record in two places before any car reached any barrier:

No holds without warrant. Traffic normal.

It was exactly, precisely, to the letter, what she was entitled to do. It was also mercy for nobody — she did not do it out of mercy; she did it out of arithmetic, the cold good arithmetic of a commander who had learned her trade from a mother who did arithmetic for a living — and she would have said so, if anyone had asked, which nobody did.

At ten to ten a car came down the coast road from the capital toward the airfield fork, and slowed at the barrier for the documents check that her order permitted and did not require, and in the back of it, behind glass, sat Sarah Okonkwo, about eighty, upright, silver-haired. She looked at the colonel through the window, gently, without apology, with the faint social warmth of a woman greeting someone to whom she had once been introduced at a dinner long ago — and perhaps she had been; a Chancellor's daughter and an Ambassador would have been in the same rooms, decades back; Sokolov did not pursue it. The documents were in order. The barrier lifted. The aircraft left the coastal airfield at ten forty-one and turned east, and Sokolov logged it: 10:41. Aircraft departed, eastbound. Manifest in order. No warrant presented.

At twenty past eleven her radio carried a report from the capital, which she logged without comment: Thomas Wei had presented himself that morning at the Justice Center, of his own accord, and had asked the duty clerk to record that he held himself available to the court. He had not asked for counsel. He had simply gone in, and asked to be written down as present, and stayed. She wrote the time and the fact and nothing else, because there was nothing else, because a man walking into a courthouse to be recorded as available was not a thing that needed a colonel's opinion attached.

In the afternoon she drafted her report to the First Minister, in the first person, formally, as she had drafted the report after Farahani died on the coast road in the war: who had passed through her command, and when, and under which order, and that the order had been hers, signed and timed, and that she stood by it. She left the last line of the report blank until the tide.

At five past six the light was going and the fog had come back off the water, real, grey, and the vessel lay at the harbour waiting on the tide. The last car came down the coast road to the harbour gate with twenty minutes in hand. Her deputy looked at her, once, the look that said the lever is still in your hand, Colonel, and there is still time, and she walked down from the command post to the barrier herself, on foot, in the fog, because a thing like this an officer did with her own two feet and did not delegate.

The rear window of the car came down.

Chen Zhao sat behind it, seventy-four, courteous, unafraid, a glass of tea in its holder on the seat beside him, steadying it with one finger against the roll of the car.

"Colonel," he said.

"Minister," said Sokolov. "Traffic normal."

The barrier lifted. The car went through to the harbour gate, and in time the vessel cast off and worked out past the mole on the evening tide, and its lights went small and then gone into the fog to the east, and Sokolov stood at the barrier until she could no longer see them; and then she went up to the command post and wrote the time into the last line of her report — 18:40. Vessel cleared harbour, eastbound. Order as issued, 08:15. Signed, Y. Sokolov, Col. — and closed the log for the day, and the design had an address now, in the east, and she had given it one, on purpose, within her authority, and she would answer for it to any court that asked, and she thought no court would, because she had been, as her mother had written to her in the spring, apparently very good at it.
