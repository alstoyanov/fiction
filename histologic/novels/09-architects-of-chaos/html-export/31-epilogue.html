Epilogue: Twenty Years

Tuesday, November 15, 2135

At the farmhouse, at dawn, Elena Volkov went out to the orchard, as she did most mornings, and counted the graves.

There were three now, under the plum trees, between the rows. Alexei, on the left, buried in the spring of 2110. Elise Vance, on the right, buried that same spring, beside him, with a grave's width of grass left between them that nobody had explained at the time and nobody had asked about. And between them, in that space, Mikhail Petrov.

He had died at the farmhouse on a Monday in January of 2116, two months after the plaza, aged seventy-seven. Elena had found him in the morning on the crate by the stove, the damper open two fingers the way he always left it, the firebox gone cold, his hands at last entirely still. The Correction Registry had had him dead since 2106, so his death changed nothing in any record that a machine kept. They had buried him in the orchard, in the space between the two he had sung for, which turned out to be exactly the width of a man, as though it had been left for him, which Elena thought, standing over it, it probably had been — that Vance had been laid a grave's width from Alexei in 2110 by people who were not thinking about it, and that the not-thinking had been the old man's doing somehow, a thing arranged by a singer who arranged nothing and foresaw everything in the one way he had, which was the way of a tune that knew where it was going before the singer did.

His name was on the stone in the capital now, with the right year beside it, 2116, cut by people who had not needed the record's permission to cut it. Elena was seventy-one. She still counted first and felt second; it was too late to change the order now and she had stopped wanting to. This morning she counted the three graves, and then she let the rest come, in the order it came, which was Alexei first and always.

She was an old woman now, and she had outlived most of the people who had made her afraid, and she found that she thought less about the design than she had expected to. It had loomed over the whole middle of her life, the great hidden thing, the machine and the men who built it, and now the machine was five years dead on this shore and the men were dead or old in the east and the thing itself had shrunk, in her mind, to its true size, which was smaller than the plum orchard. What was large was the orchard. What was large was three graves and the fruit coming on and a schoolteacher in a country she would never see asking, through a year and three couriers, for a book. The design had wanted to be the biggest thing in the world and had very nearly managed it, and the answer, in the end, had not been a bigger thing; it had been a great many small true things — days, names, graves, letters, a page under a floor — that added up, without anyone planning the sum, to more than the design had ever been. She had spent her life counting. She knew when a column had come out larger than the one beside it. This one had.

Her routes were mostly the post now. Timur Aslanov was dead; she had the fact and not the date; the Confederacy did not publish such things and she had stopped expecting it to. A provincial council in the east had published the Register on its own noticeboards, the whole of it, in its own language, and other councils had copied it, and the copying had gone on in hands that were not theirs and never would be again, which was the point. The Confederacy was opening, slowly, on paper: letters came out now where none had come for forty-seven years; there was a library in a district town that kept a shelf of things the Council had not approved; a schoolteacher had written, through three couriers and a year, to ask for a copy of Isaiah's book. Elena sent a packet east that morning anyway, from habit, the way you keep a light in a window for someone who may already be home.

At her desk, in the city, Kira Osman read the night's comparisons.

She was fifty-six, and grey, and so was Marcus. Nikolai, sixty, still sent her the comparisons at six every morning, out of the open checking tools, which still only compared and still never decided: a dozen records laid against one another, the discrepancies lit up where they fell, and there were always discrepancies, because there were always people, and people remembered their ordinary days a little differently from one another, and the tool did not resolve the difference, it only showed it, and a clerk somewhere would hear both keepers next week, as a clerk had heard both keepers of a warehouse fire twenty years ago.

The World Record still ran in Memoria and Axiom and Synthesis. Three nations, one source. It was quieter than it had been; its reconciliation traffic was public now, by the containment terms, checked every night by strangers in six nations who did not trust it and were free not to; and fewer people used it each year, because a record that could be restored from one source was a record people had learned, slowly, over twenty years, to keep a copy of by hand, just in case, which was itself the end of it, in the way such things ended — not with a collapse, but with people quietly keeping their own copies until the central one was a formality nobody quite leaned on. Kira counted the discrepancies, as she counted them every morning, and logged them, and corrected no one, because correcting no one was the whole of the job, and it had taken her a life to learn that the job was worth doing precisely because it decided nothing.

The World Record had not fallen, and she had stopped expecting it to; things built by patient people did not fall, they faded, and this one was fading the way she had predicted twenty years ago at a kitchen table — not because anyone had defeated it, but because, year on year, a few more people in Memoria and Axiom and Synthesis kept a private copy of the days that mattered to them, just in case, the way you keep a candle though the lamp is working, until the day the one record became a thing everyone consulted and no one quite relied on, a formality, a grand clock in a square that people set their watches by out of habit and would not have trusted with an appointment. That was how an idea died, when it could not be killed: not in an afternoon, not at a stone, but in the slow accumulation of people quietly keeping their own. She had built the tools that made the keeping possible and she had made them open, so that the keeping belonged to no one, not even to her, and she counted the morning's discrepancies and let them stand, a dozen small disagreements about what had happened, carried, unresolved, by a world that had learned at terrible cost to prefer a dozen honest disagreements to one enforced peace.

In a university seminar room in the capital, Tamsin Chen, who was twenty-two, took a seat near the back.

She kept a journal, as her parents had taught her, its pages countersigned by strangers — she had met most of them once, at the signing, and some she had never met at all, had only exchanged pages with through the networks, so that her record of her own life was held, a little, in the hands of people whose faces she did not know, which her father said was the only safe way to hold anything. She had come to the first lecture of a seminar being taught that autumn for the first time, on the history of designed institutions, and she had not told her father she was coming, because she wanted to see the man for herself, once, first, the way a good witness sees a thing.

Thomas Wei was seventy-four. He was tall and grey and he had been released eight years ago, after twelve years, and he taught now, quietly, and he had a thin old scar across the back of his left hand that Tamsin noticed because she noticed everything, having been raised by two people who noticed everything. He wrote one sentence on the board, with his right hand, in a clear small hand, and turned to the room, and said nothing to introduce it. The sentence was:

Nobody designs everyone.

That was all Tamsin wrote down. The stranger in the seat beside her, when the lecture ended, countersigned it for her, initialled and dated it in the margin of her journal, a young man she had never met and would likely never meet again, and she thanked him, and he said it was nothing, and that was how a fact was kept now, by two people who did not know each other agreeing that a thing had been said.

At his desk, Isaiah Okonkwo, who was sixty-eight, filed his last column for the paper.

He gave it a prefix and a sequence number and the date, as he had given every piece for thirty years, and he set down, in order, with no verdict, the things that were true as of that morning: the stone in the plaza, and the monthly gathering that had kept going for twenty-eight years; the three nations that still ran the one record, and the three that did not; the Confederacy's noticeboards, and the hands that were not theirs; the seminar being taught that autumn by a man released eight years ago; and his aunt's name, Sarah Okonkwo, among the others, with no word after it to say whether she was living or dead, because he did not know, and would not pretend to, and because the name without a verdict was truer than any verdict he could have added.

His last line was a question he admitted, in the line itself, that he could not answer:

Did we break the cycle?

He did not know. He said so. Somebody should find out, he wrote, somebody with better years ahead of them than he had, and he filed it, and that was the last thing he ever filed for the paper.

At two o'clock, at the stone, Marcus Chen stood at the back of the crowd with Kira beside him.

He was fifty-eight. The monthly gathering had kept going for twenty-eight years, and it would keep going after him, which was the point of it. The names on the side of the stone were weathering now, softening at the edges in the lake wind, Alexei Volkov and Nora Chen and Mikhail Petrov among the thousands, and that was right too, that stone weathered, that a record kept by rain and people was a record that aged like the people who kept it.

Children were at the stone, who did not know who had written the song, and they sang his round, Many Hands, the one he had made in his seventies, the first new thing he had made in forty years:

One hand writes and one hand checks, one hand holds the light; one voice fails and one voice mends, and many make it right.

One child started it. A second came in on top of the first, a phrase behind. Then a third, so that the round turned on itself and held itself up, each phrase lifting at the end like a question and the next voice answering it by starting again at the beginning; and it could not be sung by one voice, had never been able to be sung by one voice, which was the whole of its meaning, built into the shape of it by a man who had understood that a thing one voice could carry was a thing one voice could lose.

Marcus had no fact entry for it. He had stopped keeping the lists some years ago, had put them in a drawer whole and not opened the drawer, because he had found at last that he did not need the scaffolding, that a man could carry his own days now without writing down who was in the room; and he stood at the back of the crowd with Kira's hand in his and listened to the children sing a dead man's round in voices that had never been his and were no longer the singer's, and he did not reach for a column to put it in, because it did not go in a column.

The round went on after the children had stopped listening to themselves, carried along by the next ones who picked it up without being taught, the way children pick up a round, turning it over and over in the cold bright afternoon under the weathering names.

There was never one truth to keep. There were only people, keeping it for each other.
