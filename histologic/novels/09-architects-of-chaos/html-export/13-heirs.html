Chapter 13: Heirs

Monday, May 13, 2115. The teaching hospital, Chronos lakeshore city

Subject: Tanaka, Y. Relation: great-niece of MT·06, Director Michiko Tanaka (the Technologist; the Implementation Annex). Deceased 2092. Case opened for the record kept by the subject, there being no other clinician to open it.

She wrote the case note at eight o'clock at the desk she shared with Morrison on the fourth floor, in the small italic hand she used for cases, because she did not have another hand for a thing like this and would not invent one. She had carried the hand copy of page thirty-nine in her case book for three weeks now, folded in behind the clinical tabs, and beside it, where it had lived since the siege, page nineteen of copy X, Principle Four, correction is compassion, certainty is care, the page she had read aloud in a linen room in 2110 to the people whose torture it described. Two pages of the design in a doctor's case book, between a tab for storm trauma and a tab for the ward's drug log. She had decided some time ago that this was the correct place for them. A case book was where you kept the things that were true and could not be fixed.

She remembered Great-Aunt Michiko exactly, because she remembered everything exactly; it was the gift and the sentence of her particular mind. Four times. Michiko had been a small, upright woman at the New Year table in the old house, her grandfather's elder sister, who had taken no interest in children as a class and a sharp interest in Yuki as a specimen. What will you be, she had asked the child, not unkindly, the way you ask a question whose answer is data. A doctor, the child had said. Good, Michiko had said, and turned back to the adults, and that Good had steered a life, Tanaka thought now, more than any encouragement she had ever been given warmly. And when Yuki had qualified, a card had come — Michiko did not attend things, she sent cards — with one line in a hand like wire: Minds are the only instruments that matter. Tanaka still had the card. She had framed it, once, as a young doctor, taking it for a humanist's creed.

She read it now against the Annex. Belief delivery at population scale. It was the same idea in the same hand, fifty years apart: that minds were the instruments, that a mind was a thing you played upon, that the only question was how many at once. The card and the clause were one woman's single thought, written first to a great-niece as a blessing and then to a nation as a specification. Kinship: established. Influence: unknown. She left the second line open, because she did not know whether the woman who had framed that card had been made by it, and she would not write a conclusion she had not earned.

Morrison came in at half past nine with the ward list, saw her face, and sat down without being asked, because he had worked beside her for years and knew her face the way she knew his hands.

She told him the way she told patients. "James," she said — she used his given name only under strain, and he would hear the strain in it — "there is a name on the page. One you'll want to have heard from me and not from a courier. May I?"

"Yes," he said.

"JM dot oh-eight," she said. "Colonel Jerome Morrison. He wrote Principle Five. The calibration grounds. Border regions of broadcast overlap should be preserved, not resolved: they are where a population's tolerance for contradiction may be measured, and raised."

His hands shook.

She watched them do it, flat on the ward list on the desk, the fine tremor she had last seen in a linen room in the war, and she said nothing about them, because he had a rule about his hands and the rule was that if they shook it meant something, and the meaning was his to state. He stated it. "He was my grandfather," he said, low, his eyes on the desk. "My father didn't speak to him. Not once that I saw. I was told he was a hard man who'd done government work and we didn't discuss the government in that house." He turned his hands over and looked at the palms as though they belonged to someone else. "I thought it was politics. An ordinary family quarrel about politics."

"Was I prepared?" he said.

And then he listed it, in the low flat voice he used for faults, the way he had listed his failures in the war, with his eyes on the floor and nothing spared himself.

"I designed the gain calibration for the Continuity cradles," he said. "In Veridica, before Chronos, before any of this. The dosing curve for the chairs. That was Book — that was 2105. It was my work; I was proud of the elegance of it; it was used to take Marcus Chen's history out of him a curve at a time." He went on. "I recognised the same curve on a broadcast node in 2106, when we were chasing the lost hour, and I told myself it was convergent design, that there were only so many ways to do a thing. I recognised it again in the Chronos stabilisation crates in 2108 and told myself the same. I recognised it on the national network in 2110, under Reeves, when they ran the dose on the city, and I stopped being able to tell myself anything." He looked up at last. "Four times, over nine years, I met my own hand's work doing harm, and every single time it was serving Principle Five. My grandfather's page." A pause. "My first posting out of training was a calibration unit at the border facility. It was offered to me before I had applied for anything. I was a new graduate and a plum posting came to me unasked and I thought I was lucky." His hands were still now, with effort. "Did somebody put me there?"

"I don't know," Tanaka said. "I can't know that, and I won't guess it, because you'd carry my guess as a fact for the rest of your life, and it isn't a fact. I don't know."

He nodded. He had asked her precisely because she would answer that way.

She did her rounds, because the ward did not stop for the design. There were storm-trauma patients still, fewer every year now that there were no Judges in Veridica or Chronos to run the overlaps, the long tail of a damage that was no longer being made: an old man who still came apart when two memories of the same day met in him, a young woman relearning which of her childhoods had happened. The work steadied her, the way the work had always steadied her. You could not hold a dying thought about a dead director while you were titrating a sedative for a man whose own mind was arguing with itself. The ward was a mercy that way. It kept you in the one hour you were actually in.

At noon Morrison handed her a letter he had received in March and never answered. It was on heavy cream paper, under the letterhead of the Liu Wei Endowment, a foundation in Synthesis, and it invited Dr. James Morrison to a funded fellowship on the reconciliation of national records, generous terms, no strings that the letter admitted to. She read it to the foot, where the founder's name was printed small: Chairman Liu Wei.

"LW dot oh-three," she said. "The Financier. The man no one at the table could place. He wrote no principle. He paid." She turned the letter in her hand. "He's been dead since 2099, James, and his foundation is writing letters about the reconciliation of national records in 2115." She thought about it the way she thought about a hereditary condition that skipped the body and ran in the institution instead. Heir: possibly not a person. An endowment did not die, did not confess, did not flee east; it funded, patiently, for a century, whatever its dead founder had wanted funded, and it had just invited one of the design's own grandchildren to come and work on the one project the design most wanted worked on. She filed the letter unlabelled, in her case book, in the place where she kept the things that were true and did not yet have a name.

She had spent her working life at the bedsides of people whose minds had been altered against their will, and she had developed, as a result, a clinician's horror of determinism, of any account of a person that left the person out. The design was the most elegant determinism she had ever met: a clause, a bloodline, a schedule, a hundred-year foundation, all of it bending toward an outcome decided in a room before any of the people it decided for were born. And her whole profession's answer to it — the answer she had staked her life and her book on — was the smallest and least impressive answer imaginable, which was that the determinism was never quite complete; that you could arrange a boy's posting and his blood and the dosing curve he would one day recognise, and you could not arrange the moment he recognised it and was sick with it and refused; that the cradle could be built and the lying-down could not be commanded. It was not a triumphant answer. It did not undo the harm; the harm was real; Morrison's hands shook, and Marcus's prose had holes, and her own temple carried a scar she touched without knowing. But it was the true answer, and she had learned to prefer true small answers to false large ones, and she meant to write it, in the end, in the only register she trusted, which was the register of a case note that did not flinch and did not console.

At four o'clock they went to the linen room, because the linen room was where she went with a thing that had to be read, and because it was where she had read Principle Four aloud in the war, to Isaiah and the others, and the room had held it then and would hold this. She laid page nineteen of copy X on the shelf — correction is compassion, certainty is care — and page thirty-nine beside it, and she and Morrison read the Register through once more together, quietly, and then she turned to the clause at the foot of the Annex that was the whole of the day's question.

Each contributor shall prepare an heir.

Her question about it was clinical, and she put it to Morrison as she would put a differential to a colleague. "Does a clause make an heir," she said, "or does it only make the conditions for one? Because those are different findings, and the treatment is different." She answered it herself, with Kira's old rule, the rule the escaped subjects had lived by in Ashford, that Kira had got from R-2 and handed down: that the conditions could be arranged — the posting, the card, the blood, the elegant dosing curve a boy would grow up to recognise — but that the choice could not. You could build the cradle and you could not make the man lie down in it. The design had understood this perfectly, which was why it had not written each contributor shall produce an heir, a thing you cannot command, but shall prepare an heir, which is only the arranging of conditions, the thing you can. It had prepared. Preparation was all it could do. The rest was the person.

She wrote one more thing in the case book, about herself, because an honest clinician includes herself in the series when she belongs in it. In 2105 Reeves had asked her to run the Continuity trials, and she had refused. She wrote the fact down. She did not call it virtue; she did not know whether it had been virtue or squeamishness or luck, and a case note was not the place to award oneself motives. She wrote only: Subject's clinician was offered a role in the preparation of harm in 2105 and declined. Reason not established. Noted for completeness.

Late in the afternoon the light went out of the window over the lake, and Morrison sat across the shared desk with his hands flat on it to still them, and said the thing he had come round to all day.

"He never met me," he said. "He died when I was fourteen. My father wouldn't have him in the house, and then he was dead, and I never once sat in a room with him. Whatever he prepared, he prepared on paper, from a distance, for a boy he never saw."

"Then the clause failed with you," Tanaka said.

He looked at her for a moment, and his hands, under his flat palms, were steady.

That night, in her flat, with the lake dark below the window, she opened the case book and wrote the last line of the day's note, in the small italic hand, and underlined nothing, because the thing did not need underlining:

Case note: heir, declined.
