# Chapter 4: Carver Street

**POV:** Isaiah Okonkwo
**Date:** Fri Mar 29, 2115 (morning to about 01:00 on Sat Mar 30)
**Setting:** Isaiah's flat in the capital; a journal-network hall in the old eastern quarter (an evening reading); the parlour of his memory palace
**Length:** ~3,400 words

## Purpose
He has become the witness his country trusts. Then the one person in his memory who never speaks writes to him. She doesn't apologise and doesn't explain. She invites him to tea, in a house that shouldn't exist.

## Beats
- **Opening, filed:** *IO-2115-088. 29 MARCH. Flat. Post: one letter, hand-addressed.* He hasn't opened it yet. He knows the hand, a diplomat's hand, sharp and elegant, and he makes coffee first because he isn't ready.
- **Where he stands.** He is 48, legally alive, and lives in Veridica again. ***The Cost of Certainty***, written with Tanaka, came out in 2112. His words and her case notes made him the most trusted byline in a country that now trusts witnesses. Strangers stop him at tram stops to tell him what they remember, and he lets them. He still files everything with a prefix, a sequence number and a date. He still hears belief in the way people say words.
- **The letter, from Aunt Sarah** (about 80, Ambassador, retired). It is one sheet of heavy cream paper in her hand, and every line is exactly as long as it needs to be. It says:
  - that she has read his book twice;
  - that she is old and would like to see him;
  - ***"Come to tea at Carver Street on Monday the twenty-ninth of April, at four. The house is where it always was."***

  It is signed *Sarah*. It carries no apology for 2105, when he was arrested and she didn't call, and nothing about the twenty years since. He reads it three times, the way he reads a source.
- **The problem, filed in his editorial voice.** The house on Carver Street doesn't exist. The old eastern quarter was cleared and rebuilt after he stopped going there. He has said so to himself for twenty years, in a white chair and out of one. He never went back to see it cleared; he took it on the record's word. So either the record he believed was wrong, or she has built it again. He files it UNCHECKED: *If I were a guessing man. Which, professionally, I am not.* He decides **not** to walk past it before the day. He wants to see it the way a witness should, once and first.
- **Singh.** Tanaka called him on Mar 18. On Thursday the circle will hear a dead woman at the farmhouse (Ch 6), and he'll go. He notes that two people have come back in one month: a woman nobody has seen for nine years, and an aunt he hasn't seen for twenty. He doesn't make a pattern of two. He waits for three.
- **Evening, the reading.** It is a journal-network hall in the rebuilt eastern quarter, a few streets from where Carver Street was, or is. A few hundred people come. He reads a chapter of *The Cost of Certainty* and answers questions, and ends as his pieces always end, on a question he can't answer. A woman asks him about his family. He says, courteously, "I had an aunt." He hears the tense after he's said it.
- **The street sign.** On the walk to the tram he passes the end of a new street. The sign reads *Carver Street*. He doesn't turn into it.
- **Night, the palace.** He lies down with his shoes on and opens the house room by room: the kitchen with its sources, the study filed by year, and the cellar door, whose colour he lost in Unit 3. Then the **parlour**. Since he refurnished it in 2108, it has held the harmless, true, warm things again: pepper soup, his first byline framed above the fireplace. **Aunt Sarah** sits at the head of the table, sharp and elegant, as she has every night since then. She has never spoken there, and the place in front of her has always been bare.

## Ending
Tonight there is a cup in front of her. It is a teacup, her good china, set on its saucer with the handle turned toward her hand. He didn't put it there.
