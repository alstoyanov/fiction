# Chapter 28: The Signature

*Saturday, January 8, 2107. Ministry of Historical Integrity, Veridica Capital*

---

There were four chandeliers in the long hall on the second floor of the Ministry, each one a column of plain glass rods lit from inside, and Kira Osman had counted the rods in the nearest one twice before the Minister began to speak.

Two hundred and sixteen, both times. It was a small, clean satisfaction, a number that held.

She did not look at the ceiling for the rest of it. Ten floors above her head was an office with a side door and a vase of flowers that someone had presumably stopped replacing. Below her feet, in the basement, was the public archive annex, with its rows of terminals and its schoolchildren and its guard who wandered past twice. She had spent an hour of her life down there that nobody could prove. She had walked in through the front doors at seven o'clock under her own name, and a young man had taken her coat and said, "Good evening, Ms. Osman," as though the name were an ordinary one.

*Kira Osman. January eighth, 2107. Nineteen forty. Ministry of Historical Integrity, second floor. Status: living. Guest.*

The hall held about a hundred and forty people: Ministry officials in dark suits, magistrates, press behind a rope at the back, and the new commissioners in a loose group near the dais, because nobody had told them where else to stand. There were cameras in the corners, the usual Ministry domes, and she had noted their arcs coming in. Nobody else seemed to have looked for them. She had not got used to that yet: rooms full of people who did not look for the cameras.

Marcus stood beside her in the suit Kovač had found for him, which was slightly too big across the shoulders. He had his glasses on properly for once. He had been quiet since the car.

---

Minister Chen Zhao did not use notes.

He stood at the front of the dais with a glass of tea in his hand while everyone else held wine, and he spoke the way Kira imagined a favourite teacher would speak at a prize-giving: warmly, without hurry, pausing to let people laugh. He was silver-haired and not tall, and he had a way of looking at the room as though every face in it had done him a small personal kindness.

"A record," he said, "is only as honest as the people who keep it. For forty years we have told ourselves that we needed no people. That the machine would keep us honest." He smiled. "This autumn, the machine lost an hour, and we found out how much we needed people after all. So tonight I have found some." He lifted his glass an inch toward the group by the dais. "Several of them, I am told, were dead until last month. It seems to have done wonders for their character."

The room laughed. Kira did not. Beside her, Marcus's mouth moved very slightly at one corner.

The Minister read the names himself. A retired magistrate. A deputy registrar from the Correction Registry. A senior historian from the Spire. A professor of records law, who bowed. Then:

"Ms. Kira Osman, Senior Architect." A small warm pause. "Who helped build the Spire, and was then obliged to see it from the other side. There is no better education." Applause. "And Mr. Marcus Chen." The Minister turned and looked at Marcus directly, and his face creased with pleasure. "My cousin. No, truly. The Chen families of this city are all related somewhere, Mr. Chen. I'm afraid you'll simply have to put up with me."

More laughter. Marcus inclined his head, the careful way he did everything now, and said, "Minister," and the room applauded him for longer than for anyone else, and Kira watched him stand in it the way he had once stood in the atrium of Wing C: very still, taking up as little space as possible, waiting for it to be over.

"And the Commission will need a technical director," said the Minister. "Someone who knows the factorepo's architecture better than it knows itself. Someone who led the investigation into the lost hour, and did not let the easy answer stand. I could think of only one name." He turned, and held out his hand. "Director Wei."

Thomas Wei came up the two low steps to the dais and shook the Minister's hand.

He looked well: a tall man in a dark suit, gray at the temples, with the easy, attentive expression of someone genuinely glad to be where he was. The applause for him ran warmest through the Ministry people. He acknowledged it with a small lift of his glass, in his right hand, and then, when the Minister said something to him privately, laughed and lifted the other hand in a little gesture of protest.

The bandage was gone. Across the back of his left hand, where the dressing had been since November, ran a thin pink line, clean and healed, the colour new skin is for a few weeks before it fades.

Kira looked at it for exactly as long as she would have looked at anything else on the dais, and then she looked at the Minister again.

*Healed,* she thought. That was all she thought. She made sure of it.

---

Maya Reeves was standing at the side of the hall near the windows, in a dark green dress that she kept adjusting at the waist as though it had been borrowed, which it had been. Her badge said *M. REEVES, COMMISSION TECHNICAL STAFF*. Nikolai stood half a metre from her with his own face on, shaved and pale, in a jacket that fitted him for once. They were not holding hands or looking at each other, and they were so obviously together that two Ministry wives had already exchanged a glance about it.

Kira went over when the speeches were done.

"Congratulations," said Maya. "Commissioner."

"You too."

"I'm a junior technician. Again." Maya's mouth twisted. "Different letterhead."

Ten days ago her father had been convicted of murder on his own confession. Since then Maya had gone to work every morning for the man who had built the case, and tonight she had put on a borrowed dress and come to his party. Kira thought it was the kind of courage that did not feel like anything from the inside.

"How are you?" she said.

"Fine," said Maya, in a voice that meant she would not be asked again, and Nikolai, without looking at her, moved a quarter of a step closer.

---

Wei found her at a quarter to nine, by the long table with the untouched food.

"Ms. Osman." He had a glass of water now, in his right hand. "Before the Minister takes everyone off to be photographed. It's work, I'm afraid. On a Saturday."

"Go on."

"The case files. The lost hour, all of it, from the fifteenth to the ruling. The forty statements, the tribunal, the medical wing, the hearing transcripts, my own reports." He said it simply. "They have to come into the Commission archive before Friday's session. Someone has to check every file as it goes in. It's dull, careful work, and everything the Commission does afterwards will stand on it." He smiled slightly. "I'd like it to be you. Who better?"

She looked at him.

"You built half the verification layer they'll be going into," he said. "And you've been reading these files, one way or another, since November. I'd rather the first thing the Commission holds was held by someone who'd notice if it was wrong."

It was, she thought, exactly what she would have said, if she had been him. It was what anyone would have said.

"When?" she said.

"Whenever you like. Monday."

"Tonight," said Kira.

He laughed, not unkindly. "I thought you might." He took a card from his inside pocket and held it out: a plain white access card, *COMMISSION ARCHIVE, B2*. "It's the new room below the annex. They finished it on Thursday. It still smells of paint." A small pause. "There's coffee. Black, no sugar. I asked."

She took the card.

"Don't stay all night," he said, and went back to the Minister.

---

She took the service stairs.

They ran down the east side of the building from the twelfth floor to the basement, concrete and handrail and a camera at every landing but not at the turns, and she had come down them on the fifteenth of November with Marcus beside her, checking every step aloud. Nine steps to the turn. Nine steps to the landing. She counted them again now, going down, and came out in the basement corridor outside the public annex.

The annex was dark. Its glass doors were locked for the night. Through them she could see the rows of public terminals under their covers, and the stretch of floor where a school party had sat, and the booth where a child had once asked Marcus what he was typing. She did not look for the terminal they had used. She knew where it was without looking, the way you know where a step is in your own house.

The new stairs went down one more level. At the bottom was a white door with a new lock, and she put the card to it, and it opened.

The Commission archive was a long, white, windowless room with a low ceiling and the faint sweetish smell of fresh paint. Two racks of storage along the far wall, their lights steady green. One long desk with two terminals. A camera in the corner above the door, its arc covering the desk. And on the desk, beside the nearer terminal, a white cup of coffee under a lid, still hot.

She did not drink it. She moved it to the far end of the desk, sat down, and began.

---

The first batch was three hundred and twelve files. There would be more on Monday, and every day that week.

She did it the way she had been trained to do it at the Spire, the way she had written into the standards herself: one file at a time, open the structure before the content, check the internal arrangement against the declared format, check the hash chain, check the first-save record against the current one, log the result, admit the file, move on. It was the most boring procedure in the architecture. That was its purpose. *Don't make anything impregnable. Make it boring.* Anyone watching the camera feed would see a woman working late.

The forty statements went in first. Then the tribunal record of December sixth, with its eighty-eight per cent. Then the autopsy file, which she did not read; Tanaka had read it. Then Wei's case presentation of the seventeenth, which was elegant, and complete, exactly as Dmitri had said. Then the hearing transcripts, day by day, including the afternoon when the lead investigator had been asked, for completeness, to state his own movements, and had presented thirty-six statements from the colleagues in his office. She admitted them one after another. The structure was clean on every one.

It was past eleven when she reached the medical wing.

*STATEMENT RECORD. REEVES (SUBJECT). 20 DECEMBER 2106. MINISTRY MEDICAL WING, ROOM 4. VOLUNTARY. WITNESSED.*

She opened the structure.

The declared format was the medical wing's standard, the formatter every Ministry clinical record came out of, which laid a file down in the same fixed order every time: header, body, index, seals. She had seen a thousand of them.

This one had been laid down in the other order.

It had been made to look standard. Every field was where the format said it should be, and anyone checking the finished file would have found nothing. But the first-save record showed how it had been built, and it had not been built by the formatter. The formatter sealed a file forward, block after block, from the header to the end. This file had been sealed from the far end, *backwards*, block by block toward the header, like a seam sewn from the inside. And where the author field had been rewritten after the first save, the empty marker left in the index to record the change had been laid down not in the order the field was written but in the order it was removed.

Kira sat very still.

She had seen that seam before. She had seen it in the cellar under the feed merchant's shop in November, in the raw architecture of the hole that Nikolai had sent her from inside: an hour of a nation's history taken out so cleanly that nothing on either side of it disagreed, and re-sealed from the far edge, backwards, with the tombstones in the order of removal. She had found it again afterwards, going back through Nikolai's partition, in the adjustment that had turned a fifty-eight into a seventy-three on the file of the man upstairs in a suit too big across the shoulders. And she had seen it first, without knowing what she was seeing, at her station on the sixtieth floor of the Spire in the winter of 2105, staring at the place where Security Protocol Update 7.3 had been, three months of her work, entered by her own voice at twenty-two hundred on the eighteenth, and finding no scar at all. Only a quirk. The blocks after her fact re-sealed before the blocks before it.

*Layer one,* said her mouth. *Key exchange. Asymmetric, lattice-based. Rotation interval ninety seconds.*

She stopped it. She put her hands flat on the desk on either side of the keyboard and made herself stop, with her jaw shut, and the camera in the corner saw a tired woman resting her hands.

*Same hand.*

Not the same software. Not the same tool. The same *hand*: the same habit, the same backwards stitch, the same knot tied the same way every time without thinking. It had deleted her fact. It had typed Marcus's seventy-three. It had erased the hour. And it had written this file, the record of a man's sworn confession, not merely the memory he had sworn to, but the document that carried it into law.

*Ask yourself why he needed my code comment, Ms. Osman. I've never signed anything in my life.*

She understood it now, all at once, the way a failed build suddenly shows you the one wrong line. *ut apes. bee back in an hour.* A signature, put on a man who never signed, by someone who did. Someone who signed everything he made, not with a name but with the way he made it, and who had needed, just once, to sign something with somebody else's.

---

The author field of the confession record said *MEDICAL WING RECORDS (STANDARD)*. It had been scrubbed. She could see that it had been scrubbed: the field had been rewritten after the first save, cleanly, with the proper authority.

But the Ministry's document store kept its own shadow of every file's first save, for integrity, in a cache that the ingest procedure read as a matter of course. She had written that requirement into the standard herself, on page nineteen. She doubted anyone else in the building had ever read page nineteen.

She ran the ingest on the file, exactly as she had run it on every file before it.

The procedure pulled the first-save shadow and set it beside the current record, field by field, and flagged the one field that differed, as it was designed to, in the same neutral yellow it used for a changed timestamp.

*AUTHOR (FIRST SAVE): T. WEI.*
*SAVED: 20 DECEMBER 2106, 16:52.*

She looked at it for four seconds.

Then she logged the discrepancy as the standard required, *author field amended after first save; amendment authorised*, admitted the file, and opened the next one. She did nine more at the same pace. Only then did she let herself think.

*A lead investigator creating the record of a confession he witnessed is not a crime.* She made herself think that first, because it was what anyone would say. *An author field amended with the proper authority is not a crime.* Two ordinary facts.

But the man who had created the record had built it with the hand that deleted her fact, and then he had taken his name off it.

She knew what it was worth. A signature that only she could read, which a hostile engineer would call a style, and a hostile court would call her imagination. A cache entry that could be called forged by anyone who had the authority to forge it, which was him. She had a name. She did not have a proof.

And against the name, a hearing chamber in December, and thirty-six people who remembered him at his desk in the Ministry district from half past one until the Ministry's call at twenty past three. At his desk, during the hour. Not in Singh's office. Not at the side entrance at 14:21 with a hurt hand held away from his body.

*Unless.*

She took out her comm and wrote one line to Nikolai, in the old shift.

*SEND M. DOWN. ALONE. B2.*

---

Maya came eleven minutes later, holding her borrowed dress up off the concrete with one hand.

"What's wrong?" she said at the door. "Kolya said—"

"Not in here." Kira stood. "Come with me."

She took her back up the new stairs to the corridor outside the dark annex, and along it to the service stair, and up nine steps to the turn, where the landing camera above them could see their feet and not their faces. Maya had the sense not to ask why. She stood with her back to the wall, and Kira saw her look at the camera's arc, once, and understand it.

"One question," said Kira. "It's going to sound strange."

"Everything since November sounds strange."

"On November fifteenth. When you found the gap. Where was Wei?"

Maya frowned. "He wasn't there," she said. "He wasn't anywhere. It was just us on the floor until the Ministry called. The notice came up at 15:20. It appointed him, and he *arrived* at 15:32." She said it without having to think, the way she said everything with a time in it. "Twelve minutes. I remember because I timed it. I time everything. I thought he must have already been on his way."

"His alibi," said Kira, "says he was at his desk. In his office in the Ministry district. From 13:30 until the Ministry's call at 15:20."

Maya opened her mouth, and shut it.

"That's twenty-five minutes away," she said slowly. "At the best of times. On the fifteenth, with every screen in the city going off and the transit lines on manual—" She stopped. "He couldn't have been at his desk. Nobody could." Kira watched her do the next step herself, and saw it land. "And they all remember him there. Thirty-six of them. All swearing." Her voice went very quiet. "And every one of them was inside the seeded district."

"Yes," said Kira.

"The alibi was written," said Maya. "It was written too. The same way. The same as—"

She stopped, and Kira watched her arrive at the rest of it, the part that was not about Wei at all.

"The comment," Maya said. "*ut apes.* He gave me the node logs himself, and said take your time, and I found my father's joke in them, and I—" Her face was doing two things at once, crumpling and hardening, and neither of them won. "I *wrote* to him. I told him I knew it was him. And he wrote back and said it wasn't, and I didn't—" Her hand went flat against the concrete. "He's in prison. He's in prison for it. He *remembers* doing it."

"I know."

"You have to—"

"Not tonight," said Kira. "Maya. Listen to me. Not tonight, and not here." She kept her voice exactly level, the way she kept it in rooms where the machine was listening. "What we have is your memory of a time, and my reading of a file. That's all. If we say it in this building tonight, it will be his word against ours by midnight, and his word has thirty-six witnesses."

Maya stared at her, eyes wet and furious. Then, very slowly, she nodded.

"Go back up," said Kira. "Stand next to Nikolai. If anyone asks, I wanted your help with the ingest on Monday."

Maya pushed herself off the wall. At the top of the flight she stopped, and looked down, and said, in a voice gone suddenly young: "He picked me. The first day. *You found the gap first. Walk me through it.*" A breath. "I was so pleased."

Then she went.

---

Kira stood alone on the turn of the stair with the camera watching her feet.

She thought of forty statements.

She had read them in November the way she read logs, for the pattern: every one wrong about the face and the clothes in exactly the same way, because the face and the clothes had been written into them. And every one of them had kept one thing that had not been written. The wording changed from statement to statement. The detail did not. *White bandage. Wrapped hand. Holding it like it hurt.* A peripheral thing. The kind of thing the seeding did not trouble to overwrite, because it was small, and nobody would ever think it mattered.

It was the only true thing left in forty seeded memories.

*The killer had a bandaged left hand.*

She thought of a hand holding an office door open for her in Reeves's annex in December, a bandage across the back of it. A hand resting on the edge of a bunk in a detention cell, beside a paper cup of tea, in the story Marcus had told her. A hand lifted in a little rueful gesture of protest on a dais an hour ago, with a thin pink line across it where something had healed.

*Lots of people cut their hands,* she had told herself, in November, in December, deleting a name from a note. *He's protecting Marcus.*

She went back down to B2 and finished the batch, eleven files, each one checked and admitted. Then she copied the confession record and its first-save shadow and the discrepancy log to a tablet, and put the tablet under her arm, and turned off the terminal, and left the coffee where it was, cold, at the far end of the desk.

---

She climbed the service stairs slowly, counting.

Nine to the turn. Nine to the landing. On the second floor the reception came through the fire door: a hundred and forty people talking, and glass, and somebody's laugh.

She pushed the door open.

Wei was at the top of the main stairs, a few metres along the corridor, as though he had just come out of the hall to look for someone. He had a glass of wine in each hand. When he saw her he smiled, the whole of his face warming with it, a man who was genuinely pleased to find what he had come looking for.

"Kira," he said. "There you are." He held out one of the glasses to her with his left hand, the one with the scar. "Come. The Minister wants a photograph of the restorers."

She took the glass.

Her hand closed round the stem where his fingers had been a second before, and she held it at the angle you hold a glass for a photograph, and she smiled at him.

It was a good smile. She had learned it in 7-K, on the mornings after the machine had spent a session looking for Adrian, when she had to walk out past the orderlies with a calm, grateful, healing face. She had not used it since August. It came back at once, whole, as if it had only been waiting.

The wine did not move in the glass. Her hand did not shake.

She walked beside him back into the light of two hundred and sixteen glass rods, with the tablet under her arm, to stand next to Marcus in front of the cameras. It was the hardest thing she had done since 7-K.
