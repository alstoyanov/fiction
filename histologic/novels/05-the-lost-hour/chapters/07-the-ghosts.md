# Chapter 7: The Ghosts

*Monday, November 15 – Wednesday, November 17, 2106. The Ministry district, Veridica Capital, and the resistance barn, the border zone*

---

At fourteen fifteen, Alexei Volkov drove a dead man into the Ministry district in a truck full of soup.

The truck belonged to a catering supplier who owed Elena a favour. It was white and clean, with the supplier's name on its sides in a friendly green script and a Ministry delivery pass on the windscreen, and in the back, on steel racks, stood forty sealed containers of lentil soup for the canteen of the Office of Records Standards. The soup was real. The pass was real. The manifest was real. The only false thing in the whole vehicle was the driver, who had been officially dead since the second of August and was enjoying himself enormously.

"You know what I like about this?" he said.

Beside him, in the passenger seat, in a supplier's green jacket two sizes too large for him, Old Songs went on humming.

"Nobody looks for a dead man," said Alexei. "They look for outlaws. They look for people whose telemetry's gone funny. But a man whose telemetry reads zero because he's *dead*? He's not a problem. He's a filing matter." He turned onto the boulevard. The Ministry of Historical Integrity rose ahead of them in its long pale terraces, glass and stone, floor upon floor of it. "We're not even in the system. We're in the *archive*."

Old Songs hummed. It was the Lakeshore Air, the tune Alexei had heard three times a day for eight months through the meal slot of a glass cell, the tune his mother had hummed at the sink when he was small. Every phrase lifted at the end like a question. The old man had been humming it since they left the barn, steadily, the way other people breathed, and Alexei had stopped noticing it the way you stop noticing your own heartbeat, and then noticed it again every time it changed.

There were cameras everywhere in the Ministry district. Dmitri had counted them, of course: four hundred and twelve on the approach routes, their arcs and periods written out on a sheet of paper in his square, careful hand. Alexei drove like a catering man, bored and punctual, one elbow on the sill, and let the cameras see exactly what they expected.

"Roof," he said, to the air. The radio was clipped under the dashboard, short-range and encrypted, Nikolai's design. "Truck's in. Loading bay in four."

"*Received.*" Dmitri's voice, flat and very clear. "*Side entrance quiet. Two staff out for air. Nobody of interest.*"

---

Dmitri had been on the roof since twenty to two.

He was across the street, on the flat roof of a five-storey office building, with a maintenance contractor's permit, a clipboard, and a view of the side entrance that served the Ministry's east lifts, about forty metres from its door. He had been checking the same ventilation housing for three-quarters of an hour, and Alexei knew without seeing him that he would not have moved except to turn a page.

He could feel him, too. That was the other thing. Alexei could always feel him. Dmitri was a steady, cold weight at the edge of his mind, north-east of the truck and above it, like a hand resting on a table in a dark room. And further off, south and west, across most of the capital, a second weight, smaller and tighter, the way Nikolai always felt when he was concentrating: at his desk in the Regional Factorepo, pretending to be a man called Pieter Lang, eating noodles.

*Here*, the bond said. *Here. Here.* It was not words. It had never been words.

He brought the truck round into the service street behind the canteen and backed it into the loading bay, beeping, and a kitchen porter in a white apron came out and waved him in without looking at his face.

The job was simple, the way the best jobs were. Two people, four years under. Marina Holt was an archivist in the Ministry. Jakob Lind was a dispatcher in the Enforcement annex three streets away, the man who read the patrol assignments out to the cars. Between them, over four years, they had kept Elena's people alive more often than Alexei liked to count. And last Tuesday Elena's source had sent her a single line: *their covers will not survive the week*.

So today they were going to lunch, and not coming back.

"Marina at fourteen twenty-five, side entrance," said Alexei, mostly to himself. "Jakob at fourteen thirty-two, corner of the annex. Out by fourteen forty-five. Home for tea." He glanced at Old Songs. "You're very welcome to join in with the planning at any point."

Old Songs, eyes half closed, hummed a phrase that went down at the end.

"Fair enough," said Alexei.

---

At fourteen twenty-one the radio clicked.

"*A man.*" Dmitri's voice, level, as though he were reading a licence plate into a report. "*Dark jacket. Staff card. Side entrance.*" A pause. "*Not one of ours.*"

Alexei had the porter's clipboard on his knee, signing for forty containers of lentil soup. He did not stop signing.

"Security?"

"*No. Staff. He used a card at the door.*"

"Then he's staff. It's a Ministry. It's full of them." Alexei handed the clipboard back to the porter with a grin, and the porter went inside, and he said, quietly: "Ignore it."

There was a silence on the radio.

It went on for slightly too long. He felt it through the bond before he heard anything: a small, peculiar attentiveness in the cold weight on the roof, the way Dima had gone still as a boy when he saw something in the lake he could not name.

"*…He walks well,*" said Dmitri.

Alexei frowned at the radio. "What's that supposed to mean?"

"*Nothing.*" Another pause. "*He has gone in. Side entrance clear.*"

Beside him, Old Songs had not stopped humming. But the tune had changed very slightly. It had gone back to the beginning of the phrase, and started again, the way a man goes back to the top of a page he has not quite understood.

---

Marina Holt came out of the side entrance at fourteen twenty-five exactly.

Alexei did not see her do it. He saw her two minutes later, walking round the corner of the service street with a cardboard archive box in her arms and her coat open, a woman of about forty with short fair hair and fine lines round her eyes, going to lunch. She was not hurrying. She nodded to the kitchen porter. She stepped up into the back of the truck between the soup racks as though she did it every day, and Alexei heard her sit down on the floor, and then heard her breathe out, once, very long.

"Hello, Marina," he said, without turning round. "I'm Alexei. You may have seen me on some posters."

"You're dead," said Marina Holt, from the floor.

"So are you, nearly. How was work?"

"Quiet." Her voice shook very slightly, and then stopped shaking. "Nobody looked up." She shifted the box. "I brought what I could carry without looking like I was carrying anything. It's this week's paper intake. Nobody reads it."

"Perfect," said Alexei. "Nobody reads anything. That's the whole secret of the civil service."

He pulled out of the loading bay at fourteen twenty-seven, and drove, bored and punctual, three streets west towards the Enforcement annex.

---

Jakob Lind was late.

At fourteen thirty-two the corner by the annex was empty. At fourteen thirty-three it was still empty. At fourteen thirty-four a patrol car came slowly down the street from the north, two officers inside, one of them eating something, and slowed as it passed the parked catering truck, and Alexei, who had drawn up with his hazard lights on and a delivery tablet in his hand, looked up at it with the flat, faintly resentful expression of a man who has been told he cannot park anywhere in the entire capital.

The patrol car went on.

"*Enforcement vehicles,*" said Dmitri on the radio, "*two, stationary, annex forecourt. One mobile, heading west on your street. It has passed you.*"

"I noticed," said Alexei.

He found that his hands were very tight on the tablet. In the back, Marina had gone perfectly silent. Old Songs had stopped humming.

That was the worst of it. For three minutes, the old man said nothing at all, and Alexei, who had spent eight months in Wing C learning what the old man's silences meant, sat in the cab of a truck full of soup with his heart going like a drum, because silence from Old Songs had meant only one thing in Wing C, and it was *they're watching*.

*Here*, said the weight on the roof. Cold. Steady. *Here.*

At fourteen thirty-five and forty seconds by the dashboard clock, a thin man with receding hair and a dispatcher's lanyard came round the corner from the annex at a pace that was not quite a run, pulled open the passenger door because nobody had told him not to, saw Old Songs, said "Oh," and climbed into the back instead.

"Sorry," said Jakob Lind, breathless, among the soup. "Sorry. My supervisor stopped me. He wanted to talk about next month's rota."

"And what did you tell him?" said Alexei, pulling out.

"I said I'd think about it."

Alexei laughed. It came out of him all at once, too loud, the way it always did when the fear let go. Beside him, very softly, Old Songs began to hum again.

---

At fourteen thirty-eight, on the boulevard, something touched him.

It was not a storm. He knew storms; he had driven through a hundred of them, and a storm was two worlds shouting at you at once. This was nothing like that. Nothing flickered. Nothing contradicted. The boulevard was the boulevard and the Ministry was the Ministry, pale and long in the gray afternoon. It was only that, for a moment, the street seemed very slightly too bright, the way a room seems too bright when you have just woken up in it, and he had the sensation of a word on the tip of his tongue that he had never known.

It was faint. It was barely there. It was there in the bond as well, as if it had brushed all three of them at once and set the weights very slightly swinging.

Except that the weight on the roof had not swung. Or had hardly swung. Dmitri felt, through the whole of it, exactly as he had felt all afternoon: cold and still and there.

"Dima," said Alexei. "Did you—"

"*Yes.*" A pause. "*Faintly. Less than you, I think.*"

"What was it?"

"*I do not know.*" Another pause. "*It was not a storm.*"

Alexei looked sideways at Old Songs. The old man was humming with his eyes shut, steadily, the tune going round and round, not louder and not softer, the way a man walks the same route through a house in the dark so as not to trip on anything.

"Are you all right?" said Alexei.

Old Songs opened his eyes and looked at him with his pale, far-off look, and did not answer, and went on humming.

---

They collected Dmitri from the rear door of his building at fourteen forty, a man in coveralls with a clipboard getting into a catering truck, which was the least interesting thing that had happened in the Ministry district all afternoon. At fourteen forty-three they passed the district boundary, where the pale terraces gave way to the ordinary brown and glass of the capital, and Alexei felt his shoulders come down from round his ears for the first time since two o'clock.

"Clean," said Dmitri.

"Clean," said Alexei. "Home for tea."

In the back, Marina Holt put her head in her hands. Jakob Lind was saying something to her, very low. And Old Songs, in the passenger seat, stopped the tune he had been humming for two hours and hummed instead a single short phrase that rose at the end.

*Yes*, it meant, in Wing C. It had meant *yes* through a hundred meal slots. Alexei had not heard it since August. He found that he had to look out of the side window for a while.

At a minute past three, on the ring road, every screen in every shopfront they passed lit up at once with the same red alert, and the radio on the dashboard, which had been playing public music, broke off in the middle of a bar.

---

On Tuesday afternoon they were ghosts.

That was what Elena called them, standing at the head of the table in the barn with the map spread out under her elbows. The barn smelled of diesel and hay, and it had been the resistance's command post since September. The news came in on three screens at once. Elena watched all of them and swore at each in turn, in a low, steady, inventive voice that Alexei found very soothing.

"*Two Ministry employees,*" said the first screen, "*were abducted from their posts yesterday afternoon by what the Ministry describes as an organised criminal network, believed to be operating with foreign support.*"

"*Believed to be operating,*" said Elena. "Believed by whom?"

"*The Ministry has lodged a formal protest with Chronos,*" said the second screen, "*citing the network's known use of the lakeshore strip—*"

"We haven't been near the lakeshore strip in a week."

"*Chronos has rejected the allegation as*"—the third screen showed a Chronos spokesman with a very neat collar—"*an attempt by Veridica to explain its own temporal failure by inventing a foreign hand.*"

"Well," said Elena, "he's not wrong."

Marina and Jakob sat side by side on a bale by the stove with blankets over their shoulders. Alexei had watched a great many people come out of a life. Most of them wept, or talked without stopping. These two just sat, very close together, like people waiting in a hospital corridor for news that was not about them.

The problem was simple, and Elena laid it out as she laid out everything, briskly, in the voice she used for briefings. There was no record that Marina and Jakob had left their posts. There was no record that they had walked out of their buildings, or where they had gone, or with whom. Their departures had been in the hour, and the hour did not exist. All the Ministry had was two employees at their desks at one fifty-nine and gone at three o'clock. And in Veridica, when the record had nothing to say, the Ministry said it instead.

"Then we answer," said Alexei. "Now. Tonight." He was on his feet; he had not noticed getting up. "Put Marina in front of a camera. *I walked out. Nobody took me. I went to lunch.* Let them choke on it."

"And then what?" said Elena.

"And then they know the Ministry's lying."

"And then they know a woman the Ministry has recorded as abducted is sitting in a resistance safe house talking to a camera, next to a man the Registry says has been dead since August, with a face every enforcer in the east knows from a wanted poster." Elena did not raise her voice. She never did. "And the day after that, Marcus Chen goes on trial for murder, and the prosecution asks how many other dead people the resistance has walking round the capital. No."

"Lena—"

"*No*, Lyosha."

He sat down again. It felt good to be angry. It was cleaner than the other thing that had sat in his chest since Monday night: Marcus Chen's face on every screen in the country, and the word under it.

At the far end of the table, Dmitri had not taken part in any of it.

He had the sheet of camera arcs in front of him, and a pencil, and in the margin, in his square hand, he had written *14:21*, and underlined it, and was looking at it.

"You keep doing that," said Alexei.

"Yes."

"It was a man with a card, Dima. It's a Ministry. You said so yourself."

"I did not say so. You said so." Dmitri turned the pencil over. "He was not hurrying, and he was not late. People who are going to their desks are one or the other. He took the side steps two at a time." He looked at the number. "Forty people say that Marcus Chen walked into the Deputy Minister's office at twenty past two. At twenty-one minutes past two I watched a man go in at the side entrance that serves her lifts. It was not Marcus."

"Then it was someone else."

"Yes," said Dmitri. "That is what I keep thinking."

---

Old Songs asked his question on Wednesday evening.

He had said nothing on the drive home, not a word in an hour and a half. Even the humming had stopped, and he had sat with his gnarled hands on his knees and looked out at the fields with an expression Alexei could not read. He had said nothing on Tuesday either. He had washed dishes, and slept on his blanket in the corner, and once, in the night, Alexei had heard him humming very softly in the dark, the same phrase over and over, as if checking it.

On Wednesday evening they ate together at the long table. Elena had stopped swearing, which meant she was tired. Marina and Jakob had begun, very slightly, to eat. Dmitri was reading the camera sheet again. And Old Songs sat at the end of the bench with a mug of tea in front of him, stirring it, slowly, round and round, long after the sugar must have dissolved.

"Who told you," he said, "that their covers wouldn't last the week?"

It was so unexpected that for a moment nobody understood him. He did not speak often. When he did, people tended to go on doing what they were doing for a second or two, as if a clock had struck in another room.

Then the table went quiet.

Elena looked up from her plate. "My source," she said.

Old Songs went on stirring his tea. He did not look at her. He looked at the tea.

"And who told your source?" he said.

Nobody answered. Elena opened her mouth, and closed it again. Across the table, Dmitri had gone still, in the way all three of them went still when they were listening; Alexei felt it through the bond before he saw it, a cold weight settling. The stove ticked. Outside, the fog had come up off the lake and was pressing at the barn's high dusty windows, gray, real, ordinary.

Old Songs took the spoon out of his tea and laid it on the table, and drank.

---

It was Marina who broke the silence, and she did it without meaning to.

She had got up from the table while they sat there, and gone to the bale by the stove where her archive box stood, and begun, for something to do with her hands, to unpack it. Alexei watched her. She did it the way an archivist would, even here, even now: each sheet out, glanced at, squared, set down in a pile on the bale beside her. The week's paper intake. Memos. Circulars. Printed minutes that someone at the Ministry still, for some reason nobody remembered, sent down to the archive in hard copy for the permanent file.

She stopped.

She was holding a single sheet of heavy Ministry paper, with the seal at the head and a small line of type at the foot. She read it. Then she read it again. Then she turned round and held it out, not to Elena, but to Alexei, who happened to be nearest, and her hand was not quite steady.

"This is the Deputy Minister's day sheet," she said. "For Monday. They print one every morning for the archive. It's a formality. Nobody reads them." She swallowed. "Look at the afternoon."

Alexei took it.

*OFFICE OF THE DEPUTY MINISTER. DR. A. SINGH. SCHEDULE: MONDAY 15 NOVEMBER 2106. ARCHIVE COPY. PRINTED 07:40.*

The morning was ordinary: a committee, a briefing, a lunch. Then the type went on.

*14:00 — Medical crossing waiver, eastern strip. Countersignature. (SANTOS, D., and two.)*

*14:15 — Deep-cover review. Archives; Enforcement Dispatch. (Two.)*

*14:30 — Border transfer sign-off. Lakeshore. (OKONKWO, I.)*

*14:30 — Meeting. Archive researcher. Public annex visitor pass.*

He read it twice. He was aware of the others getting up round the table, of Elena at his shoulder, of Dmitri's weight in the bond gone very cold and very close. He was aware of Old Songs, at the end of the bench, not moving at all.

Tanaka's family at the fence. Isaiah at the lakeshore. Marina and Jakob, the two people sitting beside the stove. And Marcus, in the annex, with his evidence going into the record and a Deputy Minister waiting for him upstairs.

Four meetings, in one hour, in a dead woman's diary.

They were all of them.
