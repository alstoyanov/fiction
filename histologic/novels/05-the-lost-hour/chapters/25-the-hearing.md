# Chapter 25: The Hearing

*Thursday, December 23 – Monday, December 27, 2106. The hearing chamber, Justice Center, Veridica Capital*

---

The Judge had no chair.

Helena Kovač had known that, as a fact, for most of her adult life. She had never before sat in the room where it mattered. The hearing chamber of the Justice Center was a pale half-circle under a high skylight, with tiered galleries on three sides and, where a bench would once have stood, a single white panel the width of the wall, blank and faintly luminous. Below it, at a small desk, sat the Registrar of the Chamber, a gray-haired official whose only duty was to read aloud what appeared above him. That was all The Judge was, in a room. A surface that would, when it was ready, have words on it.

At nine exactly, it did.

*QUESTION BEFORE THE JUDGE: WHERE THE FACTOREPO HOLDS NO RECORD, MAY FACTS BE ESTABLISHED BY TESTIMONY?*

The Registrar read it. The galleries, which were full, went quiet.

Kovač sat at the petitioner's table with a paper folder in front of her and her hands flat on it. Behind her, in the second row, under the lead investigator's guard, sat Marcus Chen and Kira Osman, legally dead, present on a custodial order. Across the aisle, at the investigators' table, sat Wei, his bandaged hand resting on the table, with his team behind him. In the upper gallery the foreign press had their devices up: Chronos, Synthesis, even an SFW crew, who had come for Isaiah Okonkwo's story and stayed for this.

She had not slept. She had stopped expecting to some time ago.

---

She argued it the way she had argued everything in her life: as a briefing.

"The record of November fifteenth between fourteen and fifteen hundred hours does not exist," she said. "It is not contradictory. It is not corrupted. It is absent. Veridican law has no provision for absence, because Veridican law assumes the record is complete. It has been complete for forty years. It is not complete now." She turned a page, though she did not need to. "In that hour a Deputy Minister died, three citizens left the country, two state employees in deep cover left their posts, and a prisoner was released across a border. None of those facts can be established. Under current law, none of them occurred. The Deputy Minister is dead, and her death did not happen."

Nobody laughed. She had not meant them to.

"People were there. They saw. Some of what they saw has been written over. We know that now, and we know how. Seeding writes the same thing into everyone within range of a node. So the question is not whether memory can be trusted. It cannot, simply. The question is whether *many* memories, from *independent* witnesses, some of whom could not have been written, can be weighed against one another, and against what we know of the contamination. I ask The Judge to hold that they can." She closed the folder. "The alternative is to leave an hour of Veridica's history permanently empty, and to let whoever emptied it decide what goes there instead."

Undersecretary Pell rose for the Ministry. He was good. She had never pretended otherwise.

"The Director asks The Judge to take the word of the living over the silence of the record," he said. "Five weeks ago, this nation learned that memory can be issued by the state, from a broadcast node. Forty loyal citizens remember a thing that did not happen, and remember it with perfect confidence. That is what memory is. And the Director, knowing it, asks us to make it law." He looked up at the white panel. "A nation of memories is a nation of liars. Not because people lie. Because they cannot help it. The record was built so that no one would ever again have to trust a man's recollection over a fact. I ask The Judge not to undo the Founding because one hour is missing."

He sat down. There was a murmur in the upper gallery, and it was not hostile.

*He is right about one thing,* Kovač thought. And then, because she was honest with herself in private even when she could not afford to be in public: *He is right about the thing that matters most, and for every wrong reason.*

The panel changed.

*THE JUDGE WILL HEAR WITNESSES. EACH WITNESS WILL BE WEIGHTED FOR EXPOSURE TO KNOWN CONTAMINATION.*

---

They had agreed the order a week ago in a Ministry room with the blinds down. The clean witnesses first. The dead first.

"The petitioner calls Dmitri Volkov."

He came from the side door and walked to the witness stand, and Kovač heard the upper gallery lean forward. They had seen his face on the Justice Center steps a fortnight ago, twice. The panel above the Registrar changed.

*WITNESS: VOLKOV, DMITRI IVANOVICH. REGISTRY STATUS: DECEASED (AUGUST 2, 2106). THE JUDGE NOTES THE CONTRADICTION AND PROCEEDS.*

Someone in the gallery laughed, a short startled sound, and stopped.

Dmitri gave his account of fourteen twenty-one exactly as he had given it on the steps and in every room since, in the flat, formal voice of a police report. The roof, five storeys up. The distance, forty-odd metres. The light. The dark jacket, the staff card in the right hand, the left hand held slightly away from the body. The three stone steps, taken two and then one. He did not embellish. He did not look at the investigators' table. When Pell asked whether a man seen at that distance for six seconds could be identified, he said, "No. He could be described. I have described him."

"The petitioner calls Alexei Volkov."

The same face, walking in. The chamber made a noise Kovač had never heard a Veridican room make: a kind of collective intake, half delight and half dread. Alexei grinned at the white panel as if it had made a joke. *REGISTRY STATUS: DECEASED.* He gave the extraction, fourteen twenty-five to fourteen forty-three, with dates and times, and the faint wrongness at fourteen thirty-eight, and matched his brother wherever their hours touched.

Then Kovač said, "The petitioner calls Nikolai Volkov," and the side door did not open.

Instead, at the back of the investigators' table, among the lead investigator's technical team, a man stood up.

He had shaved that morning. The glasses he did not need were in his hand. For more than three months he had been Pieter Lang, a dull contractor who ate noodles at his desk, and now he walked down the aisle toward the stand with his shoulders straight for the first time, and Kovač watched the chamber understand what it was looking at. Three men. One face. All dead.

At the investigators' table the young technician, Maya Reeves, had her hand over her mouth, not in surprise. And Wei, beside her, watched Pieter Lang walk away from his team with an expression that Kovač could only describe as interest. A small smile, quickly gone. The look a senior colleague gives when a junior one has answered well.

*WITNESS: VOLKOV, NIKOLAI IVANOVICH. REGISTRY STATUS: DECEASED. ALSO REGISTERED: LANG, PIETER, CONTRACTOR. THE JUDGE NOTES THE CONTRADICTION AND PROCEEDS.*

Nikolai gave his evidence looking at his hands. The unowned block on the core, flagged on the fourteenth of November. The edge caches after fifteen-oh-one. The wrongness at fourteen thirty-eight, faint, felt through his brothers as much as through himself. His voice was quiet and precise and it did not shake, and when he stepped down he did not go back to the investigators' table. He went and sat beside Alexei.

---

The Registrar read Dr. Tanaka's written analysis into the record. Kovač had asked for it on the fifteenth and received it on the nineteenth, eleven pages in a physician's plain prose: distributed salience; three peaks on a monitor where there should have been one; a memory held from three places at once that a single-node broadcast could press on but not overwrite. *The bond is not telepathy,* Tanaka had written. *It is redundancy. The Volkov brothers remember each other's hours the way a well-designed record keeps its backups. Seeding reached them. It did not take.*

The panel was silent for nearly a minute. Then:

*WITNESSES VOLKOV, D.; VOLKOV, A.; VOLKOV, N. EXPOSURE: WITHIN RANGE OF MINISTRY DISTRICT NODE. RESISTANCE: CLINICAL ANALYSIS ADMITTED. CONCORDANCE BETWEEN WITNESSES: HIGH. WEIGHT: 0.86.*

Kovač wrote the number in her folder, on paper, and underlined it.

---

Captain Sokolov came in uniform in the afternoon.

Gray, with the insignia of a captain, the long dark coat over her arm. She took the stand as if it were a checkpoint, and gave her evidence in the register of a report. At fourteen-thirty on November fifteenth she had personally escorted the prisoner Isaiah Okonkwo across the lakeshore checkpoint into Chronos, under a lawful expulsion order. At the time she had been on the lakeshore, far outside the range of the Ministry district's node. At nine that morning she had called the Deputy Minister's office to confirm the slot, and been told by the Deputy Minister's aide that the Deputy Minister had never requested it. At fourteen-oh-two the slot had been confirmed anyway, from the Deputy Minister's own credentials. She had noted the time in her paper log. She produced the log. It went to the Registrar's desk and lay there under the white panel, a small gray notebook, and the panel considered it.

*WITNESS: SOKOLOV, YEVGENIA, CAPTAIN, BORDER SECURITY. EXPOSURE: OUTSIDE RANGE. CORROBORATING DOCUMENT: ADMITTED. WEIGHT: 0.94.*

Pell asked her whether she had anything further. "No," she said. She did not look at the Volkovs once.

On her way out she passed the petitioner's table, and did not break stride, and said, so low that Kovač was not afterwards certain she had heard it, "Keep the paper, Director."

Then she was gone.

Marina Holt and Jakob Lind followed: an archivist and a dispatcher, who had walked out of their posts between fourteen twenty-five and fourteen forty-three, and had been ghosts since. The Santos family came by link from Chronos, three faces on the chamber's side screen. Davi was careful and Lucia was quiet. Lia, when her turn came, looked straight into the camera and said, "I've already said this in one court. I'll say it in yours. Nobody took me anywhere I didn't in the end decide to go," and the upper gallery laughed, and she did not.

*EXPOSURE: OUTSIDE RANGE. WEIGHT: 0.91.*

---

On Friday morning, the twenty-fourth, the Registrar read Dr. Reeves's confession into the record.

Kovač had read it four times. It was worse aloud. The east stairs. The breath on the ninth floor. The face looking up. The letter opener, colder than expected. The flowers. The chamber listened in a silence that had weight to it, and when the Registrar reached the end, Pell closed his eyes briefly, like a man hearing a hymn he likes.

Behind her, Kovač did not turn round to look at Marcus Chen. She had seen his face on Monday, in the medical wing, and had taken it for relief. She was no longer sure it had been.

*STATEMENT: REEVES (DR.). STATEMENT VOLUNTARY. KNOWN CONTAMINATION: NONE RECORDED. WEIGHT: 0.88.*

Then, because he had been on the list and she had promised Elena Volkov that she would call him, she said, "The petitioner calls Mikhail Petrov."

He came in slowly, stooped, the white ponytail tied back, in a brown coat made for a broader man. He did not go to the stand. He stood in the aisle, a little way from it, and looked up at the white panel with his pale eyes the way he might have looked at weather.

*WITNESS: PETROV, MIKHAIL. REGISTRY STATUS: DECEASED.*

"Mr. Petrov," said Kovač. "You were in the Ministry district during the interval. In a delivery truck, with the Volkov brothers' extraction. Will you give The Judge your account?"

He considered the question with great courtesy.

"No, Director," he said. "Thank you. My memory isn't evidence." A small movement of the shoulders. "It's a song."

The chamber laughed. It was a warm laugh, relieved, the laugh of a room that has been serious for too long and has been handed an old man to be fond of. The Registrar smiled. Even Pell smiled.

Kovač did not.

She had certified this man's death in August, along with nine others, at a desk in Ashford, in a building where he had pushed the meal trolley for seventeen years, eleven of them while she was its Director. She had hardly ever heard him say a word. And she had learned in the last month what he had been carrying up and down those corridors, three times a day, humming. She looked at him standing in the aisle with his gnarled hands folded in front of him, being laughed at kindly by the whole of the Justice Center, and understood that he had chosen it. That he had been choosing it for forty years. That a man who is furniture cannot be weighted.

He inclined his head to her, very slightly, and went out.

---

Pell rose at eleven.

She had known it was coming. She had known since the fifteenth of November, sitting in the dark in her car in the Ministry garage. She had not known it would be so quiet.

"Director," he said. "A procedural point, before The Judge weighs these witnesses. Six of the people named before this chamber are recorded as dead. Their deaths were certified in August. By you." He looked at her with something that was almost regret. "If they are alive, as The Judge has just watched them be, then those certificates are false. I would ask The Judge to weigh the petitioner's own reliability before it weighs her witnesses. Did you certify the deaths of Dmitri, Alexei and Nikolai Volkov, knowing them to be alive?"

The chamber was completely silent. She heard the skylight tick in the cold.

She could have asked for time. She could have said that the certificates had been issued on telemetry, which they had. She had survived an inquiry for four months by saying exactly what the Registry said and nothing else. She knew every sentence that would keep her in her post, and she could feel them in her mouth.

She pushed her glasses up her nose.

"Yes," she said.

Somebody in the upper gallery drew a breath.

"And if the record can be wrong about the dead," said Helena Kovač, "it can be wrong about anything. That is the question before you."

She did not look at Pell. She looked at the white panel, which had no face, and waited.

*THE JUDGE NOTES THE ADMISSION. THE PETITIONER'S STATEMENT IS WEIGHTED ACCORDINGLY.*

She sat down. Her hands, when she put them on the folder, were perfectly steady, and she was aware, in a distant and administrative way, that she had probably just ended her career. Behind her, very quietly, somebody said "Director," and she thought it was Marcus Chen, and did not turn round.

---

The panel changed again before the recess.

*THE JUDGE REQUESTS THAT OFFICERS OF THE INVESTIGATION STATE THEIR OWN MOVEMENTS DURING THE INTERVAL, FOR COMPLETENESS.*

It was a standard request. Kovač had seen it on a hundred inquiry transcripts. Wei rose at once, as if he had been waiting to be asked.

"Of course," he said.

He had it ready, because he had everything ready. From thirteen-thirty until the Ministry's call at fifteen-twenty, he had been at his desk in the Systems Architecture office. He had been reviewing a node maintenance schedule. His colleagues had seen him there. He had not thought it would be needed, he said, with a slight apologetic smile, but his office had been thorough: here were their statements. The clerk carried them to the Registrar's desk. Thirty-six of them. Kovač watched the stack go past, and heard the gallery's small approving murmur, and saw Pell nod.

*NOTED.*

Nobody questioned it. There was nothing to question. A lead investigator, asked for his movements, had produced three dozen colleagues who remembered him at his desk. It was more witnesses than the forty had been, almost. It was the kind of thing a careful man had in a drawer. Wei sat down, and folded his hands on the table, and listened to his junior technician state that she had been on the monitoring floor of the Regional Factorepo from noon.

Kovač looked at her folder.

The Systems Architecture office was in the Ministry district. She knew the building. She had walked past it every week of the inquiry. It was four streets from the one she had spent that hour in, well inside the range of the node that had written forty people's memories and left her own hearing room unable to agree whether there had been a recess at fourteen twenty-five or fourteen forty.

Thirty-six witnesses. Every one of them inside the district.

She did not know what to make of it. It might be nothing. He was the man who had stood in the rain at a checkpoint for Marcus Chen. He was the man whose case had just given the whole capital permission to breathe. And she had been in that district herself, and could not reconstruct her own hour well enough to swear to it; she had no standing to doubt anyone else's.

She wrote it down anyway, on paper, because on paper nobody could adjust it.

*W. 13:30–15:20, desk. Witnesses: SA office (36). District.*

She underlined the last word, once, and closed the folder over it.

---

The Judge deliberated for four days.

Evidence closed at noon on Christmas Eve. The panel said *THE JUDGE IS CONSIDERING THE QUESTION* and then nothing else, and the chamber emptied into a capital that did not know how to wait for a machine that had never needed to be waited for. Crowds gathered on the Justice Center steps, where the dead had testified a fortnight before. Someone brought candles. The Synthesis feeds ran Isaiah Okonkwo's face and his article on a loop. Chronos commentators debated whether Veridica was about to discover it had been wrong for forty years, and seemed to hope so.

Kovač went home. She spent Christmas Eve in her flat with the inquiry papers spread on the table and did not read them. At some point after midnight she found herself thinking about her nephew Adrian, who used to take apart her kitchen clock, and to whom she had not known what to say at Christmas for six years, and whom she had not said anything to since. She thought that she would like, now, to be able to tell him that she had said *yes* in a room full of people. She did not know where he was. She went to bed and did not sleep.

On Christmas Day she walked for three hours in the cold. On Sunday she sat in her office at Ashford, which was still, for the moment, her office, and looked at the overwatered plant, and at the pond with its fountain, and waited for someone to come and tell her to leave. Nobody came.

On Monday morning the twenty-seventh, at nine exactly, the chamber was full again, and the panel changed.

---

The Registrar stood to read it. He had never stood before.

*DETERMINATION OF THE JUDGE.*

*WHERE THE RECORD IS ABSENT, FACTS MAY BE ESTABLISHED BY THE CONCORDANT TESTIMONY OF MULTIPLE INDEPENDENT WITNESSES, WEIGHTED FOR EXPOSURE TO KNOWN CONTAMINATION.*

*THIS DETERMINATION IS FINAL.*

For a moment nothing happened at all. Then the upper gallery stood up, all at once, the way the Volkov brothers stood, and the noise came down into the chamber like water.

Kovač sat very still at the petitioner's table with her hands on her folder. Behind her she heard Kira Osman say something short and fierce, and Marcus Chen say nothing at all. Across the aisle Alexei Volkov had his arms round both his brothers, and Dmitri was allowing it. At the Ministry's table Pell sat with his hands folded, looking at the panel, his face gray and composed; he had lost, and he was a man who knew how to lose in public. At the investigators' table Wei was on his feet, applauding, gravely, like a man at the end of a concert he had hoped would go well.

*For the first time,* she thought, *The Judge will accept what people remember.*

It did not feel like triumph. It felt like the moment after the override took, in B-4, in August, when the shielding held and she understood that she would have to go on typing it every four seconds for as long as it mattered.

---

Outside, the steps were white with frost and full of cameras.

She came out into the cold with the folder under her arm and the noise hit her like a wall. Someone shouted her name. Someone else was singing. She had not prepared anything to say, and found that nobody wanted her to say anything; they only wanted to look at her.

And then the crowd parted, courteously, the way crowds part for ministers, and Minister Chen Zhao came down the steps toward her.

He was silver-haired and slight in a dark coat, and he was smiling with open, uncomplicated delight, the smile he had worn in his office nearly four weeks ago over a glass of tea when she first told him what she wanted. He took her hand in both of his. The cameras turned to them, all of them at once, and the light of them was very bright.

"You've restored something precious, Director," said Chen Zhao.

*Restored.*

Kovač smiled for the photographers. She held the Minister's hand and turned her face to the lights and smiled exactly as long as the moment required, the way she had once stood in front of the inquiry and said precisely what the Registry said.

And she felt the word land in her stomach like a stone.
