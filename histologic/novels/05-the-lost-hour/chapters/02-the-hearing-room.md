# Chapter 2: The Hearing Room

*Monday, November 15, 2106. Hearing Room 4B, fourth floor, Ministry of Historical Integrity, Veridica Capital*

---

The inquiry did not permit witnesses to take notes. It had been explained to Director Helena Kovač at the first session, in August, with great courtesy: the proceedings were recorded directly into the factorepo, every word and every pause, verified and entered as they happened, and a witness's private notes could only ever be less accurate than the record. There was therefore no need for them. There was, in fact, a rule against them.

She had kept paper for four years because on paper nobody could adjust it. She sat at the witness table in Hearing Room 4B with her hands folded on the bare wood in front of her, and no pen, and nothing to write on, and thought that this was the kind of joke she would have appreciated more if it had been about somebody else.

It was 13:40. The ninth session.

The room was long and plain, with a window at one end onto the gray inner courtyard and a clock at the other, the same white Ministry clock as in every room in the building. The inquiry sat at a raised table under it: Chair Halvorsen in the middle, a member from the Department of Correction's legal office on one side, a member from the Registry on the other. Below them, the inquiry's counsel, a thin young man with a stack of tablets, and the recording clerk at her console. By the door, two Ministry security officers. At the witness table, Kovač, and beside her Varga, her own clerk, who had come in with her from Ashford for every session since August and who was not permitted to speak unless asked a question of fact.

And at the side of the room, in a chair against the wall that was not quite part of the proceedings and not quite apart from them, Undersecretary Iver Pell, watching, with a young aide from his office at his elbow.

Twelve people. She had counted them at the first session and counted them every time since. It was a habit from Wing C: know who is in the room.

"Director," said Halvorsen. "Thank you for your patience. We'll resume where we left off on Thursday. The night of the first and second of August."

"Of course."

Halvorsen had a courteous voice and used it for everything. It was courteous when it thanked her for coming and courteous when it read her own signatures back to her, and it had a way of saying *thank you, Director* that meant *not yet*. She had learned, in eleven years, to tell the kind of chair who wanted the truth from the kind who wanted a finding. Halvorsen wanted a finding. Halvorsen wanted it politely, and would be very polite to her on the day it arrived.

---

The counsel took her, again, through the certifications.

Ten deaths in the Correction Registry, entered in the early morning of August the second, over her signature. Six of them the subjects of a programme called Continuity. Four of them others: an inmate named Petrov, a Continuity engineer named Morrison, two patients from the Wing C overflow. All ten recorded as *cognitive failure during fact storm*. Remains processed according to standard protocols. No family viewing.

"You signed these certificates personally," said the counsel.

"Yes."

"Is that usual?"

"In a mass-casualty event, the Director signs. It is in the Department's procedures. Section nine, paragraph four." She did not look at Varga. Varga would have the paragraph number right; she always did.

"And you saw the remains."

"I saw ten bodies bagged for processing. I did not open the bags. That is also in the procedures." She pushed her glasses up her nose. "I have signed that form a great many times. I know exactly what it says."

It was true. Every word she said in this room was true. That was the whole of her method, and it had held for nine sessions: to say only what the forms said, in the order the forms said it, and to be so dull, so procedural, so tediously exact, that there was nothing for anyone to take hold of.

The counsel moved on to the storm itself. The shielding. The timing of the alarm.

Kovač answered, and while she answered she thought about the building above her head.

---

Eight floors up, on the twelfth, was the Deputy Minister's office.

Kovač had met Dr. Singh four times, formally, across a table like this one, and had not been able to decide what she thought of her. A careful woman. A woman with her sleeves rolled up. A woman who had opened an inquiry into Continuity on the morning after Okonkwo's account was published, which was either very brave or very foolish, and in the Ministry those were frequently the same thing.

And at 14:30 this afternoon, according to the one short message Elena Volkov's people had allowed themselves to send her, a man whom Kovač had certified dead on the second of August would walk into that office on a visitor's pass in a false name.

She had not asked how. She had not wanted to know. She knew only that he would be in the building, above her, while she sat here and swore on the record that he was ash.

And there was the other thing. At 14:30 this afternoon, at the lakeshore checkpoint, Isaiah Okonkwo would be walked across the line into Chronos under an expulsion order that her office had requested, and that she had signed. She had wanted it done quietly, and quickly, before anyone upstairs thought to ask why the one survivor of Wing C was still in a concrete cell two hours north of the capital. Varga had brought her the slot on the eleventh. *The only one available, Director.* Monday, 14:30. She had looked at it, and thought that she did not like the coincidence, and then thought that the Ministry's scheduling was full of coincidences because the Ministry's scheduling was full of Mondays, and signed.

Two of them, at the same half hour. One above her and one at the water's edge.

She could feel the whole of it in her chest, the way you feel a breath you have been holding without noticing: a small, steady pressure under the breastbone. It had been there since she woke. She answered the counsel's questions through it, precisely, in order, and nobody in the room could have seen it.

At 13:58 she glanced at the clock. It was a thing she would remember afterwards, that she had glanced at it. She would remember very little else.

---

The hour passed.

That was how she would put it later, to herself, when she tried to take it apart and found that it would not come apart in her hands. The hour passed. There were questions. She answered them. The counsel asked about the shielding logs and she explained the override protocol; the member from the Registry asked something about the timing of the flatlines, and she answered that too, or thought she did. At some point there was a recess. At some point Varga poured her a glass of water from the jug on the witness table, and she drank half of it.

And at some point, she could not have said when, there was a moment in which the room was slightly too bright.

It was nothing. Nothing flickered. The lights did not change and the window did not change, and the gray courtyard outside it stayed exactly the gray it had been all afternoon. It was only a sense, very faint and gone almost before it arrived, that the room was lit a little more than it should be, the way a room seems when one has not slept and has been looking at a screen for too long. Like a word on the tip of one's tongue, if the word were a colour.

She had spent eleven years among people who had lived through fact storms. This was not that. It was so much less than that that it hardly seemed worth the comparison.

*Fatigue,* she thought. She had been sleeping badly since August. She filed it and went back to the question in front of her, which was about paragraph four.

---

At 15:01 every device in the room went off at once.

The recording clerk's console first, a flat, rising tone Kovač had never heard; then the counsel's tablets, all of them, in a stack; then the phones in pockets and the security officers' handsets by the door, a whole room of small machines crying out together. The member from the Registry was on her feet. Halvorsen said, courteously, "Order, please," and nobody heard.

Pell had his phone out and was reading it with no expression at all.

"It's the primary," said the recording clerk. She was a young woman with very straight hair and she had gone white to the lips. "The primary repository. It says—" She looked up at the raised table. "It says fourteen hundred to fifteen hundred has failed. Chair. The session. The whole session since two o'clock. It isn't there."

"That's not possible," said the member from Correction.

"It's not *there*, sir."

Kovač sat very still at the witness table with her hands folded. She looked at the clock. 15:02. She looked at the pressure under her breastbone, which had not gone anywhere, and which had, she found, become something else.

At 15:05 one of the security officers' handsets, which had been left open on the building's internal channel, carried a sound into the room.

It was a man's voice, somewhere else in the building, very close to the microphone at the other end, and it was not saying anything. It was screaming. It went on for three or four seconds, high and ragged, the sound of a person who has opened a door and seen what is behind it, and then someone at the other end said *twelve, twelve, get up here*, and the officer fumbled the handset and shut it off.

Nobody in Hearing Room 4B said anything at all.

It was twenty minutes before the word came down the building, in the way words do: from the corridor, then from a phone, then from everyone at once. The Deputy Minister was dead. Her aide had found her in her office on the twelfth floor. Lucian Bell, the aide. It had been him on the channel.

Kovač took her glasses off and rubbed her eyes, and put them back on.

---

Security held the room. Nobody was to leave the building until the floors had been cleared, and the floors would be cleared one at a time, from the top.

So they waited. The counsel sat down on the floor with his back against the wall, as though his legs had given out. Pell made calls in a low voice by the door, one after another, with his back to the room.

At 15:50, Chair Halvorsen said, "I think we should make a record."

Everyone looked up.

"Of the session," said Halvorsen. "The record is gone. Since two o'clock there is no account of anything that was said in this room, and there may be questions, later, about who was where. I think it would be prudent for us to agree now, while it's fresh, what took place. For the record, since the record is gone." A small, courteous smile. "Clerk, if you'd take it down. On paper will do."

The clerk found a pad. Halvorsen began to dictate.

"Session resumed at thirteen forty. Counsel's examination of the witness on the August certifications, sections nine and ten. At approximately fourteen twenty-five, the Chair called a recess of ten minutes—"

"Fourteen forty," said Varga.

She said it quietly, apologetically, the way a clerk corrects a figure. Everyone turned.

"I'm sorry, Chair," said Varga. "It was fourteen forty. The recess. I remember looking at the clock."

Kovač opened her mouth to agree with Halvorsen, and closed it again.

Because she remembered it too. The recess at 14:25. She remembered it quite distinctly: the counsel had just finished on the shielding logs and Halvorsen had looked at the clock and said *let's take ten minutes*, and it had said 14:25. She could see the hands. And Varga, who had worked beside her for six years, who had never once in six years been wrong about a time or a paragraph or a date, Varga whom she would have trusted with her life and very nearly had, was sitting beside her with her hands in her lap saying 14:40, and meaning it.

"Twenty-five," said the member from Correction. "I'm sure it was twenty-five."

"I thought there were two recesses," said the counsel from the floor.

Halvorsen went on, courteously, as if nothing had happened. It kept happening. It happened with almost every sentence. Halvorsen dictated that the member from the Registry had asked about the flatline timings, and the member from the Registry said no, it had been the counsel, she remembered the counsel asking, and the counsel said it had been the Chair. One of the security officers said the witness had asked for water at the recess, and Kovač said, before she could stop herself, that she had not asked for anything; Varga had simply poured it. Varga looked at her with surprise and said, gently, "Director, you asked."

And then Halvorsen said, "The Undersecretary remained in the room throughout," and Kovač said, "No."

The word was out before she had decided on it. Pell turned from the door.

"I'm sorry," said Kovač. "Undersecretary. I thought you left at the recess. For some minutes." She could see it. She could see the empty chair against the wall. "I'm quite sure of it."

Pell looked at her for a moment with an expression of mild interest. "Are you, Director."

"He didn't leave," said Varga, in a small voice, beside her. "Director, he didn't. He was in that chair the whole time."

"He left," said the member from the Registry. "I saw him go."

"I don't believe I did," said Pell pleasantly, and turned back to his call.

Every one of them was certain. That was what Kovač would think about afterwards, in the car, in the dark. Not that they disagreed. People disagree; she had read a thousand witness statements and knew exactly how people disagree, with hesitations and qualifications and *I think it might have been*. These people did not hesitate. Each of them remembered the hour clearly and exactly and differently, and each of them was as sure of it as of their own name. And she was one of them. She was as certain of the 14:25 recess and the empty chair as of anything in her life. And she was contradicting a clerk whose memory she had relied on for six years over matters far more serious than a recess.

Halvorsen finished the summary at 16:20. It was two pages long and full of brackets.

*Shock,* Kovač told herself. It was the reasonable explanation. Twelve people in a locked room, a woman murdered upstairs, alarms, a scream on a handset: of course they could not agree about a recess. Shock scrambles things. Everyone knew that.

She sat with her hands folded on the witness table and did not believe it.

---

They were released at 16:30.

In the fourth-floor corridor, outside Hearing Room 4B, the whole floor seemed to have come out of its rooms at once. People stood in knots, talking low, looking at the lifts, which were held. Someone was crying quietly by the water fountain.

Pell stopped in the middle of the corridor, with a knot of officials from the fourth and fifth floors around him, and said, in a clear, ordinary voice that carried perfectly well:

"Well, I think we all know who we'll be looking at. The Continuity escapees. The ones who got out of Ashford in August. It's the obvious place to start."

Somebody said, "But they're dead."

"Are they?" said Pell. He did not look at Kovač. He did not need to. "I should think the Director has an opinion on that."

Kovač walked past him to the stairs. She said nothing. She was the Director who had certified them dead, and there was nothing she could say in that corridor that would not be taken down and used, by someone, later.

It was only afterwards, going down the stairs with Varga silent behind her, that she thought: *it was half past four.* No one had named a suspect yet. No one had said anything about who had been seen, or how. And the Undersecretary had already known where to look.

---

It was dusk when she came out onto the Ministry steps.

The sky over the district had gone the colour of wet slate, and the lights were on in all the towers, and the square was full of enforcement vehicles with their lamps turning. She stood at the top of the steps with her coat buttoned and her hands in its pockets and let herself think it through, in order, the way she would have laid it out for a committee.

The escapees were alive. She knew that. She had known it since the second of August, since she had sat in maintenance room B-4 retyping an override every four seconds to keep the shielding down so they could run. Marcus Chen had been in this building this afternoon. If anyone could prove that, then her ten certificates were ten lies, and her signature was on all of them, and the inquiry that had spent nine sessions failing to find anything would find it in an afternoon.

And someone had just told a corridor full of witnesses where to look.

*Today*, she thought. *They will find out today.*

She had been so careful. Nine sessions. Every word true. And none of it would matter, because the thing she had been hiding had walked into the building on a pass with a Deputy Minister's name on it, and the Deputy Minister was dead.

---

Her car was on the second level of the Ministry garage. She got into it and shut the door and sat, with the engine off, in the concrete dark.

Her comm lit up on the seat beside her.

An unknown number. No header, no routing, nothing she could have traced if she had wanted to. Plain text, all capitals, the way the resistance wrote when they had no time for anything else.

*THEY ARE SAYING HE DID IT. HE DIDN'T. — K.*

She read it three times. Then she turned on the car's screen, because she had to know what *they are saying* meant, and the evening news was already running.

The Ministry at dusk, the steps she had just come down, the enforcement lamps turning. The Deputy Minister's photograph. And then a newsreader, very composed, reading from a sheet: forty Ministry employees had given statements to enforcers during the afternoon. Forty people, interviewed separately. All of them had seen the man who walked into Dr. Singh's office at twenty past two. All of them named him.

The picture changed to a face. She knew the face. She had looked at it across her own desk in June with the recording switched off, and seen, in it, the thing that had made her turn.

Director Helena Kovač sat in the dark of the garage with her hands in her coat pockets and her comm glowing on the seat beside her, and for the first time since August, for the first time in all those careful weeks, she was frightened for herself.
