# Chapter 3: The Upload

*Monday, November 15, 2106. Public archive annex (B1), Ministry of Historical Integrity, Veridica Capital*

---

*November fifteenth, 2106. Thirteen forty-five hours. Ministry of Historical Integrity, public archive annex, basement level one. Involved parties: Harrow, O., archive researcher. One assistant. Status: entering.*

The entry came to Marcus of its own accord as they went down the steps from the street, and he let it. It was steadying. It was, he thought, the last entry he would make today that nobody could check.

The public annex was reached by its own door, at the side of the Ministry, down a short flight of stone steps from the pavement, so that researchers and school parties would not have to pass through the great hall above. There was a glass vestibule at the bottom. There was a guard at a desk inside it, a heavy man with a gray moustache and a cup of something steaming at his elbow, and a reader on the desk for passes, and a camera dome in the ceiling above him with its small green light.

Marcus held out Mr. Harrow's pass. His hand did not shake. He had wondered, on the train in from the border, whether it would.

The reader chimed. The guard glanced at the screen, and at Marcus, and at Kira beside him with her satchel on her shoulder, and at the screen again.

"Research," he said. "Deputy Minister's authorisation. Very nice." He did not sound impressed. He sounded like a man who had seen a great many passes. "Terminal nine. Your window's two to three. You've got a two-thirty upstairs, it says."

"Yes," said Marcus.

"Lift's at the back. They'll send down for you." The guard waved them through, and picked up his cup, and did not look at them again.

---

The annex was a long, low room with a vaulted ceiling painted white, and it was very quiet.

Twenty public terminals stood in two rows down the middle, each in its own shallow booth. Along the walls were reading tables, and shelves of bound finding-aids that nobody had needed for half a century and that the Ministry kept, Marcus supposed, because they looked like history. At the far end a school party of perhaps fifteen children in blue uniforms sat cross-legged on the floor around a young woman who was explaining, in a bright voice, how a fact became a fact. Half a dozen researchers were at the terminals. An old man at a reading table was asleep with his chin on his chest.

And cameras. Marcus counted them without turning his head, the way Kira had taught him in the garden at Ashford. Six on the ceiling. One over each end door. The small dark eye in the top of every terminal booth.

He was wearing a borrowed pair of heavy black frames in place of his own glasses, with lenses near enough to his prescription that the edges of the room swam only a little when he turned his head. He had a researcher's cardigan under his coat and a researcher's canvas bag, and he had let his hair grow over his ears since August. None of it would have survived a second look from anyone who had studied the photograph in Isaiah's article. He was relying on there being no second look.

He walked down the room toward terminal nine with his head a little lowered and his face arranged into nothing.

That, at least, he knew how to do. He had learned it in Wing C, in months of meal trays and garden hours and white chairs: how to let the face go slack and the eyes go dull and the shoulders round, how to be one more piece of furniture that the cameras would pass over. He had hated every day of learning it. He found now, walking between the rows of terminals under the painted vault, that his body remembered it perfectly, and he was grateful, and he hated that too.

Kira walked beside him, half a pace behind, as an assistant would. She did not look at the cameras. She had never needed to.

---

Terminal nine was halfway down the left-hand row, facing the wall. Marcus sat. Kira pulled the second chair in beside him and set her satchel on the floor between her feet.

"Two minutes," she said quietly. "Don't touch anything yet."

He did not touch anything. He sat with his hands flat on his thighs and watched her work.

She laid a thin gray case on the desk, no bigger than a paperback, and opened it, and connected it to the terminal's researcher port with a short cable she had made herself at the cottage table over three evenings. Elena's drive. Six terabytes. The core of Project Continuity, taken out of the data centre at Ashford in the middle of a fact storm, and the two hundred and fourteen statements from the border, and Isaiah's article. Everything they had. Then she brought up the terminal's submission interface, which was designed to let a visiting researcher propose a sourced fact for verification, and began, with a kind of cold, fast patience, to make it do something it had never been designed to do.

He watched her hands. He could follow perhaps a third of it. She was building a bridge, she had explained to him at the cottage, between the researcher's intake, which took one sourced entry at a time, and the ingestion queue behind it, which took anything. The pass authorised the intake. The intake would hand everything to the queue. The queue would hand everything to the primary. And the primary would do what it always did with a sourced fact from an authorised terminal, which was verify it, cross-reference it, and enter it.

*So that it happened*, he thought.

"There," said Kira at 14:08. "It's live. It'll carry the archive on its own." She turned her head very slightly toward him. "The statements are yours."

---

That had been the arrangement, and he had asked for it.

The archive could go in as a block; it was files, and files went in as files. But the statements were testimony, and testimony had to be entered as a historian enters things, one at a time, in the proper form, each with its source and its date and its witness, or the primary would reject them as unstructured. Kira could have written a script to do it. He had asked her not to.

"I want to type them," he had said, at the cottage, a week ago. "Every one. I know it's slower." The accounts themselves she had formatted in advance; what he would type was the entry, the header, the part that made each one a fact.

She had looked at him for a long moment across the table, and then said, "Yes. All right."

A historian and an architect, he thought now. Doing illegally, in a basement, with borrowed glasses and a dead woman's cable, the two jobs they had once done legally for the state. There was a kind of joke in it somewhere. He did not quite have the energy to find it.

At 14:10 he put his fingers on the keys.

*Statement 1 of 214. Border district 7, lakeshore sector. Witness: Albescu, R., resident. Date of events: 14 March 2091. Location: Harbour Street, residential block, full length. Account: Evacuation of the street at approximately 06:00 by Border Security personnel. Duration of evacuation: nine hours. Witness observed onset of disorientation among neighbours at approximately 08:30. Official record states no evacuation occurred on this date. Corroborating witnesses: statements 2, 3, 11, 40. Status: submitted.*

He pressed the key. The terminal accepted it. A small line of green text appeared beneath it and moved up the screen.

*Statement 2 of 214.*

He had typed perhaps thirty thousand fact entries in his life. He had typed them at desk 14-08 on the fourteenth floor of the Regional Factorepo for years; he had typed them in his head in Wing C, lying on his back in the dark, to keep a face alive in him that the white room wanted gone. He knew the format better than he knew his own handwriting. His fingers found it without him.

*Statement 7 of 214. Border district 7. Witness: Oduya, M., fisherman. Date of events: 22 October 2098. Account: A Border Security vehicle parked at the end of the witness's road at approximately 19:00, one hour before the onset of a fact storm, and departed at approximately 21:30, one hour after the storm subsided…*

And something happened in him that he had not expected, and had no field for.

He felt useful.

It came up through the tiredness and the fear like warmth coming back into cold hands. He had not felt it since August. He had not felt it, if he was honest, since October of the year before, since the last morning he had sat down at desk 14-08 believing that what he did there mattered. It was a simple, stupid feeling, the feeling of a man doing the job he was good at, and it filled him so completely that for a moment his eyes stung behind the borrowed frames and the screen swam more than the lenses could account for.

*Statement 31 of 214.*

He did not stop. He did not let it show. He typed.

---

At 14:21 the room was too bright.

It lasted less than a second. Marcus was in the middle of statement fifty-three, a woman in border district 4 who had watched her neighbour's house be a school and a warehouse and a field in the space of an hour in 2102, and he was typing the word *field*, and the light in the annex changed.

No. It did not change. That was the thing. Nothing changed. The white vault overhead was exactly as white; the terminal screen was exactly as bright; the children at the far end were still sitting on the floor. Nothing flickered. There was no second room behind the first, no pressure, no taste of metal, none of the things he knew in his body from the night of the storm and from the dreams that came after it. It was only a feeling, very faint and very brief, that the room was lit a little more than it ought to be. Like a word he almost remembered. Like a name on the tip of his tongue that went back down before he could catch it.

Then it was gone, and the room was the room.

He sat with his fingers on the keys. His heart was going hard.

He looked at Kira. She was watching the progress of the archive on the small inset window, her jaw set, her eyes moving. She had not looked up. If she had felt anything, she gave no sign of it. He did not ask. He could not have said, out loud, in that room, what he would have been asking.

*Fourteen twenty-one,* he told himself instead, in the dullest voice he had. *Annex. Brief perceptual anomaly. Brightness. No flicker. No contradiction. Duration: under one second. Cause: unexplained.*

And then, because he had promised Tanaka and because he had promised himself: *UNCHECKED.*

He finished the word *field*. He pressed the key. *Statement 54 of 214.*

---

The upload ran on.

At 14:26 the guard from the vestibule came strolling down the aisle between the terminals with his hands behind his back, the way guards stroll when they have been told to stroll, and Marcus kept typing and did not look up, and felt the man pass behind his chair and go on, and felt the back of his neck go cold and then warm again.

At 14:33 the school party got up from the floor and was led, in a long blue line, past the end of the row, and one of the children, a small boy with a very serious face, peeled off from the line and came and stood at Marcus's elbow and looked at his screen.

"What are you typing?" said the boy.

Marcus looked at him. Kira's hand, under the desk, had gone very still on her knee.

"History," said Marcus.

The boy considered this. "Is it true?"

"Yes."

"Our teacher says everything in there is true."

"She's right," said Marcus. "That's why I'm putting it in."

The boy nodded, satisfied, and ran back to his line, and the young woman at the head of it glanced round, smiled apologetically at Marcus, and led them out.

Kira let out a breath through her nose. It was not quite a laugh.

At 14:39 the guard strolled past again, the other way. At 14:40 the archive passed the three-quarter mark on Kira's inset window, and Marcus passed statement one hundred and fifty. His wrists had begun to ache. His eyes were watering behind the lenses. He was typing faster now than he had ever typed at desk 14-08, and more accurately, and he was aware of the clock in the corner of the screen as he had never been aware of a clock in his life, the half-past come and gone upstairs, a Deputy Minister waiting in her office for a man who did not exist.

*She said it would wait,* he told himself. *She said, in the message, that if we were late, she would wait.*

*Statement 171 of 214.*

At 14:47 Kira said, very low, without moving her lips, "Six minutes."

*Statement 203 of 214.*

---

At 14:52 the last statement went in. Two seconds later the inset window on Kira's side of the screen turned from gray to green, and a single line of plain text appeared in it.

*INGESTION COMPLETE. ENTRIES QUEUED FOR VERIFICATION: 6,214,338. STATUS: ACCEPTED.*

Neither of them said anything.

For three seconds they simply looked at each other, side by side at terminal nine in a quiet basement under a painted vault, a historian and an architect in borrowed clothes, both of them officially dead. Kira's face was very pale and very still. Her eyes were wet. He did not think he had ever seen her look so frightened, or so glad.

Then she reached out, and unplugged her cable, and closed the gray case, and put it in her satchel.

"Upstairs," she said.

---

The lift was at the back of the annex, as the guard had said. Mr. Harrow's pass opened it for level twelve. They stood side by side in the small steel box with the camera over the door and did not speak, and Marcus watched the floor numbers climb and thought about what he would say to her. *Thank you*, first. That was the only part he was certain of.

*Fourteen fifty-five. Level twelve.*

The doors opened on a corridor.

It was long, and carpeted in a deep blue that swallowed their footsteps, and lined on both sides with pale wooden doors, each with a small pale nameplate beside it. It was very quiet. Somewhere far away a phone was ringing and nobody was answering it.

The Deputy Minister's office was at the end, where the corridor turned. They could see it from the lift: a wider door than the others, with an aide's desk set across the corridor in front of it, the way aides' desks are set, like a small gate.

The aide's desk was empty. The chair was pushed in. The screen was dark. There was a cup on the desk, half full, and a pen lying beside a pad with nothing written on it.

The door behind it was shut.

Marcus looked at Kira. She looked at the door. He went round the empty desk and knocked, twice, not loudly.

Nothing.

He waited, and knocked again, and then, because he did not know what else to do, he tried the handle. It did not turn. The door was locked.

"She said she'd wait," he said quietly.

"She might have been called away." Kira's voice was flat and level. "It's a Ministry. People get called away."

"Her aide too?"

Kira did not answer.

They waited. It was the only thing they could do; to go looking for her in that building would have been insane. They stood by the aide's empty desk, a little apart, like two visitors who had arrived early, and watched the corridor. Nobody came. The phone somewhere stopped ringing, and after a while began again. Marcus looked at his watch. 14:56. 14:57.

Halfway back down the corridor, on the left, one of the pale doors stood a few inches ajar. He had not noticed it on the way in. There was no light behind it that he could see. But there was a smell coming from it, faint, green, cut: the smell of flowers, of stems freshly cut and standing in water, the kind of smell that did not belong in a corridor on a November afternoon. He noted it. He did not know why. It was simply there, in the carpeted quiet, and he was a man who noted what was there.

*Fourteen fifty-eight. Level twelve. Deputy Minister's office: door locked. Aide: absent. Office on left, ajar. Smell of cut flowers. No persons observed.*

At 14:59 Kira touched his sleeve.

"We can't stay," she said. "Not up here. Not with this on us." She meant the pass, and the faces, and the satchel. "We go. We send word. She'll understand."

He looked at the locked door for one more second. Then they went, not to the lift, with its camera, but to the stairwell beside it, and started down.

---

On the stairs he checked it.

He did it out loud, low, in order, the way he had learned to do it at the cottage, because it was the only way he trusted anything any more: one of them said what had happened, and the other said whether it was so. He had promised Tanaka. *Check your memories. Every one.*

"Thirteen forty-five," he said, on the twelfth-floor landing. "The side door. The steps down. The guard."

"Gray moustache," said Kira. "A cup. He said *very nice*."

"Terminal nine. Left-hand row."

"Facing the wall. Yes."

"Fourteen oh eight, you said it was live."

"Fourteen oh eight." Tenth floor. Their feet were loud on the concrete. "You started at fourteen ten."

"The boy. At fourteen thirty-three."

"*Is it true?*" said Kira. "You said yes."

"The guard, twice. Fourteen twenty-six and fourteen thirty-nine."

"Yes. Both."

"Fourteen fifty-two, complete. Six million two hundred and fourteen thousand—"

"Three hundred and thirty-eight. Yes."

Seventh floor. Sixth. He was breathing hard. "The lift. Level twelve. The desk empty. The door locked. I knocked twice and tried the handle."

"Yes."

"The door ajar on the left. Flowers."

"Ajar," said Kira. "Yes. Flowers. I noticed."

It matched. All of it. Every time, every place, every person, the whole long hour, told over by two people on a staircase, and not one thing that one of them said did the other deny. He had not realised until that moment how afraid he had been that it would not match.

*CHECKED*, he thought, and felt something in his chest ease that had been clenched since August.

There was one entry he did not say aloud: the brightness, at 14:21. He kept that where he had put it, under UNCHECKED, and did not ask. He would ask her tonight. He would ask Tanaka.

In his coat pocket, against his hip as he went down, was the photograph the Deputy Minister had sent with the pass: a torn page, typed, with a sentence on it, and on the back, in pencil, *Have you seen this before? A.S.* He had looked at it once, at the cottage table, and not been able to answer, and put it away. He would look at it properly tonight too. He would bring it up to her office next time, and tell her whatever he could.

---

They came out through a fire door at the bottom of the stairwell onto a side street, and walked quickly, not running, round the corner of the Ministry and out onto the boulevard.

It was a gray, ordinary afternoon. Traffic. The transit line overhead. People walking with their collars up. Marcus looked at his watch as they reached the corner, the way he looked at everything now, and it said 15:01.

Every screen on the boulevard changed at once.

The big public screens on the towers opposite, the small ones in the transit shelters, the advertisement panels along the pavement: all of them, in the same instant, went to the same plain white field with the same black text. Around them people stopped walking. A woman beside Marcus lifted her face to the tower opposite and put her hand over her mouth.

*NATIONAL NOTICE. PRIMARY REPOSITORY FAILURE. INTERVAL AFFECTED: 14:00–15:00. CITIZENS ARE ASKED TO REMAIN CALM. THE RECORD WILL BE RESTORED.*

Kira read it. He watched her read it. He watched the colour go out of her face all at once, as if someone had opened a drain, until she was whiter than the screen.

"Fourteen hundred to fifteen hundred," she said.

"Kira—"

"The upload." She was not looking at him. She was looking at the screen, and doing arithmetic, the way she always did arithmetic, with her whole body gone still. When she spoke again it was almost a whisper. "Marcus. The upload was *inside* the hour."

He looked up at the white field on the tower. At the numbers.

Two hundred and fourteen statements. Six million entries. Continuity's core, and Isaiah's article, and every witness on the border who had trusted them with their names. Every word he had typed at terminal nine with his wrists aching and his eyes wet behind a stranger's glasses, every entry that had gone in green.

*Status: accepted.*

Gone. With the rest of the hour. As if none of it had ever happened at all.
