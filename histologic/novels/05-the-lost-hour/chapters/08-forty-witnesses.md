# Chapter 8: Forty Witnesses

*Monday, November 15 – Wednesday, November 17, 2106. The streets of Veridica Capital, and the border zone*

---

The laundry's yard was eleven steps across, and Kira Osman counted every one of them in the dark.

Behind her, above her, the window of the rented room was still open. She had heard the boots on the stairs, and Marcus saying *go*, and his weight against the door, and then she had been over the sill and down the drainpipe onto the laundry's flat roof and down again, with the drive in an inside pocket of her coat and Singh's photograph in its envelope against her ribs. Four seconds. He had held the door for four seconds. She had used every one of them, and she had not looked back, because he had asked her not to, weeks ago, at the cottage, for exactly this.

*If they come for me, you go. You're the drive. I'm just the man who carried it.*

She had told him that was sentimental. He had agreed.

At the yard gate she stopped, and made herself breathe, and listened. No shouting behind her. No alarm. They had not come round the back. They had come for Marcus Chen, the dead man, the man with the face, and they had found him waiting on the other side of the door with his hands open. They would not start looking for a second dead person until someone told them there was one.

She went out into the lane, and turned left, and walked.

---

She had helped to build the city's eyes. That was the thing she held on to, walking.

Not the cameras themselves. She had never designed a camera. But she had spent six years on the sixtieth floor of the Spire writing the standards by which everything the capital saw was carried to everything the capital knew, and she knew, better than almost anyone alive, where the eyes were and how they talked to one another and where they did not quite meet. The camera masts on the transit lines synchronised to their relays in batches, and for a few seconds in every cycle the batch at the end of the line was talking and not looking. The listed buildings of the Archive District could not be drilled for mounts, so the district was covered from the rooftops opposite, and the covering stopped a metre short of every doorway. The river walk had been refitted in 2104 and the contractors had cut corners on the lighting, which she had reported at the time, in writing, and which nobody had fixed.

She walked through the capital on those gaps, one after another, like a woman crossing a stream on stones.

Her interface reported nothing to anyone. It had read zero since August. She was a dead woman in a dark coat with her hair pulled back and her collar up, and the city's eyes, where they saw her at all, saw a face that belonged to no living citizen and so, according to every system she had helped to build, was nobody's concern.

At some point after midnight she realised she was reciting. Under her breath, in time with her steps. *Layer one: key exchange. Asymmetric, lattice-based. Rotation interval ninety seconds.* The old compulsion. It had been fading for weeks. She stopped, and stood in a doorway on the river walk with her hand over her mouth, and waited until the words had gone back down into her, and went on in silence.

At a quarter past four, under the third arch of a railway bridge in the eastern goods yards, a car with its lights off was waiting where Elena had once told her one would wait, if it ever came to this. The driver was a woman Kira had never seen before. She did not ask Kira's name. She said, "Lie down in the back," and Kira did, and at dawn, with the fog coming up gray off the fields, they were on the eastern road out of the capital, and she was asleep.

---

The cottage was exactly as they had left it, which was the worst thing about it.

Two rooms and a stove. The window onto the lake. The steel box under the floorboards, empty now. The table by the window, with two chairs, and on the table a pile of paper held down with a mug.

Marcus's lists.

Not all of them. He had taken the current sheets to the capital, folded in his coat: he never went anywhere without them. These were the older pages, the ones he had copied out fresh and could not bring himself to throw away. August and September. CHECKED, UNCHECKED, HOPES, in his small, careful historian's hand, ruled into three columns with the edge of a book. *Aug 23. Border zone. With Kira. By choice.* was near the top of the first page. She could see it from the doorway.

She did not go any nearer to the table. She sat down on the floor by the stove instead, with her back against the wall and the drive in her lap, and did not look at his chair.

The news came in on Elena's receiver at eight. She listened to it once and then turned it off. Forty witnesses. The Deputy Minister. A face from the August exposé. *THE DEAD MAN WHO KILLED.* An Undersecretary called Pell, calm and reasonable, saying that the capital had a right to know how many more of these people there were.

She had been with him every second of the hour.

She had sat beside him at the public terminal in the annex while he typed the statements in, one by one, *Statement 1 of 214*, with the borrowed frames sliding down his nose. She had watched the progress bars. She had gone up the stairs with him at fourteen fifty-five and waited outside a locked door that smelled of cut flowers, and come down the stairs with him at fourteen fifty-nine, checking every step aloud. She was the only living person who knew where Marcus Chen had been at twenty past two.

And she was not living. According to the only record that counted, she had died on the second of August, of cognitive failure during a fact storm.

At noon a message came from Elena, in the shift. *NOT SAFE THERE. PICKUP 18:00. BRING WHAT YOU NEED.* She packed the drive and the envelope. At the last moment, with the car's engine already running outside, she went to the table after all and took the top sheet from the pile of lists, without looking at it, and folded it into her coat.

---

The safe room was under a feed merchant's shop in a lakeshore village, a whitewashed cellar with a camp bed and a table and a terminal wired through two relays and a very angry technician at the regional node whom Kira had never met. It had no window. That suited her.

Nikolai's feed began at ten on Tuesday night.

It came as he always sent things, in careful layers, with a covering note in the border-zone shift that his father had taught three boys under a green lamp.

*K. I HAVE ACCESS TO THE ERASURE DIAGNOSTICS AS PIETER. THIS IS THE RAW STRUCTURE OF THE HOLE, BOTH EDGES, ALL REPOSITORIES. I DON'T KNOW WHAT I'M LOOKING AT. I THINK YOU WILL. — N.*

She opened it.

For the first hour it was only what the whole country already knew, set out in the language she had spoken for most of her adult life. From 14:00:00.000 to 14:59:59.999 on the fifteenth of November, the primary repository held no entries. Neither did any regional repository, or any local store, or any of the three backups. The hash chains did not break at the edges of the hole. They did not show corruption. They simply went from 13:59:59 to 15:00:00 as if an hour had never been scheduled to exist, and every index that had ever pointed inward from either side had been rewritten to point somewhere else.

There were no contradictions. That was the extraordinary thing, and she sat back from the screen for a while and let herself admire it, coldly, the way you might admire a very clean wound.

A deletion that size should have left scars everywhere. A factorepo was not a list; it was a mesh of cross-references, every fact leaning on dozens of others, and to take out an hour of a nation you had to find every fact outside the hour that referred to anything inside it and quietly resolve it: a payment begun at 13:58 and completed at 14:03, a patient admitted at 13:40 whose next observation fell at 14:10, a train that left one station before two o'clock and arrived at the next after. Someone had found them all. Hundreds of thousands. Someone had tied off every one, like a surgeon closing vessels, so that nothing outside the hour bled.

Then she began to look at how they had done it, and she stopped admiring anything.

It was in the order.

Anyone closing a hole in a chain would work from the near edge outward. It was how the tools were built. It was how she had been taught, and how she had taught others. You started at the last good block before the gap and re-sealed forward. Whoever had done this had not. They had started at the far edge, at 15:00:00, and re-sealed *backwards*, towards the hole, block by block, like a person sewing a seam from the inside. And the tombstones they had left in the index, the empty markers that recorded where references had once been, were laid down not in the order the references had been written but in the order they had been removed.

It was not a technique. Nobody would teach it. It was a habit. It was the way one particular pair of hands had learned to do something, and did it every time without thinking, the way a person always ties the same knot.

She had seen it once before.

---

She had seen it a year ago, at her station on the sixtieth floor, in the days before Kade.

She had spent those days staring at the place where Security Protocol Update 7.3 had been. She had entered it herself at twenty-two hundred hours on the eighteenth of November. She had spoken it aloud and watched it verify green. And then it was gone, and there was no hole, and she had gone through the logs around it again and again looking for a scar, because there had to be a scar, because facts could not be deleted, and there was none. Only one small thing. The blocks after her fact had been re-sealed before the blocks before it. And the tombstones in the index were in the wrong order.

She had noticed it, and put it aside, because it was not a contradiction and could not be used as evidence of anything. It was a quirk. She had told herself it was a quirk in a maintenance process. Priya had said, *Kira, this doesn't make sense*, and she had agreed, and gone on looking for something that made sense, and within a fortnight she had been in gray.

She pulled up the two structures side by side. Her missing fact, from the notes she had carried in her head through eight months of Ashford, which the machine had never quite reached because she had hidden them inside the specification of her own key rotation. And the hour.

The same seam. The same backwards stitch. The same tombstones laid down in the order of removal.

The same hand.

Kira sat very still in the cellar under the feed merchant's shop.

She had spent the whole of Ashford being told that nobody could delete a fact. She had spent the months since being told that someone could, and trying to imagine who. Reeves had said, in the data centre, with his hands spread, *I stole her fact*. She had believed him. Some part of her had been glad to believe him, because it gave the thing a face she already hated.

But Reeves was a neurologist. Reeves worked with cradles and salience maps. This was not a neurologist's work. This was an architect's. This was someone who knew the factorepo the way she did, from the inside, from the core outward, and who had reached into it once, a year ago, and taken a single fact out of her life so that she would report its absence and be convicted for it. And who had now reached in again, and taken an hour out of a nation's.

The anger came up, and she let it, and it did not come up hot. It had not come up hot since the winter. It came the way it had learned to come in Wing C, like deep water under ice: very cold, and very still, and absolutely clear.

*Hello*, she thought. *I know your handwriting.*

---

The forty statements came on Wednesday evening.

Nikolai had copied them from a sealed Enforcement bundle that had reached the investigation that afternoon. She did not ask how. They came as images of the original interview transcripts, each one headed with the Enforcement Center's seal, the witness's grade and floor, and a time, and she read them the way she read logs: all of them once, fast, for shape, and then all of them again, slowly, for pattern.

*Witness 3. Records officer, grade four, twelfth floor. I saw him in the corridor at about twenty past two, walking towards the Deputy Minister's office. He was in gray. Like the patients in those photographs from the summer.*

*Witness 11. Administrative assistant, twelfth floor. It was maybe quarter past, maybe a bit after. Gray clothes. Hospital clothes. He had a bandage on his left hand. White.*

*Witness 17. Security liaison, eleventh floor, on the twelfth for a meeting. 14:22 or so. I noticed because I'd just looked at the time. Gray. Loose, like a uniform. His hand was wrapped. The left.*

*Witness 26. Clerk, twelfth floor. I think it was before half past. I didn't look. He was holding his hand like it hurt. The left one. And he was wearing gray, I remember that very clearly.*

*Witness 34. Senior archivist, twelfth floor. Twenty past, give or take. It was him, the man from the exposé. Gray. Wrapped hand.*

She laid them out. Then, because she could not think any other way, she built a table.

The times varied. Of course they did. Fourteen seventeen, fourteen twenty, *about twenty past*, *before half past*, *maybe quarter past*. That was right. That was what people were like. Human memory smeared time like a thumb across wet ink. Where the witnesses had stood varied: the corridor, a doorway, the lift lobby, a desk by the window. What he had been doing varied: walking, turning, reaching for the door. Some of them said he had looked at them. Some said he had not. Two said he had been carrying something, and could not say what. One said he had been in a hurry, and one said he had not.

The face did not vary. Every one of them had seen Marcus Chen.

The clothes did not vary. Every one of them, in forty different sets of words, had seen him in gray. *Gray, like a patient's clothes. Hospital gray. A sort of uniform, gray.* It was Wing C gray. She knew it the way she knew the colour of her own skin; she had worn it for eight months. And it was the gray from the photograph in Isaiah's article, the Continuity file image the whole country had seen in August and the news had shown again last night: a thin young man in cell clothes, looking into the camera with nothing behind his eyes.

Marcus had been wearing his dark coat. She had helped him into it at the rented room at one o'clock. It was the only coat he had.

And the hand did not vary.

*White bandage. Wrapped hand. A dressing on the left. Holding it like it hurt.* Every one of the forty mentioned it. Most offered it unprompted. A few had been asked about his hands and said, yes, now you mention it, the left one, bandaged. The wording drifted from statement to statement. The detail did not move at all.

His hands were unmarked. She had watched them on the keyboard for forty-two minutes.

---

She sat back, and did what she had done every day of her working life when a system produced an output that should not exist. She stated the problem.

*Input: forty independent observers. Condition: brief sighting, a corridor, a busy afternoon, a sudden death. Expected output: forty different accounts, varying in every particular, converging only on what was genuinely there.*

That was what people did. She knew it the way she knew load tolerances. You put forty honest people in a corridor and showed them a man for four seconds, and you got forty men. Some tall, some short. Some in blue, some in black. The ones who were frightened would make him bigger. The ones who had been thinking about lunch would not remember his face at all. People did not misremember the same way. They misremembered *differently*, each according to their own fault lines, and it was precisely because they misremembered differently that you could lay forty accounts side by side and find, in the overlap, the thing that had actually happened.

These forty did not overlap. They coincided.

The small things scattered, the way they should. The large things did not. The face, the clothes, and one small, strange, specific detail about a hand, down to which one.

There were two possible explanations, and she wrote them down.

*One: forty people lying in concert.* She looked at that for a while. Forty Ministry staff from three floors and six departments, coached overnight into a matching story, with none of them breaking and none of them overdoing it. It could not be organised in a day. It could not be organised in a month. And the statements did not read like lies. Liars agreed on the times. These people did not even agree on the time.

*Two: something gave forty people the same memory.*

She did not write anything after it. She did not need to. She was already in the data centre at Ashford, on the second of August, with the racks falling and Tanaka's blood on the floor and Reeves with his hands spread, smiling, delighted to be asked.

*There are carrier signals running in the broadcast network now, in three districts, very low, barely above noise. We're measuring baselines.*

*Erase, then seed.*

*Remove the doubt. Plant the loyalty. And the person never knows that anyone was there.*

---

It was nearly midnight when she saw the other thing, and she almost did not see it at all.

She was looking at the table she had built. Two columns for the two constant details. And she noticed, without at first understanding why she was noticing, that they were not the same kind of thing.

The gray was Marcus's. It was his photograph, his file, his face in his cell clothes, the image the country had been shown. If you wanted forty people to see Marcus Chen, that was the Marcus Chen you would give them. It fitted. It came from somewhere.

The bandage did not come from anywhere. It was not in his file. It was not in the photograph. It was not in Isaiah's article or on the news. Marcus had never, as far as she knew, had a bandaged hand in his life. If someone had been writing Marcus Chen into forty heads, why would they have given him an injury he did not have?

It was a small thing. It was the kind of thing a person noticed at the edge of a log and set aside. She had done that once before, a year ago, and it had cost her everything.

She could not finish the thought. There was nothing yet to finish it with. So she did what she always did with an unresolved reference. She wrote it down where she would see it.

*Bandage: whose?*

---

She took out the sheet she had folded into her coat at the cottage.

She had not looked at it all day. She looked at it now. It was the first page of his August lists, in his small hand, the three columns ruled with the edge of a book. CHECKED. UNCHECKED. HOPES. Near the top, under CHECKED: *Aug 23. Border zone. With Kira. By choice.*

She turned it over. The back was blank.

She thought of the only neural specialist in the world she trusted, in a hospital office on the far side of the lake, with a cup of tea going cold at her elbow. She picked up the pen, and wrote three lines, and read them back, and did not change a word. Then she photographed the page and sent it through the two relays and the angry technician and the long dark line under the lake, to Dr. Yuki Tanaka.

*Forty identical false memories. Same wrong clothes. Same wrong hand.*

*Someone didn't just erase the hour.*

*Someone* wrote *it.*
