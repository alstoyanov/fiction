# Chapter 26: Status: Living

*Wednesday, December 29, 2106. Registry Records Office 4; a Ministry flat, Veridica Capital*

---

The Registry office behind the Justice Center had four chairs, one window, one clerk and a queue of dead people.

Marcus stood in it with his coat buttoned and his hands in his pockets, and read the notice on the wall beside the door because he could not stop himself reading notices. *RECORDS OFFICE 4. AMENDMENTS TO REGISTRY STATUS. PETITIONERS WILL BE CALLED IN ORDER.* The notice was old. Someone had taped a new sheet of paper under it that morning, still curled from the printer: *PETITIONS UNDER THE RULING OF 27 DECEMBER (TESTIMONY) — THIS WINDOW.*

*Fact entry,* he thought, from habit. *December 29, 2106. Ten-oh-two. Registry Records Office 4. Involved parties: Chen, M.; Osman, K.; Volkov, D.; Volkov, A.; Volkov, N. Event: petition. Status:*

He stopped there. He did not know how to finish it yet. That was the point of being here.

Kira was in front of him. She had her hair up in its knot and her hands folded behind her back, and she was counting something; he could see it in the slight movement of her lips. Ceiling panels, probably. In front of her, Dmitri stood exactly as he had stood in every queue Marcus had ever seen him in, like a man on duty who had been told to wait and would wait until the building fell down. Alexei was talking to him in a low voice. Nikolai was at the head of the line, clean-shaven as he had been since the morning of the hearing, and he still kept touching his own jaw as though it belonged to somebody else.

Last week their faces had been on every screen in the capital. This morning the two other people on the chairs, a woman with a folder of property documents and an elderly man asleep with his chin on his chest, did not even look up. A Registry office does not expect the dead to walk in.

---

The clerk was a young man with careful hands and a tie he kept straightening. He had a printed form in front of him that he had plainly never used before, and he read each line of it aloud, slowly, as though the words might change if he went too fast.

"Petition for amendment of status," he said. "Subject: Chen, Marcus. Current status in the Correction Registry: deceased. Recorded cause: cognitive failure during fact storm. Recorded date: August second, 2106." He looked up at Marcus over the counter, and then down again at the form, and then up again. "You're him."

"Yes."

"I'm sorry. I have to ask. The form says—" He found the line with his finger. "*The subject's presence does not constitute evidence of the subject's status.*" He had the grace to look embarrassed. "It's the testimony that counts."

"I understand," said Marcus. And he did. He had spent years of his life entering facts that sensors and cameras had seen, and never once had it occurred to him that a man standing in front of him might not be enough.

"Witnesses?"

They came to the counter one by one. Kira first, because she had asked to go first. She gave her name and her own status, which was also *deceased*, and the clerk wrote it down without comment, because the ruling allowed it and the form had a box for it. Then, in the cadence Marcus had last heard in the middle of a storm in Wing C, the cadence of a woman entering a fact aloud:

"Kira Osman. December twenty-ninth, 2106. Ten-oh-six. Registry Office Four. I testify that the man standing beside me is Marcus Chen, and that he is alive." A pause. "I've been checking."

The clerk typed it. His hands were not quite steady.

Then Dmitri, who gave the date and the time to the second and his exposure on the fifteenth of November with the precision of a report. Then Alexei, who said, "It's him. I'd know that face anywhere. It looks worried." Then Nikolai, who said nothing unnecessary at all.

Then the door opened behind them, and a man in an enforcer's coat came in out of the cold, took off his gloves, and joined the end of the line.

Marcus turned round and looked at him.

Officer Kade was grayer than he had been in the room above the laundry, and much grayer than he had been in October 2105, in the interview room with the camera in the corner. He nodded to Marcus, the same courteous, unhurried nod.

"Mr. Chen."

"Officer Kade. Are you—"

"I was asked whether I'd attend," said Kade. "By the Director's office. I said I would." He stepped up to the counter and gave his name and his badge number to the clerk, who had gone slightly pale. "I arrested this man on the evening of November fifteenth," Kade said. "I interviewed him for four hours. I've arrested him twice, in point of fact. I testify that he is Marcus Chen, and that he is alive." He considered, and added, in the same matter-of-fact voice: "I had occasion to notice."

The clerk typed it. Then he pressed a key, and they waited.

---

The Judge did not take long. It never did.

The screen on the clerk's side of the counter was angled so that Marcus could see it if he leaned, and he leaned. Five witness entries, each with a small figure beside it: exposure on the day of the lost hour, contamination weighting, concordance. Dmitri's was the lowest. Kira's was higher, because she had been in the Ministry building for the hour. Kade's was lower than Kira's, because Kade had spent the fifteenth of November in the Enforcement Center across the city, outside the district's range.

Then a single line.

*Concordant testimony of five independent witnesses. Weighted. Probability that subject is living: 0.994.*

Marcus read the figure and something in him laughed, silently, a long way down. Fourteen months ago The Judge had looked at him and found him seventy-three per cent contaminated, and someone had typed the seventy-three. Now it looked at him and found him ninety-nine point four per cent alive, and five people had sworn to it.

The screen held for a second. Then the entry wrote itself into the record, and verified, and turned green.

*CHEN, MARCUS. STATUS: LIVING.*

He knew that green. He had watched it ten thousand times from desk 14-08, at the end of a traffic incident or a birth or a property dispute. *Verified. Entered. True.* He had loved it. He had not expected, ever, to see it next to his own name.

"That's the first," said the clerk, in a strange voice. He was looking at his screen, not at Marcus. "I've just checked. There's no precedent in the index. That's the first fact ever entered on testimony alone." He swallowed. "Congratulations, Mr. Chen. I don't know if that's what you say."

Marcus found that his knees had stopped working. There was a chair by the wall, and he sat down in it, next to the elderly man who was still asleep, and put his hands flat on his thighs, and looked at the floor.

*Fact entry,* he thought. *December 29, 2106. Ten-oh-nine. Registry Records Office 4. Chen, M. Status: living.*

He had not been able to finish his own entry for five months. He finished it now, in his head, and it held.

Kira came and stood in front of him. She did not say anything. She put one hand on the side of his face, very briefly, the way she would have checked a machine that had been running hot, and took it away again.

Behind her, Kade was putting his gloves back on. He looked at Marcus sitting in the chair, and his mouth did something that, on another man, would have been a smile.

"Mr. Chen," he said. "The Registry says you are alive."

Then he nodded to the clerk, and went back out into the cold.

---

After that it went quickly, because the form had been done once and now the clerk knew it.

Kira. *OSMAN, KIRA. STATUS: LIVING.* She watched it go green without any change of expression, then turned and looked out of the window at the wall opposite, and Marcus did not go to her, because he knew that face. It was the one she wore when a verification layer she had built finally passed.

The three brothers, one after another, each swearing to the other two and to the rest of them, the same face three times on the clerk's screen. When the third entry went green, Alexei put his arm round Nikolai's neck and pulled his head down, and Nikolai let him, and Dmitri stood beside them with his hands behind his back and his eyes shut.

Tanaka came through on the clerk's link from Chronos, sitting very straight in her office in front of a window onto a car park. She testified to all five of them in her clinical voice and they testified to her. *TANAKA, YUKI. STATUS: LIVING.* When it went green she took off her glasses and cleaned them, which was, Marcus had learned, what Tanaka did instead of crying.

"James sends his regrets," she said. "He has decided to remain deceased. He says it's restful."

Then she told them the rest. The Chronos court had dropped the trafficking charge against her at nine that morning, on the strength of Lia Santos's testimony and the ruling of the twenty-seventh. The Santos family's departure had been entered in the Veridican record as *voluntary*. Isaiah's crossing at the lakeshore checkpoint on the fifteenth of November had been affirmed as a lawful expulsion under escort, on the testimony of Captain Sokolov and two guards at the barrier. Marina Holt and Jakob Lind had been recorded as having *resigned their posts*. The phrase made Alexei laugh until he had to sit down.

The clerk had one more item, and he read it out carefully, because Marcus had asked him to check.

"Marsh, Hanna. Ionescu, Pavel. Status: deceased. Cause: cognitive failure during fact storm." He looked up. "You said the cause was wrong."

"They died in a crash," said Marcus. "On the lakeshore road. On the night of August first."

"A correction of cause is a separate petition," said the clerk apologetically. "It needs its own witnesses."

"We'll bring them," said Kira.

The clerk wrote it on a slip of paper and gave it to her, and she folded it and put it in her coat.

---

The last of the verdicts came in on Nikolai's comm, and he read it out without inflection.

Marcus Chen's conviction before the tribunal of December sixth was quashed. The order for his transfer to Secure Care Unit 3 was cancelled. And Dr. Reeves, on his own sworn confession of December twentieth and the concordant testimony of the Ministry witnesses, had been found guilty of the murder of Deputy Minister Amara Singh. He was also to be held on the charges arising from Project Continuity, which were separate, and which were real.

Nobody said anything for a moment.

Nikolai put the comm away. He looked at nothing. Marcus knew where he was looking. There was a young woman on Wei's team whose surname had just been read out as a murderer's, again, and Nikolai would be seeing her tonight.

*I remember it the way you remember your field.*

Marcus put that away too. Not today.

---

Old Songs had come in with them and sat on the fourth chair the whole time, in a coat of Elena's finding that hung off his shoulders, with his hands folded on his knee. He had watched each entry go green. He had hummed, very softly, a rising phrase, each time it did.

When the brothers were finished the clerk looked at his list and then at the old man.

"Petrov, Mikhail," he said. "You're on my list as well, sir. Deceased, August second." He cleared his throat. "If you'll come to the window, we can do yours now. These five can testify for you."

Old Songs got up, slowly, and came to the window.

"Thank you," he said. "That's very kind. No."

The clerk blinked. "Sir?"

"I've been dead to the record for forty years, one way or another," said Old Songs, gently, as if explaining something to a child he liked. "It suits me. The record can go on thinking I'm furniture."

The clerk opened his mouth. "Leave it blank," said Old Songs, and inclined his head, courteous as an old librarian, and turned away.

Alexei started to say something, and stopped. The clerk, finding no help anywhere, put a small mark in the margin of his list.

Marcus watched the old man sit down again on the fourth chair and fold his hands.

He thought he understood. What had survived the machine in any of them was what it had not thought worth looking at: a song, a spec, a primer, a meal-trolley tune. Old Songs had spent forty years making himself into something not worth looking at. He was not going to walk up to a window and ask the record to see him again.

What Marcus did not understand was why he would need to. Not now. They had won. The record had just been corrected, by people, in front of him. What was the old man keeping himself safe *from*?

He looked at Old Songs, and Old Songs looked back at him with his pale blue eyes that always seemed to be looking a little beyond, and hummed three notes, and the last one lifted, like a question.

---

The capital had heard.

They came out onto the steps behind the Justice Center at half past eleven, into a cold, bright morning, and there were people on the pavement who had not been there when they went in. Not a crowd. A few dozen. Somebody had seen something on a feed. A woman held up her comm to film them. A man in a delivery jacket called out "Welcome back!" in the tone of someone greeting a neighbour off a long trip, and then looked embarrassed, and then called it again.

On a screen above a café across the square, a Synthesis channel was running a clip of Isaiah at the mission in Chronos, with a caption under it that Marcus could read from where he stood: *THE DEAD ARE LIVING.* Further down, a Veridican news ribbon: *CAPITAL PROPOSES "DAY OF TESTIMONY" FOR NOVEMBER 15.* The Ministry had not taken long. It had decided, sometime between the twenty-seventh and this morning, that the Doctrine was its own.

They had nowhere they needed to be, for the first time in a year and a half, so they walked, the six of them, under the cameras, and Marcus kept waiting to be afraid of the cameras and was not.

The clerk had told him, as he left the window, that his interface would be read into the Registry again within forty-eight hours. *Telemetry resumed.* The Registry would know where he was. It would know his heart rate and his location and the steady hum of his cognitive status, the way it had known them every day since he was eight years old. He had expected that to feel like the white room.

It felt, instead, like being counted. Like his name on a roster.

At the corner of the plaza a man stopped in front of him, a stranger of about sixty with a scarf wound twice round his neck, and held out his hand. Marcus took it. The man shook it, hard, and did not let go, and his face worked, and then he was crying. He did not say why. He only held Marcus's hand in both of his own on the pavement with people walking round them, and said, "Thank you. Thank you," and then let go and walked away very fast without looking back.

Marcus stood on the pavement with his hand still out.

"You'll have to get used to that," said Kira, beside him.

"I don't know what I did."

"You didn't do anything," she said. "You're just the one they recognise."

---

The guards had gone from the landing that morning, and taken their chair with them. The flat had not changed at all, and it was completely different, because it was no longer a place they had been put. It was a place they could leave.

They did not leave.

Kira made coffee, black, no sugar, two cups, from the first packet she had been free to buy. Marcus found the heel of a loaf and some cheese and a jar of something Alexei had pressed on them that morning, and they ate it standing up in the kitchen, and then sitting on the floor with their backs against the couch, because the couch was too far away. Outside, the lights of the capital came on one district at a time, and at nine o'clock somewhere across the city somebody set off fireworks, which was illegal, and nobody came to stop them.

They talked about nothing: the clerk's tie, Kade, whether Alexei had really said *it looks worried*. Kira laughed, once, with her head back against the couch, a sound he had heard perhaps four times since Wing C, and he kept it.

*Fact entry,* he thought. *December 29, 2106. Twenty-one-oh-four. The flat. Osman, K. Event: laughed.*

For one evening they let themselves be happy. It was harder than he had expected. He kept reaching for the next fear, the way a hand goes to a pocket for keys it has already put away, and finding nothing there.

---

Later, when Kira had gone to shower, he took out his lists.

He kept them in the inside pocket of his coat, folded in four, the paper soft at the creases. He spread them on the kitchen table under the lamp and went down them, as he did every night, the way a historian goes down a day's entries before logging out.

At the bottom of the UNCHECKED column, in his own small hand, in pencil:

*T.W.?*

And on the next sheet, under HOPES:

*Please be wrong.*

He looked at them for a long time.

He thought of a hand resting on the edge of a bunk beside a paper cup of tea, and a voice saying *Another day in paradise*, and the blank smooth place in his memory where a face should have been. He thought of the rain at the checkpoint on the sixth of December, and the man in the wet coat holding a signed order up to the light, and the car afterwards, and his own voice saying *thank you* with everything he had. He thought of the hearing: the lead investigator standing up, calmly, *for completeness*, and the statements read into the record one after another, dozens of colleagues, all of them certain, *at his desk from half past one until the Ministry's call at twenty past three*.

Wei had testified. Wei had an alibi. Wei had walked into the rain for him when nobody else in Veridica would.

A bandaged hand and an office joke half the city used. That was all it had ever been. Lots of people cut their hands.

He picked up the pencil, turned it round, and erased *T.W.?* from the bottom of the column. He did it carefully, the way you correct an entry, until the paper was clean.

Then he looked at *Please be wrong*, and after a moment he put a small tick beside it. Answered.

Kira came back in with her hair wet and stood behind him and looked over his shoulder at the lists. She saw the clean place at the bottom of the column. He felt her see it.

She didn't say anything. She put her hand on his shoulder, and after a moment she said, "All right," in a voice he could not read, and went to make more coffee.

---

The knock came at a quarter to ten.

Marcus went to the door. For a moment his whole body remembered the stairs above the laundry and the four seconds he had held the door, and then he opened it and there was a courier in a Ministry coat on the landing, holding out an envelope.

"Mr. Chen? Sign here, please." Marcus signed, and the courier looked at the signature and then at him, and said, "Oh. Good evening, sir," and went away down the stairs.

The envelope was heavy, cream-coloured, with the seal of the Ministry of Historical Integrity embossed in the corner. Inside was a card, printed, formal.

*Minister Chen Zhao requests the honour of your presence, and Ms. Osman's, at the founding of the Historical Integrity Commission. Saturday, January 8, 2107. The Ministry of Historical Integrity. Seven o'clock.*

Clipped to the back of it was a smaller card, plain white, written in a neat, even hand, in blue ink.

*With gratitude for what you have restored.*

*— T. Wei, Technical Director (designate)*

Marcus stood in the doorway and read it twice. Behind him, in the kitchen, the kettle began to sing.
