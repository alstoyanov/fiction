Chapter 27: The Editor

Tuesday, January 4, 2107. The Sovereignty of Free Will mission, Chronos

The mission took eleven newspapers, and on the first Tuesday of the new year Isaiah Okonkwo read all of them before breakfast.

He read them at the long table in the press room on the ground floor, where the morning light came in off the harbour through tall windows that had no cameras in their frames. Seven weeks in the mission had not cured him of looking for the cameras. He looked every morning, in the corners and above the door, and found nothing, and felt the small drop in the stomach that a man feels when he reaches for a banister that has been taken away.

He laid them out in order of origin, the way he had once laid out the week's competitors at the Capital Review: Synthesis, the Sovereignty, Memoria, Axiom, the thin Chronos sheet printed for the district's interfaceless residents, and three from Veridica, a day late, brought across on the morning ferry in the mission's mailbag.

Every one of them led with the same story. That was the first thing he noted, and he filed it before he read a word.

VERIDICA LEARNS TO LISTEN. That was Synthesis, above a photograph of the Registry office behind the Justice Center, with a young clerk in a tie looking startled in the doorway.

TRUTH RETURNS TO THE PEOPLE. That was a Veridican paper, over a picture of Minister Chen Zhao shaking hands with Director Kovač on the steps. The Minister was smiling. The Director was also smiling, like a woman who knew exactly what her face was doing.

THE MACHINE CONCEDES. That was the Sovereignty's largest daily, and underneath it, in the measured voice of the paper's senior commentator, three columns on how the Lost Hour Doctrine had proved, at last and from inside, what the Sovereignty had always said: that no machine can hold the truth of a people, and that when the machine fails, the people must be asked. Isaiah read it twice for the argument and a third time for the adjectives. The commentator called the Doctrine a defeat. He called it a confession. He called it, in the final paragraph, the first crack in the Histologic wall.

Memoria called it humane. Axiom published a proof that it was an error. Chronos called it regrettable, in one paragraph on page four.

And on the third page of the second Veridican paper, below the fold, in a column of short items, a line that nobody else had thought worth leading with: DAY OF TESTIMONY PROPOSED. Ministry sponsors annual reading of citizens' recollections, November 15.

Isaiah sat back.

He did not feel what the papers felt, and it was not a new feeling. He had felt it in 2104, in a border town, standing on a pavement while a storm arrived all at once in every head in the street, and every official voice afterwards had said natural. It was not suspicion, exactly. It was the feeling of a room in which everyone has agreed on something very quickly.

Everyone is saying the same thing, he thought. They are saying it in eleven papers, from six countries, which have agreed on nothing else in forty years.

He filed that too. Then he went upstairs to write.

He had a room on the third floor at the back, with a bed and a desk and a window onto the wall, and beyond the wall, the quarantine district's narrow streets running down toward the water. The mission had given him a terminal, which he used, and a stack of plain paper, which he used more. For the first week he had not been able to write on it at all. His hand had kept reaching for the burned match. He still carried the match, wrapped in a strip of newsprint, in the inside pocket of his coat. He did not examine why.

He wrote the column by hand first, as he had written everything since Unit 3, and then typed it, and read it aloud to himself with the door shut, striking out each word that did not need to be there. He struck out a great many. The first draft had been angry. Anger was a parlour thing: warm, true, harmless, and exactly what a reader expected. He took it out.

What was left, at eleven o'clock, fitted on two pages.

In November, Veridica learned that memory can be written.

This is not an accusation. It is a finding. For one hour on the fifteenth of November, a broadcast node in the Ministry district of Veridica's capital carried a signal that placed the same false details into the memories of forty people. A clinician has described how it was done. The engineer whose calibration was used has recognised his own work. The Ministry itself, after a month of denying it, no longer denies it. Everyone who has looked at the evidence agrees.

Last week, Veridica made memory the law.

On the twenty-seventh of December The Judge ruled that where the record is absent, facts may be established by the concordant testimony of multiple independent witnesses, weighted for exposure to known contamination. This was a just ruling. I say so plainly, because I have been asked by several friends to say that it was not. Six people who were legally dead are alive this morning because of it. A family of three who chose to leave their country are recorded, correctly, as having chosen. I am recorded as having been expelled, rather than as having run away. I am grateful. I would be a liar if I wrote anything else.

But I have spent twenty years learning to notice when a story is finished too quickly, and this one was finished in a week.

A nation that cannot trust its record has chosen to trust its memory in the same season it learned that memory can be written. We should be asking who is best placed to write it.

The ruling weights testimony "for exposure to known contamination". Known to whom? The contamination of the fifteenth of November is known because a handful of people, most of them officially dead at the time, went looking for it. The next contamination will be known only if someone looks again. The Doctrine assumes that we will always be able to tell which witnesses were reached. I do not know why it assumes this. Nobody has told me.

I am not arguing that the Doctrine should be undone. The pure record, which cannot be questioned, is worse. I am arguing that a law which makes memory into fact has made memory into something worth owning, and that the people most able to own it are the people who have already shown that they can.

Who can write memory? Who has the calibration, the nodes, the access, the time? I do not know. I have asked, and I will go on asking. I would like someone to find out before the first Day of Testimony, and not after it.

He read it through twice more, and added nothing.

It was, he thought, the least angry thing he had ever written. It was also the only thing he had written since November that the Sovereignty would not want.

He sent it to the desk at twelve.

The envoy came up at a quarter past three.

He knocked, which he always did, and waited to be asked in, which he always did, and sat in the second chair with his hands on his knees and his silver beard trimmed very close for the new year. He had brought no tea. Isaiah noticed that, and filed it.

"The desk has read your piece," the envoy said. "I have read it too. I asked to."

"And?"

"And it is very good." The envoy said it simply. "It is the best thing you have written for us. The desk editor wept a little, she tells me, at the part about the family. She has a daughter of that age." He looked at the window. "She will not be running it."

Isaiah waited.

"You understand the difficulty," said the envoy. "I think you understood it when you wrote it." He spread his hands, the small placing gesture Isaiah had seen in November, as if setting something invisible on the table between them. "For forty years the Sovereignty has said one thing to the world. That truth cannot be mechanised. That a people is not its record. We have said it in parliaments and in the pages of these papers and at the edges of minefields, and for forty years the Histologic nations have laughed at us. Now the oldest of them has stood in front of its own machine and said: where the record fails, ask the people." His voice did not rise. It warmed. "Do you know what that means to our readers, Mr. Okonkwo? It means that we were right."

"It means Veridica has a new instrument," said Isaiah.

"It means Veridica has conceded," said the envoy. "That is the story. Your piece says the concession is a weapon. Perhaps it is. Perhaps in ten years we will all be reading your column again and wishing we had printed it. But this week, if the Sovereignty's most famous witness writes that the Doctrine is dangerous, then Veridica's Ministry will quote you by Thursday. They will say that even the Sovereignty's own man agrees: memory cannot be trusted, and the record must be restored." He paused. "You would be handing your enemies their argument. I don't think you want that."

It was, Isaiah thought, a good argument. It was an editor's argument. He had heard a version of it from his own editor at the Capital Review in 2099, about harbour contracts, and had published anyway, and had been right, and a man had lost his job.

"The piece doesn't say the record must be restored," he said.

"No. It says we should be asking who is best placed to write memory." The envoy smiled faintly. "Which is a question with no answer. You say so yourself. You end on it."

"I always end on it."

"I know. It is the thing people most admire in your work." The envoy stood. "And it is the thing that makes a desk editor nervous in a week when she needs an answer. I'm sorry, Mr. Okonkwo. The piece is spiked. It remains yours, of course. We would never presume to tell you what to do with your own words."

He went to the door and stopped there, as he had stopped in November.

"Your papers are being renewed at the end of the month," he said. "A formality. The committee meets on the twenty-seventh. I mention it only so that it doesn't come as a surprise." He inclined his head. "The committee thinks very highly of you."

The door closed behind him.

Isaiah sat for some time with his hands folded on his knee.

The man had not threatened him. He was quite sure of that. He had been threatened by professionals, and this had not been it. The envoy had simply told him three true things in a row: that the piece was good, that it would not run, and that his asylum was to be reviewed by a committee which thought highly of him. He had left it to Isaiah to put them in order.

A witness who embarrasses his hosts, Isaiah thought, is a witness who can be un-hosted.

He did not need to write that down. He wrote it down anyway.

At dusk he walked the house.

He did it lying on the bed with his eyes shut and his shoes still on, as he had done it on a concrete bed in Unit 3 and a steel bed in 7-E. The house on Carver Street opened round him, room by room: the parlour, bare since August, where his aunt had once sat at the head of the table over the pepper soup; the kitchen, with its sources; the study, where the drawers were filed by year and 2106 was the fullest drawer he had ever had to close.

He went down the cellar stairs.

The door at the bottom had no colour. He had stopped trying to remember what it had been. He put his hand on it, as he always did, and went in.

He had cleared a shelf for this in November, and it was still almost empty. He walked to it and looked at what was there.

A typed page, torn across the top, as Kira had described it to him. When the record fails, the people will believe whoever restores it. Arrange to be the one who restores it. A numeral in the corner that meant nothing to him, and a small typed mark at the foot, blurred past reading.

Beside it, the pieces of a sentence Kira had half-remembered from an old man's cracked voice, to a tune that had come through the meal slot of Wing C three times a day: a people that has lost part of its past … any past it is given … gently … by someone it trusts.

Two fragments. He had put them side by side in November and felt them fit, the way two pieces of a torn letter fit along the tear.

He looked at what else was on the shelf now.

A record that had failed, on the fifteenth of November, in every repository and every backup at once.

A people that had lost an hour of its past.

A doctrine, restored to them by The Judge, allowing them to fill that hour, and every hour like it, with whatever they remembered.

And a restorer. He could not see its face. He could only see where it stood: at the front of every photograph in every paper on the long table downstairs, gently, trusted, the kind of hands a frightened people would accept anything from. The Ministry that had sponsored Continuity was sponsoring a Day of Testimony. The Minister who had signed the order that saved Marcus was shaking hands on the steps. The papers of six nations had agreed, in a week, on one word, and the word was returned.

One fragment is a curiosity, he thought. Two is a pattern.

He stood in the cellar and made himself say the rest of it, because it was his rule, and a rule is worthless if it is only kept when it is convenient.

A pattern is not a proof. I have two. I will wait for three.

He did not know who had written the page. He did not know who the restorer was, or whether it was one person or a building or a design. He knew only that everything on the shelf was consistent with everything else on it, and that this was exactly the feeling he had spent twenty years learning not to trust in a story he had not finished.

He left the cellar and climbed the stairs and opened his eyes, and it was dark in the room.

The letter came up with the evening post, in a plain envelope that had been opened and resealed at least twice, by the look of it, on its way from the lakeshore. The mission's clerk apologised for its condition. Isaiah told her it was in excellent condition for a letter from where it had come from.

Inside was a single sheet, folded once. The handwriting was Kira Osman's, small and square, an engineer's hand, every letter the same height. At the top she had written: Dictated by M.P., 2 January. He says to send it exactly as he said it and not to improve it.

Under that, one line.

The one who stays is the one who remembers. You stayed. Keep remembering.

Isaiah read it three times, the third time aloud, very quietly, to the room.

He had last heard the first sentence through the book-slot of 7-E, late on a July night in 2106, from an old man bent down beside an empty meal trolley. That was always my job. Forty years of it. Now it's yours. He had not known then that the old man was keeping more than names. None of them had. It had taken a torn page and a Deputy Minister's photograph and an afternoon at a farmhouse stove for anyone to find out what the tune through the meal slot had been carrying.

You stayed.

He had stayed in Wing C so that the story would come out as testimony instead of a fugitive's forgery. He had been right. It had cost him three months in concrete and the colour of a door. Now he was sitting in a room with no cameras, in a house that was not his, in a country that did not want him, being paid by a nation that needed him to say one thing, and he had written the other thing, and nobody was going to read it.

Keep remembering.

He understood that it was not comfort. The old man did not deal in comfort. It was an instruction, and like all of Mikhail Petrov's instructions it had been folded very small, so that it could pass through a meal slot, or a mailbag, and come out whole on the other side.

He did not throw the column away. He did not send it to Synthesis, which would have run it, or to the resistance, which would have pasted it on walls. He thought about both for most of an hour, and understood, at the end of the hour, that both of them were ways of being right in public, and that being right in public was not what the piece was for.

He took a fresh sheet of the mission's paper and copied the column out again by hand, all of it, the two pages, in the small steady print he had learned in the Carver Street kitchen at his aunt's elbow. It took him forty minutes. A typed page could be retyped by anyone. A page in his hand was a witness.

When he had finished he folded it in three and wrote a covering note on a smaller sheet, and dated it, because he dated everything.

IO. 4 JANUARY. CHRONOS.

Marcus,

The Sovereignty has declined this. They are right to, from where they stand, and I am not going to publish it anywhere else.

Don't publish this. Read it at the Commission. Then decide who you are.

I.

He put both sheets in an envelope, and sealed it, and addressed it by the route Kira had given him in December, the one that went across the lake by hand and not by any line that anyone could read.

Then he sat for a while at the desk with his hands folded, looking out at the wall and the dark streets beyond it running down to the water, where a ferry was coming in late, with its lanterns low.
