Chapter 15: The Doctrine

Wednesday, December 1, 2106. Ministry of Historical Integrity, Veridica Capital

At ten past six in the morning, in her office at Ashford, with the pond outside the window still black and the fountain switched off for the winter, Helena Kovač wrote the problem down on paper, because that was where she wrote things she did not want adjusted.

She wrote it as an agenda. She had always found that fear was easier to look at in numbered items.

1. On Monday, December 6, a tribunal will rule on the Deputy Minister's death.

2. The only evidence is forty statements by Ministry staff who saw Marcus Chen enter her office at 14:20.

3. Mr. Chen was in the basement annex at 14:20. I know this because I knew he would be. Nobody will ask me.

4. The record for that hour does not exist. The Judge cannot rule on what is not there. The tribunal will therefore rule on the statements alone, and the statements are unanimous.

5. The statements are unanimous because they were written.

She stopped there and looked at the fifth item for a while. Then she took off her glasses and rubbed her eyes, and put them on again.

It had been Yuki's note that did it. Eight days ago, through the channel Kovač was not supposed to have, a single page in Dr. Tanaka's hand, as precise as a case file: Forty identical false memories are not confabulation. Confabulation is individual. This was a broadcast. Morrison recognises the calibration. Helena, the people at the edge of the node's range would have received a partial signal. They would remember the hour slightly differently from one another, and each of them would be sure.

Kovač had read that and sat very still, because she had been in a room of twelve people at the edge of the Ministry district's range on the afternoon of November fifteenth, and she had listened to them disagree about the time of a recess, each of them certain. She had been certain too. She had contradicted a clerk she would have trusted with her life.

She had not slept properly since. That was not new.

6. There is no category in Veridican law for "seeded". A witness is a witness. Forty is forty.

7. One dead man. No defence the law can hear.

Under that she drew a line, and under the line she wrote, more slowly:

The question is not whether Mr. Chen did it. The question is what the law does when the record is gone.

She wrote it out properly then, in the form a Ministerial referral would take, because she knew the forms: a single question, to be put before The Judge. Where the factorepo holds no record of an event, may the testimony of witnesses establish a fact? If The Judge said no, the tribunal would convict on forty statements that were not evidence of anything except a broadcast. If The Judge said yes, then testimony would have to be weighed, and weighing meant asking who each witness was, and where each had been, and what could have reached them. Forty clerks in one building against the three Volkov brothers, whose bond had once defeated the whole of Continuity. Against Captain Sokolov, who had been at the lakeshore at 14:30, well outside the district. Against a family of three who had been in a field with a gap in its fence.

She read the page through. It was good. It was the best thing she had written since the report.

And the first thing it would establish, if it worked, was that the Volkov brothers were alive. And the second was that the Director of Ashford had certified their deaths.

She pushed her glasses up her nose, folded the page into her inside pocket, and went to be questioned.

The inquiry sat in the fourth-floor hearing room, eight floors below the office where the Deputy Minister had died.

The corridor outside smelled of floor polish and the lilies somebody had put on a table by the lifts in the week after the fifteenth, which nobody had yet had the heart to throw away. Lucian Bell was sitting on a bench beside them with a folder on his knees. He looked as though he had lost weight he could not spare. An inquiry clerk was taking his statement standing up, reading back from a tablet, and as Kovač passed she heard Bell say, in a voice worn very thin, "No. I don't recall making it. I would have recalled. I make all her entries, and I don't recall it."

The clerk said something she didn't catch.

"I know how it sounds," Bell said. "I'm telling you what I remember."

Kovač did not slow down. She filed it.

The hearing room was the same room as on the fifteenth: a long table, a lower table facing it, the window at one end onto the gray inner courtyard, and the white Ministry clock at the other. Twelve chairs for the public, four of them occupied. Undersecretary Iver Pell sat at the end of the long table, not on the panel and not quite off it, with his hands folded on a single sheet of paper.

Inquiry Chair Halvorsen opened the session at ten exactly, with the usual courtesy.

"Director. Thank you for your continued patience. We have a new matter to put to you this morning. It arises from the Deputy Minister's calendar." A tablet was turned to face her across the gap between the tables. "On November eleventh, the office of the Director of Ashford Correctional Facility requested a border transfer slot for the expulsion of the prisoner Isaiah Okonkwo. The slot requested was 14:30 on November fifteenth, at the lakeshore checkpoint." A pause. "Is that correct?"

"Yes," said Kovač.

"Your office requested that slot."

"My office requested it. It was the slot we were offered."

"Offered by whom?"

"By the scheduling system. My clerk was told it was the only one available." She said it flatly, and heard, as she said it, how thin it was. She had not thought to ask, at the time, who had made it the only one available. She had been grateful for it. It had meant that every piece of the day would happen at once, and that the Ministry would be looking the other way.

"The only one available," Halvorsen repeated, and made a note. "So. At 14:30 on November fifteenth, the Director of Ashford was to release a prisoner across the border. Whether she did, the Ministry disputes. At approximately the same time, eight floors above this room, the Deputy Minister was killed. The Deputy Minister, I would remind the record, had an open inquiry into Project Continuity, which operated in the Director's facility." The Chair looked up. "You were in this building, Director. In this room."

"I was in this room," Kovač agreed. "So were you, Chair."

There was a small sound from the public chairs that might have been a laugh. Halvorsen did not smile.

"The panel draws no inference. It notes the coincidence."

"It is a coincidence," said Kovač. "I requested a slot for a lawful expulsion order. I was offered one. I took it. The order was carried out by Border Security under escort, and I imagine the escorting officer can say so."

"No doubt." Halvorsen turned a page. "Undersecretary. You had a question."

Pell unfolded his hands. He had a clipped voice that carried without being raised. He did not look at her while he spoke; he looked at his sheet of paper, as if reading from it, though Kovač could see from where she sat that it held only three lines.

"Director. In August you certified six deaths. The six escaped Continuity subjects, recorded as cognitive failures during an interior fact storm. On the night of the storm. Without family viewing, and without independent examination." He looked up then, briefly. "Since November fifteenth, the Ministry has been told by forty witnesses that one of those six walked into the Deputy Minister's office. And the Enforcement Center holds a man who answers to his name and has his face. I'd like to ask you, simply, whether those deaths are also in doubt."

Ten, Kovač thought. I certified ten. He had counted only the ones that were useful to him.

"The deaths are recorded in the Registry," she said. "I signed the certificates. I know exactly what they say."

"That isn't what I asked."

"It is what I can answer." She held his eyes over her glasses. "If the Undersecretary wishes to dispute a certified entry, there is a procedure for it. I would be glad to see it followed."

Pell looked at her for a moment longer, with something that was almost appreciation. Then he folded his hands again.

"Thank you, Director," he said. "That's very clear."

It went on for another hour. She answered precisely and gave nothing away, as she had for eleven sessions. But somewhere in the middle of it, while Halvorsen was reading the dates of her certifications aloud into a record that would once more be kept only in the room's own minutes, she understood what was being done.

Every fact they had put to her was true. Her office had requested the slot. She had been in the building. She had certified the deaths. Singh's inquiry had been into her facility. None of it was invented, and none of it could be denied, and laid out side by side, in that order, it made the shape of a woman who had arranged a prisoner's release to coincide with a murder and then lied about the dead to cover what the dead were doing.

Someone is building a case against me, she thought, out of real facts, arranged.

It was exactly the method she would have used.

At the recess she went into the corridor, past the lilies, and called her clerk at Ashford from the landing where the signal was worst.

"The border slot for Mr. Okonkwo," she said. "The offer that came in on the eleventh. Not our request. The offer. I want the original, with every field on it, including the ones the system doesn't display. On paper. Today."

"Is something wrong, Director?"

"Yes," said Kovač, and rang off.

The Minister received her at three.

She had asked for twenty minutes, on the telephone, through two secretaries, and had expected a week. His office had called back within the hour. The Minister would be delighted.

The Minister's office was on the top floor, at the end of a corridor so quiet it seemed carpeted even where it was not. It was not large. It had a desk with nothing on it but a closed folder and a pen, two armchairs by the window, a low table, and the whole of the capital laid out beyond the glass in the early winter dusk: the Ministry district, the river, the Spire. There were no pictures on the walls. It was the office of a man who did not need to be reminded of anything.

Minister Chen Zhao rose to meet her. He was sixty-five, silver-haired, slight, with a courteous stoop and the kind of face that looked as though it had spent a long life listening. He took her hand in both of his.

"Director Kovač. Sit, please. You'll have tea."

It came in glasses, in small metal holders, the way people had drunk it in the capital when Kovač was a girl. He held his by the rim, between finger and thumb, and did not seem to feel the heat.

"You've had a hard autumn," he said. "I've been reading your inquiry's minutes. You're very good at it. I don't mean that as a compliment, exactly. It's an observation."

"Thank you, Minister."

"What have you brought me?"

She had rehearsed it in the car. She took out the folded page and did not give it to him; she read from it, in the voice she used for committees. The absence of the record. The tribunal on Monday. The position of the law, which had never imagined an hour with no facts in it and therefore had no instrument for one. The single question she proposed be referred to The Judge: where the factorepo holds no record, may testimony establish a fact? And if it could, then testimony must be weighed, witness by witness, for what each had been exposed to, as The Judge already weighed every citizen's exposure every day.

She did not say seeded. She did not say broadcast, or Continuity, or Phase Two. She did not mention the photograph of a torn page that Ms. Osman had described to her, over the same forbidden channel, a week ago, or the sentence on it. She had decided that in the lift, without quite knowing why. She had simply found, on the way up, that she did not want any of it in this room.

When she finished, the Minister was smiling. Not politely. With open, unguarded pleasure, like a man who has been handed something he did not know he wanted.

"A doctrine of testimony," he said. "How very humane." He turned the tea glass a quarter-turn in its holder. "How very restorative."

Kovač felt the word go past her, and then felt it come back. She did not know why. It was an ordinary word. It was the kind of word ministers used.

"It would restore the possibility of a verdict," she said carefully. "At present there is none."

"Yes. Yes, exactly." He set the glass down. "You know, I've been following young Chen's case myself. Quite closely." He smiled at her expression. "Oh, not officially. It's a family weakness. The Chen families of the capital are all related somewhere, you know. My grandmother used to say there were only nine of us to begin with, and we've been marrying each other's cousins ever since." He waved a hand. "He's a historian, I gather. A good one, before all this."

"So I understand."

"And you think he didn't do it."

It was not quite a question. Kovač chose her words the way she chose where to put her feet on ice.

"I think the tribunal will not be able to know whether he did, Minister. I think it will convict him anyway. And I think the law ought to have something better to offer than that."

He looked at her for a long moment with his head a little on one side. Then he nodded, once, as though she had confirmed something.

"I'll refer it," he said.

She had come prepared for an argument. She had three more pages in her bag: precedent from the old pre-Histologic courts, a draft weighting schedule, a list of objections and her answers to them. She had expected him to take it under consideration. She had expected to be thanked and shown out and never hear of it again.

"Minister?"

"I'll refer it to The Judge. On the question exactly as you've put it. The date will depend on the Judicial Office, and on how loudly the Undersecretary shouts, but I'll sign the referral this week." He smiled again. "You look surprised, Director."

"I am, a little."

"Don't be. It's a good question. The best questions always sound obvious once someone's had the courage to ask them." He rose, and so she rose. At the door he took her hand again in both of his, warm and dry. "Thank you for bringing it to me. I think it may turn out to be very important. For all of us."

Walking back down the quiet corridor, Kovač found that her hands were in her coat pockets and that she was holding the folded page so tightly the edge had cut her palm.

It had been far too easy. She had spent eleven years in charge of a building where nothing she wanted had ever been easy, and she knew the feeling of a door that opens before you have touched it. It meant that someone on the other side had been waiting for you to knock.

Pell was at the lifts.

He had not been there when she went in. He was standing with his back to the lilies, reading something on his comm, and he did not look up until she had pressed the button.

"Director." He put the comm away. "You've been to see the Minister."

"I have."

"He likes you." Pell said it without any particular emphasis, as a man reads out a weather report. "He likes most people. Enjoy it."

The lift was taking a long time. Kovač watched the numbers.

"Did you know," Pell said, in the same tone, "that the Deputy Minister opened a second file in October?" He waited until she looked at him. "Not on Continuity. The first file was Continuity. The second was on you. On you, and on Ashford." He tilted his head slightly. "It's not in her office. It's not in the Registry. It went wherever her files went when she died. But I saw the request, because I sign for the requests. October the twentieth."

The lift arrived. The doors opened on an empty car.

"Why are you telling me?" Kovač asked.

"Because the Minister likes you," said Pell, "and I don't. And one of us ought to be honest with you." He stepped back to let her in. "Good afternoon, Director."

She sat in her car in the Ministry's underground garage in the same bay she had parked in on the fifteenth, with the engine off and the lights off, and did not start it.

A second file. On her. Opened in October by a woman who had then asked Marcus Chen to walk into her office on a pass in a false name, and been killed, in the hour Kovač's office had booked a prisoner across the border.

Real facts, arranged. She could see the rest of the arrangement now, the way you see the rest of a staircase once you have found the first step in the dark. And she could see, very clearly, that she would not be able to prove where any of it had come from, because everything that could have proved it was in an hour that no longer existed.

Her comm lit up on the seat beside her. It was the resistance channel: the one Nikolai had built into her personal device in August, disguised as a pharmacy reminder service, which she was not supposed to have and had never once used first. The message was in plain text. Whoever had sent it had not bothered to be careful.

YOUR OFFICE BOOKED ISAIAH'S SLOT. YOUR BUILDING. YOUR DEAD. TELL ME WHY I SHOULD TRUST YOU. — A.

She read it twice.

She thought about the clerk at Ashford, pulling the original offer out of the scheduling system. She thought about Bell on the bench saying I don't recall making it. She thought about the Minister's warm dry hands, and the word restorative, and why a perfectly ordinary word should have gone past her and come back.

She had answers. She had a great many answers, in numbered items. She did not have one she could prove.

She sat in the dark with the comm in her lap, and did not reply.
