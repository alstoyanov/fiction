Chapter 12: The Singer

Wednesday, November 24, 2106. The resistance safe house, the border zone

The stove in the farmhouse kitchen had a crack in its door, and the old man had learned in three months exactly how far to open the damper so that the crack glowed and did not smoke.

He stood at it now with a wooden spoon, stirring a pot of barley and onions that did not need stirring, and hummed.

It was the Lakeshore Air. It was nearly always the Lakeshore Air. The boys teased him about it, Alexei did, and said he had one tune and a great many moods, and he let them, because it was true, and because it did not matter what they thought a tune was for. He hummed it low, under the noise of the pot, and every phrase went up at the end, the way it always had, the way it had when his grandmother sang it on the pier at home with the fog coming in: a small lift, like a question left open for someone else to answer.

Nobody had answered it yet. He had been waiting sixty years.

Outside, the afternoon was going. The window over the sink looked across a yard of frozen mud to the barn, where Elena kept the trucks and the brothers kept their arguments, and beyond the barn to a line of bare poplars, and beyond the poplars to the lake, which today was the colour of pewter and very still. The fog would come in at dusk. It always did. Real fog, ordinary weather. He had lived beside it for most of his life and he still liked it, the way you like an old dog that has never once done what it was told.

He heard the car before he saw it.

Kira came in with the cold on her coat and a satchel under her arm, and did not take the coat off.

She had been at a safe room further east since the day after the hour, working, Elena said, the way she worked, which was without stopping. He had not seen her in nine days. She looked as she had looked in Wing C in the first week, when she had stood at the glass of 7-K and counted everything: thinner, very straight, the knot of her dark hair pinned tight, her face pale and completely level. He had learned in Wing C what that levelness meant. It meant that something under the ice was moving very fast.

"Mikhail," she said.

He nodded. He did not stop humming. It was a greeting, and she knew it.

She sat at the kitchen table and opened the satchel and began to lay things out, in rows, the way she laid out everything. He watched her from the stove.

A thick sheaf of paper, forty sheets or near it, which he knew without being told were the statements. He had heard about the statements. Everyone in the farmhouse had heard about them, and about the man they described, who was in a cell in the capital and could not be reached. A single page on stiff Ministry paper, printed, with a column of times down one side: the page Marina had found in her box of files. And a photograph, small and slightly curled, the kind a person carries in a coat pocket for a week without quite looking at it.

She put the photograph at the end of the row, by itself.

The door from the yard opened again and the brothers came in, Alexei first, talking, and Dmitri behind him, not. Alexei saw Kira and stopped talking, which was rare, and came round the table and put a hand on her shoulder for a moment, and she let him. Dmitri stood by the door with his back to the wall, where he could see the window and the yard. He had stood that way in every room for three months. Elena came in last, in her boots, with the cold, and looked at the table once from left to right and said, "Right. Show me."

The old man went on stirring.

Kira talked. She talked in the flat, precise voice she used for things that mattered, and he listened to it with half his mind, the way he had listened to everything in Wing C, through slots and doors and walls.

Forty statements, forty witnesses, one face. Dr. Tanaka on Monday, from Chronos: not confabulation, not hysteria. A broadcast. Morrison's gains on the Ministry district node. Erase, then seed. He knew those words. Everyone at this table knew them. A memory had been written into forty people, and it had Marcus's face on it, and Wing C gray.

"And the rest of it?" said Elena.

"The rest is real," said Kira. "Tanaka thinks. The seed takes the face and the clothes. It leaves the small things. They really saw someone go in."

She did not say what small things. He noticed that. He noticed that she did not look at anyone when she did not say it, and that her right hand, flat on the table, pressed down on the wood for a moment as if something under it might move. He filed it, as he filed everything, somewhere a machine would never think to look.

Then Kira pulled the photograph toward her and said, "And this."

He left the pot. He did not decide to. His feet took him across the kitchen with the spoon still in his hand, and he stood behind her chair and looked down over her shoulder, the way he had once looked over the shoulders of students at their desks, and read it.

A single typed page. Photographed at an angle, under a lamp, by someone in a hurry. The heading had been torn across, and only the first word remained, in capitals: PRINCIPLE —. After it, a ragged edge. A page number at the bottom: 30. In the lower right-hand corner, very small, a numeral. And at the foot of the text, a short typed mark that the photograph had blurred into nothing he could read.

In the middle of the page, one sentence, set by itself, the way a text sets the thing it most wants you to keep.

When the record fails, the people will believe whoever restores it. Arrange to be the one who restores it.

He read it once.

He stopped humming.

He did not decide to do that either. It simply stopped, in the middle of a phrase, at the place where the tune began to lift. And the kitchen stopped with it.

Alexei's head came up. Dmitri, by the door, went still in the particular way the brothers had, all at once, like a hand closing. Kira's shoulders did not move at all, which was how he knew she had heard it too. In Wing C, silence had been the signal. A rising phrase meant yes. A falling phrase meant no. And silence, from the old man with the trolley, meant they're watching. Three of the four people in the kitchen had learned it through a meal slot, and their bodies had not forgotten.

"Misha?" said Alexei, very quietly.

He did not answer. He was not in the kitchen any more.

It was 2063, and he was twenty-five, and the reading room of the Historical Archive smelt of dust and floor polish and the apples Anselm Kerr ate at eleven every morning, peeled in one long curl with a pocketknife.

Kerr was the senior archivist, a tall, stooped, courteous man who wore cotton gloves to handle paper and took them off to eat, and who had taken the young Petrov on because he had come to the interview with a notebook full of folk songs from the lakeshore and had said, when asked why, that he did not want them to be lost. That's all an archive is, Kerr had said. A place where things are not lost. Most people think it's a place where things are kept. It isn't the same thing. Mikhail had not understood the difference then. He did now.

That spring there had been a small quarterly on the periodicals trolley. It came in a plain gray cover. Journal of Post-Democratic Governance. It was the kind of thing that arrived by subscription and nobody read, and it was his job to catalogue it, so he did. He gave it a shelf mark, Periodicals, Governance, 114, and wrote the card in his own hand, and because he was twenty-five and could not help it, he read it.

Three issues that year. Essays, all unsigned. Short, calm, lucid, beautifully made. They were about how a people might be governed after the Fracture Years, when nobody could agree on what had happened yesterday. Governed not by force, and not by argument, but through the record. Whoever held what was remembered, the essays said, held what was possible. They did not rant. They did not threaten. They reasoned, gently, the way a good doctor reasons with a frightened patient, and every paragraph was so reasonable that you nodded, and at the end of every essay he had sat very still in the reading room with his hands flat on the table and felt cold to the bone.

He had done with them what he had always done with things he did not want to lose. He did it without thinking, the way other men underline. He took the lines that frightened him most and set them to tunes. Old tunes, lakeshore tunes, the ones every child in the border zone knew. It was how his mind worked. A thing sung was a thing kept.

There had been a young woman who came to the reading room on Thursdays.

He did not let himself think of her name, even now, even here. She had been from the systems department at the university, a theorist, with ink on her fingers and a quick, dry, frightening intelligence. She read everything on the periodicals trolley, and she read the gray journal the moment it came in, closely, slowly, making notes, the way you read something you mean to build on. And she argued with him. About poetry, mostly. She said that rhyme was a kind of compression algorithm and he said it was a kind of prayer, and they had fought about it across the reading-room table every Thursday for two years, in whispers, while Kerr pretended not to hear.

He had liked her very much. He had never once wondered what she wanted with the gray journal.

Then, in the year the system was founded, it had been classified.

He remembered the morning. Two men from a ministry that had not existed the year before came into the reading room with a list, and went to the periodicals shelves, and took the issues away in a box. All of them. The card was gone from the catalogue by evening. The shelf mark, Governance, 114, was simply not there any more; the numbers ran 113, 115, as if they always had.

He was not the only one who noticed. Kerr noticed. Kerr, who believed that an archive was a place where things were not lost, went to ask where they had been taken, and why, and on whose authority. He asked politely. He asked in writing. He asked twice.

In the spring they took Kerr away for a correction.

When he came back, he came back to his desk, and he took his gloves off at eleven and ate his apple. He peeled it in one long curl with his pocketknife, exactly as he always had. He was very pleasant. He was very calm. He did not remember the journal, or the box, or the questions he had asked, and when Mikhail said the shelf mark to him, very quietly, one morning by the trolley, Kerr had smiled at him with perfect courtesy and no recognition at all, and said, I'm afraid I don't know that one. Are you sure you have the number right?

He had never come back as himself.

That was when Mikhail understood what the songs were. Not a habit. Not a young man's way of underlining. They were the only copies. Every issue in every archive had gone into a box, and the box had gone somewhere, and the only place left in the world where those sentences still existed was inside a twenty-nine-year-old archivist's head, set to the tunes his grandmother had sung on the pier.

He had kept them there on purpose ever since.

"Misha."

It was Kira. She had turned in her chair and was looking up at him. He did not know how long he had been standing there with the spoon in his hand.

He put his finger on the photograph. On the sentence.

"The same hand wrote this," he said. His voice came out rough. He did not use it often. "Forty years apart."

Nobody spoke.

"Wrote what?" said Kira. "Mikhail. The same as what?"

He did not answer her with words. He had not, for a very long time, answered anyone with words when a tune would do. He drew a breath, and closed his eyes, and in the warm farmhouse kitchen with the stove ticking and the fog beginning to come up off the lake, he sang.

It was the Lakeshore Air. The same tune he had hummed through every meal slot in Wing C, three times a day, for more than a year. The tune the brothers' mother had hummed over the washing-up. The tune that had given Marcus Chen back a lullaby. His voice was cracked and very quiet, an old man's voice that had spent too long in corridors, and he did not try to make it anything else.

> A people that has lost part of its past > will accept any past it is given — > provided it is given gently, > and by someone it trusts.

The last phrase lifted at the end, like a question.

He opened his eyes.

Kira was staring at him.

He had seen her face do a great many things in Wing C. He had seen it angry and shaking and blank and counting. He had never seen it do this. It had come open, all at once, like a door blown by the wind, and behind it was a woman sitting at a slot in a glass cell eight months long, hearing two seconds of a tune three times a day, every day, and never knowing.

"It had words," she said. Barely aloud. "All that time. It had words."

"It always had words," he said. "Several sets. That's the thing about an old tune. People keep putting new ones in it." He looked down at the photograph again. "Those were mine."

Alexei had sat down, slowly, on the bench against the wall. Elena was standing very still with her arms folded, her dark eyes going from the old man to the photograph and back. Dmitri had not moved from the door, but his face had done the thing the brothers' faces did, the thing that looked like nothing and meant the opposite.

"Where is it from?" said Elena. Flat. Commander's voice.

"A journal," he said. "A quarterly. 2063. I catalogued three issues. That passage was from the second." He was quiet for a moment. "They took it away in a box. And everyone who asked where it had gone."

Kira was already moving.

She pulled the resistance terminal across the table toward her, the old one Elena's technician had built out of parts, which reached the factorepo through three borrowed nodes and a dead man's credentials, and she typed. Fast. She did not recite this time. He watched her lips move and nothing come out.

Journal of Post-Democratic Governance.

Nothing.

She tried the title in fragments. The year. The publisher's city. The subject heading. She went into the Historical Archive's own catalogue, the public face of it, and then, with a look at Elena that Elena answered with a nod, into something below the public face that she did not explain.

"Shelf mark," she said, without turning round.

"Periodicals, Governance, 114."

She typed it. He watched the screen over her shoulder.

Periodicals, Governance, 113. Periodicals, Governance, 115.

There was no 114. There had never been a 114. The numbers ran on, clean and continuous, the way the numbers on the other side of a hole run on, as if the edges had been folded together so well that the record could not feel the fold.

"Nothing," Kira said. "Not an entry. Not a citation. Not a catalogue number. It isn't deleted. It isn't flagged. It was never there." She sat back. "Officially, it never existed."

"Officially," said the old man, "neither do I."

Nobody said it for a while. It was Kira, in the end, who did, because it was Kira's way to say the thing that the numbers said, however much she did not want to.

"Then it's older than the nation."

She laid it out, level, in the voice she used for faults in a system. A journal in 2063, before the Founding, before the Act, before the factorepo existed. A voice in it, calm and reasonable, explaining how a people could be made to accept a past. The journal removed from the world so thoroughly that its absence left no mark. And forty years later, among Continuity's papers on a Deputy Minister's desk, one typed page in the same voice, explaining what to do when the record fails.

And then an hour in which the record failed.

"Whoever erased the hour," Kira said, "may not have been improvising. They may have been following something. Something written before any of this was built." She looked at the photograph. "Instructions."

"You can't know that," said Elena.

"No," said Kira. "I can't."

The old man said nothing. He had gone back to the stove, because the barley had begun to catch, and he stood with his back to them and stirred it, and did not hum.

He thought, stirring, about why he was still here.

He had been corrected more times than he could count, in more places than he could name, over forty years. They had looked for what he loved, every time, the way the machines always looked, for the heat. And every time they had found an old man who hummed. A harmless, broken, pleasant old man with a white ponytail and a trolley, who knew a great many tunes and nothing else. The hood came down and the probe went looking, and it passed over the songs the way a hand passes over furniture in a dark room. Rote. Rhythm. Something learned by heart and sung a thousand times, low heat, not worth the gain. It skimmed them, because it had been built to skim them. And under the tunes, all that time, the words had waited, perfectly still.

Anya was in there. Katya was in there, at three years old, singing the ferry verse wrong. Kerr was in there, peeling his apple. And a gray journal in a box, and a young woman's inky fingers, and the Thursday arguments about rhyme.

The machine had never once thought to listen.

He turned round from the stove. They were all looking at him, the four of them, the way people look at something they have walked past a thousand times and have just seen.

"They think I'm furniture," he said. "Furniture remembers everything that happened in the room."

Kira picked up the photograph. She held it to the lamp, tilting it, the way she had once held a water-systems manual up to the light in 7-K looking for a word. He crossed back to the table to take it from her, meaning only to look at the sentence again.

He did not look at the sentence.

He looked at the corner. The lower right. The small typed numeral he had seen before and not read, because the words had come first, as words always did.

XI.

He had been an archivist for long enough to know what it looked like. The small mark a careful office puts on a document that exists in more than one copy, so that each copy can be told from the others, and each can be traced. It might have been something else. A volume. A section. But it looked to him like a copy numeral.

The eleventh copy of something.

He stood very still again. Not humming. The kitchen went quiet round him a second time.

"Where did she get this?" he said.

"Continuity's papers," said Kira. "Their Ministry correspondence. Singh found it weeks ago."

The old man looked at the numeral for a long moment. At the torn heading. At the page number, thirty, which meant there had been twenty-nine pages before it, and at least one after.

"Then Continuity," he said, "was quoting scripture."
