Chapter 24: I Remember Doing It

Monday, December 20, 2106. The Ministry medical wing; a Ministry flat, Veridica Capital

December twentieth, 2106. Oh-nine-forty hours. Ministry district. Vehicle: Ministry pool car, dark gray. Involved parties: Chen, M. (material witness); Wei, T. (lead investigator); driver, unnamed. Destination: Ministry Medical Wing. Purpose:

Marcus stopped the entry there, because he did not know how to finish it.

The car went east along the boulevard and turned in under the Ministry's shadow, and he watched the buildings come up on either side, pale and ordered, their windows catching the flat December light. He had not been inside the Ministry district since the fifteenth of November. He had walked out of it at fourteen fifty-nine with Kira beside him and every step checked, and at fifteen-oh-one the screens on the boulevard had told him that none of it had happened.

He found that he was counting the windows. It was the kind of thing Kira did. He made himself stop.

"You don't have to come in," said Wei, beside him. "I want to say that clearly. You can wait in the car. I'll tell you what he says."

"You asked me to come."

"I did." Wei was looking out of his own window. The dressing on his left hand was smaller now, a pad and a strip of tape across the back of it, and the hand lay on his knee. "I thought you should hear it. From him. So that you can hear it and be free of it." He turned his head. "You've spent five weeks not knowing whether you did this, Marcus. I read it on your wall. I don't want you to have to take it from a report."

Kira had not been allowed to come. Ms. Osman is not a material witness. She had stood at the door of the flat with her arms folded while the guard held it, and looked at Wei, and then at Marcus, and said nothing at all, which was worse than anything she could have said.

"Thank you," said Marcus.

He meant it. That was the difficulty. He meant it entirely.

The medical wing was on the third floor of a building behind the Ministry's east face, and it was quiet in the way that Ashford's ordinary wards had been quiet: soft floors, pale walls, a smell of something clean. He walked between Wei and an enforcer along a corridor of closed doors, and at the end of it they came into a room that he recognised before he had seen it properly, because he had seen a hundred like it.

A table. Chairs. A camera dome in the corner with its steady green light. A clerk at a side desk with a recording tablet. A medical officer in a pale coat. Undersecretary Pell, already seated, with his hands folded on the table and a look on his face that Marcus could only describe as appetite. Director Kovač at the end of the row, coat on, rimless glasses, a folder closed in front of her.

They put Marcus in a chair against the side wall, two metres from the table, where he could see everything and be part of nothing.

Material witness. Location: side wall. Status: observing.

Then the inner door opened, and they brought Reeves in.

He had been shaved. That was the first thing. In the annex, Kira said, he had been thin and unshaven; now his face was smooth and pale and he wore the soft gray tunic of the medical wing, loose at the neck, the colour of a patient's clothes. He walked between two orderlies without being held. He sat down in the chair across the table from Pell and crossed his legs and folded his clean hands on his knee, and Marcus's whole body went still, because he knew that posture. He had seen it on a stool beside a console in treatment room C-2, in May, the morning he lost the library.

Reeves looked round the table with his light brown eyes. Pell. Kovač. Wei. The clerk. The medical officer. And then the side wall.

"Mr. Chen," he said pleasantly. "Good morning."

"Good morning," said Marcus. His voice came out level. He did not know where it had come from.

"I'm glad you're here," said Reeves. "I think you should be."

The clerk said the date and the time and the names of everyone present, and that the statement was voluntary and being recorded, and asked Dr. Reeves to confirm that he understood. Dr. Reeves confirmed that he understood.

"In your own words," said Wei. "Whenever you're ready."

"I went up at fourteen twenty," said Reeves.

He said it the way he had once said Good morning, Mr. Chen, from behind a mirror: unhurried, conversational, a colleague showing a visitor round.

"The side entrance. I had a staff card from my Ministry days. Nobody ever cancelled it. Nobody ever cancels anything in that building." A faint smile. "I went up the east stairs. Twelve floors. I remember being out of breath on the ninth, and stopping, and thinking that was absurd. Then the corridor. It was very quiet. Her door was open."

He paused, not for effect, but the way a man pauses to see a thing again.

"She was at her desk. She had her sleeves rolled up. She was reading something with her glasses on, the ones on a cord, and she looked up." His eyes were steady on the middle distance. "I remember her face. She wasn't frightened. That's the thing I remember most. She looked at me as if she had been expecting someone, and I wasn't quite who she'd expected."

Pell had leaned forward.

"There was a letter opener on the desk. Steel, with a flat handle. I remember picking it up." Reeves turned his right hand over on his knee and looked at the palm, briefly, as if it might still be there. "I remember that it was colder than I expected."

Nobody in the room moved.

"There were flowers on the table by the window. White ones, in a low glass. Chrysanthemums, I think. I remember the smell, afterwards, on the stairs. It stayed with me all the way down." He looked up. "That's odd, isn't it? Of everything. The smell of the flowers."

Cut flowers, Marcus thought, and was on the twelfth floor again, at fourteen fifty-five, outside a locked door, with Kira beside him and an empty desk where the aide should have been, and down the corridor an office door standing ajar, and the smell coming out of it, faint and green and sweet.

He had smelled them from a door down the corridor. Singh's door had been locked.

The flowers were in the case file. He had read the inventory himself, in the flat, in the copy Kovač had arranged for them: cut flowers (white chrysanthemums), low glass vase, side table by window. It had been one line among four hundred pages. Reeves remembered it as if it had been the whole afternoon.

Item: flowers. Location: Deputy Minister's office, per case file; corridor, per Chen, M. Status: He did not know. He put it in UNCHECKED, in the place in his head where the columns were, and kept watching.

Pell asked the questions after that. He asked them well, for a man who had wanted a different answer for five weeks. What time had Reeves left the building? Reeves thought about twenty to three, by the east stairs again. Where had he gone? Back to the annex. What had he done with the jacket? Hung it up. He had not thought about it.

"And the broadcast," said Pell. "The seeding. The forty witnesses. Chen's face."

Reeves was quiet for a moment.

"Yes," he said. "It was my carrier. It must have been. The comment is mine; I've seen it. I suppose I must have prepared it in the annex, before." He frowned, very slightly, the frown of a man consulting a note he has not quite read. "It would have been the natural choice. His face. He was the best result I ever had."

Marcus heard the difference.

He did not think anyone else in the room heard it. Pell was writing. Kovač was looking at her folder. Wei was watching Reeves with a grave, attentive stillness, his hands folded on the table. But Marcus heard it as clearly as a change of key. When Reeves talked about the stairs and the desk and the cold steel, he talked the way a man remembers. When he talked about the carrier, he talked the way a man concludes. It must have been. I suppose I must have. It would have been the natural choice.

The murder was a memory. The rest was a deduction from it.

Seeding doesn't bother with small things, Tanaka had said. And the method did not bother with the small things. It went for the face, and the colour, and the hand on the steel. It went for what a mind would hold hardest.

They brought the statement to him to sign. The clerk turned the tablet and held out the stylus, and Reeves took it and looked at it for a moment, turning it in his clean fingers.

I've never signed anything in my life, he had said to Kira, a week ago, being led out of the annex. She had written it in paragraph nineteen of her report.

He signed. Carefully, in a small neat hand, like a man doing a thing for the first time and wanting it to be right.

Pell sat back in his chair. He did not smile, quite. He put both hands flat on the table and looked at them, and there was something in his face that Marcus had seen in enforcers at the end of a long raid, a kind of physical satisfaction, as if a door he had been leaning on for a month had finally given. "Thank you, Doctor," he said. "That will be all."

Kovač had taken off her glasses. She was rubbing the bridge of her nose with two fingers. When she put them back on she looked, for the first time in all the weeks Marcus had known her, simply tired, and not afraid.

Wei stood. He said, gravely, to the clerk, "Thank you. Please see that the Director has a copy today." And then, to Reeves, quietly, with no trace of triumph at all: "Thank you, Doctor. I know that wasn't easy."

"No," said Reeves, considering it. "No, I don't suppose it was."

Marcus watched his eyes the whole time.

He knew those eyes. He had listened to that voice for ten months from a white chair, and in the last three of them he had looked up into the face that went with it. He had learned, in the vault, to read the difference between the voice's boredom and its interest, the way a prisoner learns the footsteps of every guard. He knew what Reeves looked like when he was performing: the pleasant, tilted patience of a man telling a patient what was good for him, a voice arranged a little in advance of its words.

This was not that.

Reeves was not lying. He was not performing. He sat in his gray tunic with his hands folded and his eyes clear, and he believed every word he had said. He believed in the stairs and the flowers and the cold of the steel. He believed in her face looking up.

Marcus knew exactly what that looked like from the inside. He had a field in his head. Wet grass, and stars over it, and Kira beside him counting them. He would have sworn to it in front of The Judge. He could still feel the damp through his shirt when he thought of it. There had never been a field.

The orderlies came. Reeves stood, without being asked, and turned toward the inner door, and his route took him along the side wall, past Marcus's chair.

He stopped.

He did not look at the enforcer or the orderlies or the table. He looked down at Marcus with his light brown eyes, and they were as interested as they had ever been, the way they had been interested in him on every morning of Wing C. He spoke so quietly that only Marcus could hear it, and pleasantly, as if they were colleagues comparing notes after a seminar.

"Isn't it interesting, Marcus? I remember it the way you remember your field."

Then the orderly touched his elbow, and he went on, through the inner door, and it closed behind him.

Marcus sat very still.

He had never told Reeves about the field. He had never needed to. Reeves had built the holes the storm had filled, and he would have known, better than anyone alive, what grew in holes like those.

The cold began in his hands and went inward.

He understood it all at once, the way he had once understood, standing in Wing C, what the word acquired meant. Reeves had been seeded. Someone had put a hood over his head in a quiet room at night, with the gain set deep, and written into him the stairs and the desk and the face looking up and the steel. And Reeves knew. Not with the part of him that remembered, which believed it absolutely, but with the other part, the part that stood a little to one side of every experiment and took notes. He knew it the way Marcus knew about the field: as a fact you carry beside a feeling that will not be moved by it.

The man who had put Kira in front of him to see what would grow had had a murder planted in him to see whether it would take.

It had taken.

In the corridor Wei fell into step beside him. The enforcer walked two paces behind.

"How do you feel?" Wei asked.

Marcus did not answer at once. Wei did not press. They walked the length of the soft pale corridor, past the closed doors.

"I don't know," said Marcus at last. It was true.

"That's all right. It'll come." Wei glanced at him, and there was nothing in the look but kindness, and something tired underneath it, the look of a man at the end of a long case who has done what he said he would. "You can move it now, I imagine," he said. "To the other column."

Marcus looked at him.

"The upload," said Wei gently. "The stairs. Everything you wrote on that wall. You can put it back where it belongs."

"Yes," said Marcus. "I suppose I can."

In the car, going back, he made himself think it through in order, because that was what he had instead of courage.

Item: Reeves's confession. Status: sincere. Content: false.

Item: the confession clears Chen, M.

He looked out at the boulevard. The Ministry district slid past, pale and ordered, full of people who remembered him walking into a dead woman's office in prison gray. And now it would have another memory to set against theirs: a confession, signed, sincere, recorded, from the man everyone in Veridica already knew was a monster. Pell would take it to the tribunal. The tribunal would take it to The Judge. The Judge would do its arithmetic. And Marcus Chen would be cleared of murder by a false memory in another man's head.

His freedom depended on a lie that nobody but him could see.

And he could not say so. If he stood up in front of The Judge and said Reeves has been seeded, then there was no confession. Then there was no culprit. Then there were only forty witnesses again, and one dead man in Wing C gray, and a white room waiting at Unit 3 with a cradle set to depth. He could not use the truth without destroying himself.

He sat with his hands in his lap. They were unmarked. He looked at them for a long time.

Kira was waiting when he came in.

The guard closed the door behind him. The flat was as it always was: clean, anonymous, a sofa, a table, a window with a view of a wall. No cameras inside. I asked. She was standing at the table, and she did not come to him, and she did not ask him anything. She waited.

He told her. All of it, in order, as a fact entry, because it was the only way he could get it out. The stairs. The flowers. The cold of the steel. The difference between remembering and concluding. Reeves signing, as if for the first time. Isn't it interesting, Marcus. When he came to the field his voice went, and he stopped, and started again, and finished.

Kira listened without moving. When he had finished she did not say anything for a long time.

Then she went to her coat, hanging on the back of the door, and took two things from the lining, and came back, and laid them on the table between them.

The first was a photograph. Nikolai had lifted it from the team room's own feed at the Regional Factorepo, in November: screens, desks, the backs of heads. At the end of the room a tall man with gray at his temples, holding a cup of tea in his right hand. His left hand at his side, wrapped in a clean white dressing.

The second was the forty statements. Kira had had them since the first week, in a folder gone soft at the corners from handling. She opened it without looking, the way you open a book at a page you have opened many times, and turned it to face him.

He was in gray, like a patient. His left hand was bandaged.

White bandage. Left hand.

He was holding his hand like it hurt.

Wrapped hand. The left one. I noticed because he held the door with his other.

Forty times. The wording changed. The hand did not.

Marcus looked at the photograph. He looked at the statements. He looked at the photograph again.

Another day in paradise. A voice he could not place, saying it lightly, like an old joke. A smooth blank place where a face should have been.

"Say it," said Kira, very quietly.

He could not.

He knew what it would make him. A man accusing the only official in Veridica who had stood in the rain at a checkpoint with a signed order and said I'm not going to let them empty you. A man accusing him with nothing: a hand, which lots of people cut, and an office joke half the city used, said in a voice he could not place, and the word of a seeded prisoner who called it interesting. The man who had given him a car to sit in and a flat without cameras and Kira. The man who had said, five minutes ago, in a pale corridor, You can put it back where it belongs.

He took out his lists. The paper was worn soft at the folds. CHECKED. UNCHECKED. HOPES.

At the bottom of UNCHECKED, in his small, precise historian's hand, in pencil, he wrote:

T.W.?

He looked at it for a long time. Kira watched him and said nothing.

Then he moved his pencil across to the last column, and below everything else he had ever filed there, below the field, he wrote:

Please be wrong.
