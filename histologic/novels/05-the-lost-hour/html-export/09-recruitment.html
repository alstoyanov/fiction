Chapter 9: Recruitment

Wednesday, November 17, 2106. Regional Factorepo, Veridica Capital

Pieter Lang took the 07:40 tram from the western districts, as he had every weekday for nine weeks, and got off one stop early, because a man with a bad back and nothing to hurry for might want the walk.

He did not have a bad back. He had a way of holding his shoulders that suggested one. He had a beard that itched in the heat of the tram, a pair of glasses with plain lenses that he kept pushing up his nose, and a canvas bag with a lunch carton in it that would be noodles, because it was always noodles. He had the citizen number of a man who had died in a storm in August, and an interface signature that Elena's technician had built over six weeks, so that the gates of the Regional Factorepo would read it and say yes.

The gates read it and said yes.

Nobody looked at him in the lobby. Nobody ever did. He had studied for nine weeks exactly how little a man had to do to be invisible in this building, and the answer was: less than you would think. Eat at your desk. Answer questions accurately and at slightly too much length. Never be the one who notices anything.

He loved it. That was the part he had not expected. He loved Pieter Lang, who had no brothers and no past and no father in a Registry file, and whose worst fear was a missed maintenance window. And every morning, walking through the gates, he was terrified by how well it worked. A disguise that good was not a disguise. It was a second person. He did not know yet what it was doing to the first.

The big display in the lobby, which for as long as anyone could remember had shown the national repository's integrity figure in calm green, had been switched off on Monday night. There was a rectangle of slightly cleaner wall around its frame. In the lift, two women were talking about the hour in the voices people use at funerals.

At his desk on the contractors' floor there was a message waiting, flagged urgent.

P. Lang. Please attend the investigation room, monitoring floor, at your earliest convenience. M. Reeves, for T. Wei.

He read it twice. Then he put the lid back on the noodles, very carefully, the way you put the lid on something that might spill, and noticed that his hands had gone cold.

She came to get him before he could go.

She was younger than him by several years, with a tablet under her arm and the hollow-eyed brightness of someone who had not been home properly since Monday. She looked at the name on his terminal and then at him.

"Pieter Lang? You flagged the core block. Fourteen hundred to fifteen hundred on the fifteenth. No owner." She said it the way other people said good morning. "You filed the query at eleven on Sunday night. Who reads the maintenance schedule at eleven on a Sunday night?"

"I do," said Pieter Lang. "It's very dull. It helps me sleep."

One side of her mouth moved, and stopped, as if a smile had been scheduled and then postponed.

"Well, it didn't work," she said. "Come on. He wants you."

The badge on her lanyard said M. REEVES, MONITORING. Nikolai read it as they walked, and the cold in his hands went up to the wrists.

It was a common enough name. There were hundreds of them in the capital. He put it away, the way he had once put things into the dead space of the Registry, in a block nobody would ever read, and walked beside her to the lift with his eyes on his shoes.

The investigation room had been a conference room until Monday. Someone had taped paper over the lower half of its glass so that the monitoring floor could not see the screens, and the screens were everywhere, on the walls, on trolleys, propped among cups and cables on the long table. Most of them showed the same thing in different forms. And in every one of them, a gap.

Traffic records, births, deaths, transactions, broadcast logs, camera archives. The lines ran along the timeline, dense and continuous, until 13:59:59. Then they stopped, every one, at the same place, as if cut with the same blade. There was a clean white space an hour wide. At 15:00:00 they began again, as dense as before.

It was not corruption. Corruption had noise and contradiction, and the checksums flagged it in red. The checksums on either side of this gap agreed with each other perfectly, as if the hour had been lifted out and the edges folded together so well that the system could not feel the fold.

Nikolai had spent ten years hiding things in systems. He knew what it took to make a hole that clean. He stood inside the door looking at it and felt something that was not admiration and was very close to it.

"Mr. Lang."

The man at the end of the table had stood up. He was tall and graying at the temples, in a dark suit that had been slept in, or near. He came round the table with his right hand out, and his voice was quiet and entirely unhurried, as if there were no hole in the wall behind him at all.

"Thomas Wei. Thank you for coming. You flagged the block on Sunday night. The schedule went to four hundred people, and nobody else did."

"It was only a query," said Pieter Lang. "A window with no owner. It's supposed to have an owner."

"Yes. It's supposed to." Wei looked at him with an interest that was mild and friendly and very complete. "You saw it before anyone. I want people who notice. Will you join us? I've cleared it with your contract manager. It's not glamorous. Mostly it's reading logs that were never meant to be read."

Say no, Nikolai thought. A dull man does not want to be noticed by the lead investigator of the most important crime in the history of Veridica.

But a dull man, asked by a senior architect in front of a room of colleagues, does not say no either.

"If you think I'd be useful," he said.

"I think you already have been, Pieter." Wei smiled. "We're all first names in here. It saves time, and we haven't got any."

He had got the name right the first time, without a glance at a tablet or a lanyard. Nikolai noticed it, and filed it, and did not know what it meant.

Wei went back to his place and picked up a stylus to mark a printout. He wrote with his right hand. His left lay flat on the table, holding the page steady, with a dressing across the back of it, a neat square of gauze and tape, the kind you put on a cut that is healing.

Nikolai's eyes went to it and away again, the kind of glance a man makes at anything white in a room.

Wei saw it. He lifted the hand an inch from the table, rueful, and put it down.

"Server rack," he said. "Tuesday. Carelessness." And then, to the room, "Right. Pieter's with us. Maya, would you show him where we are?"

Where they were was the edges.

Wei explained it at ten, standing by the widest screen with one hand in his pocket, the way a good teacher explains something he still finds interesting.

"The hour is gone from the primary, from every regional and local repository, and from all three backups. Whoever did this knew the repository better than any of us, and left nothing in it. So we stop looking at the record, and we look at everything that talks to the record." He touched the place where the lines resumed. "Caches. Buffers. Devices that held data locally and tried to send it later. Anything," he said, "that didn't hear the hour was over."

Somebody laughed a little. It was a good line.

"At fifteen hundred the repository started accepting writes again, and every device in the country that had been holding data for it tried to hand it over. Most of it was rejected, because it was timestamped inside an interval the repository no longer recognised. But rejected isn't the same as destroyed. Some devices will have logged the rejection. A few may still be holding what they tried to send. I want every one of them. Traffic sensors. Medical monitors. Door controllers. Transit gates." His eyes passed over the room, and over Nikolai, and did not stop. "The hour was taken out of the record. It wasn't taken out of the world. The world keeps its own receipts."

It was brilliant. It was exactly right. And it was, almost step for step, what Nikolai had done himself on Monday at 15:01, alone at his desk, with his heart going like a drum.

He sat at the end of the table and looked at his hands.

He had felt it at 14:38.

He had been at his desk doing nothing, the way Pieter Lang did nothing, and something had touched him that was not in the room. The bond had never been thoughts. It was weight, and he knew his brothers' weights the way he knew the position of his own hands in the dark: Dima a cold steady mass at the edge of his mind, Lyosha a pressure behind the eyes. They were both across the city that afternoon, in the Ministry district, doing whatever Elena had planned for the hour nobody owned.

At 14:38 both weights had gone, for a moment, flat, as if someone had laid a sheet of glass over them. A lurch, like a lift that drops a centimetre before it stops, and gone. He had sat for twenty-three minutes with his hands pressed between his knees, waiting.

At 15:01 every alarm in the building went off.

While the monitoring floor was still getting up from its chairs, he had opened a maintenance session on the edge-device relays, which a contractor on his tier was entitled to do, and begun to copy. Not the repository. The queues. Traffic sensors on the boulevard with an hour of counts to hand over. A patient monitor in a hospital on the east side with an hour of somebody's heart rhythm that it very much wanted someone to take. Transit gates. A weather station on a roof. And a door controller at the Ministry of Historical Integrity, on a side entrance, which had logged its events locally for an hour and was now trying, politely and repeatedly, to send them.

He had four minutes. At 15:05 the repository's reconciliation service woke up, recognised a flood of writes timestamped inside an interval that did not exist, and told every device that sent one to discard it. He watched the queues empty across the city like lights going out.

By then he had his copy. It was on a drive in the lining of his canvas bag, and it had not left him since. Among the heartbeats and the traffic counts there was one line he had read perhaps two hundred times.

SIDE ENTRANCE B. 14:21:07. CARD PRESENTED. ACCESS GRANTED. HOLDER: [field unreadable]. CLASS: SYSTEMS ARCHITECTURE, SENIOR.

The name field was garbage. The buffer had been written while it was being read, or something like it, and the one field that mattered had come out as noise. The class had survived.

He had not given it to Wei. He was not going to. He had learned at the border facility and at Ashford, and in ten years of forging papers for his brother, that the man who holds the only copy of something decides who else gets to hold it. He was not ready to decide.

"No," said Maya, at half past two. "That's wrong."

"It isn't."

"Look at it." She leaned across and tapped his screen, which he did not like people doing and which she did twice more in the next ten minutes. "The hospital monitor says it lost its connection at 14:00:37. Everything else we've got goes at 14:00:00 exactly. So either the hour didn't start on time at that hospital, or—"

"Or its clock is wrong."

"Hospital clocks aren't that wrong."

"This one is. It was last synchronised on Saturday morning and it gains about seventeen seconds a day. I checked its history." He pushed his glasses up. "By Monday afternoon it was thirty-six or thirty-seven seconds fast. So the real time was 14:00:00. It went at exactly the same moment as everything else."

Maya looked at the screen for a long moment. Then she looked at him, differently.

"So it's nothing."

"It's nothing. But now we know it's nothing."

"You checked its history. On your first day."

"It seemed rude not to."

She almost laughed. It came out as a breath through her nose. Something in her face eased, the way a person's face eases on discovering that the new colleague is going to be an argument rather than a weight. For an hour they worked side by side without speaking. Every so often she said a timestamp aloud, to nobody, the way some people hum, and he found he had begun to listen for them.

At four she said, without looking round, "You type like a Registry man."

His fingers did not stop. He had trained them not to.

"The shortcuts. You never touch the pointer. Correction contractors type like that. People who've done Registry maintenance. My—" She stopped, and started again, flatly. "I've seen it before."

"Border-zone work," he said. "Everything out there runs on old Registry terminals. You learn the shortcuts or you go mad."

"Hm," said Maya.

She did not ask anything else. But a quarter of an hour later he saw her glance at his hands again, the way she glanced at a timestamp she had decided to come back to.

The forty statements arrived at five, in a sealed bundle from the Enforcement Division: forty Ministry employees who had been in the building on Monday afternoon and had seen a man walk into the Deputy Minister's office at twenty past two. Nikolai opened the bundle on the room's shared terminal with everyone else and read the first three with everyone else. Then, while the others were reading, he copied it to his own session, broke it into pieces, and sent them out under a weather-station update that nobody in Veridica would ever open.

He sent the door-controller line with it.

And because Kira would want to see the room she was fighting, he sent three stills. The investigation room recorded itself, as every Ministry room did, and that afternoon Wei's people had asked him to fix its feed, because the audio kept dropping. He took three frames from the buffer before he fixed it. The wall of screens with the gap in it. The long table. The door. In the third, at the edge of the frame, Wei was half turned toward someone out of shot, a cup in his hand, saying something that had made them laugh.

He wrote it in the border smuggler's shift, the one his father had taught all three of them in the study under the green lamp.

TO K. FORTY STATEMENTS ATTACHED. DOOR LOG ATTACHED. THE CARD IS REAL. THE NAME IS GONE. I'M INSIDE. PHOTOGRAPHS OF WHERE. — N.

Then he ate the rest of his cold noodles and argued with Maya about a transit gate until half past six.

He met Wei in the corridor by the lifts, nearly at seven. Wei had his coat over his arm and the look of a man who had been in the same room for fifty hours and had enjoyed a surprising number of them.

"Pieter. Maya says you corrected a hospital monitor's clock by hand. She was quite cross about it. That's high praise, from Maya."

"It was only drift," said Pieter Lang.

"Only drift. Most people wouldn't have looked." Wei shifted the coat to his other arm, keeping the weight off his left hand. "I had a look at your file this afternoon. I like to know who I'm working with." He said it pleasantly, the way a man mentions the weather. "It's very thin, your file, Pieter."

Nikolai's heart stopped. His face, which had been trained by the border facility and by Ashford and by nine weeks of beard and glasses, did not move at all.

"I haven't done very much," said Pieter Lang.

"No. I like that in a man." Wei's smile widened, easy and warm. "Thin files. They're the only ones that still have room for surprises." He pressed the button, and the lift opened, and he stepped in. "Goodnight, Pieter. Get some sleep. I want you sharp."

The doors closed on him, smiling.

Nikolai stood in the corridor with his bag against his hip and his hands like ice, and could not for the life of him tell whether it had been a joke.

His room was on the fourth floor of a building in the western districts, above a shop that sold electrical parts and closed at six. He locked the door and put the chain on and stood in the dark for a full minute before he turned on the lamp.

Then he sat on the bed and took out his wallet.

He did it every night. He had told himself in September that he would do it once a week, the way a man rations something, and had lasted three days. The page was in the inner pocket, folded very small: a single page torn from Arithmetic for Young Citizens, Book Two, soft at the creases.

He unfolded it and held it to the lamp. The light came through the pinpricks, hundreds of them, small and even, a whole night's work with a library pin. Fog off the lake. The tar still warm. Three of us on the parapet. He could not feel it. He never could. What he held was not a memory but a story, told by someone who had been there, about someone who had been there and did not know it any more.

You were always on the right.

It was there. Nobody had touched it.

He folded it and put it back and zipped the pocket. Your file's very thin. A man with a dead man's number, and a page of pinpricks in his wallet that any Ministry technician with a lamp and an hour could have read.

The terminal chimed.

It was Kira. She had decoded everything in under two hours and read all forty statements, which he had expected, and she had sent back one line, which he had not.

THE CARD CLASS. WHO IN SYSTEMS ARCHITECTURE HAS A SENIOR CARD FOR THE MINISTRY?

He opened a session on the staff directory, which a member of Wei's team was now entitled to read, and built the query the way she would have built it. Systems Architecture. Senior grade. Access rights to the Ministry of Historical Integrity, side entrance B. Current as of November 15.

It took eleven seconds.

41 RESULTS.

Architects, most of them, from the Ministry district offices, a few from the Spire, two from a transmission group he had never heard of. Forty-one people who could have walked through a side door at 14:21 on Monday with a card in their hand. Forty-one was a lot. It meant almost nothing.

The list was alphabetical by surname. He had known, before he ran it, where the W's would fall.

WEI, THOMAS. SENIOR SYSTEMS ARCHITECT. MINISTRY DISTRICT.

It means nothing, he told himself. He has Ministry access, and so do forty others. He was appointed at 15:20. He is the one who asked for every door controller in the country. A man who walked through that door would not ask for its log.

He closed the terminal and turned off the lamp. He lay on the bed in his clothes, with his wallet in his hand and the chain on the door, and listened to the trams on the boulevard, one every eleven minutes, until the last one at half past one.

He did not sleep.
