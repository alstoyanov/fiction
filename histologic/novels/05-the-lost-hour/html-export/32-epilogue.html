Epilogue: One Year Later

Tuesday, November 15, 2107. The central plaza, Veridica Capital, and elsewhere

The stone was plain.

Marcus had argued for that, in committee, for most of an afternoon in March, against a design with bronze figures and another with a clock whose hands were stopped at two. In the end they had given him the plain stone because he was the dead historian and it was easier than arguing with him. It stood at the edge of the central plaza, pale gray, waist-high, a few metres from the fountain. The inscription was cut into the top in plain capitals:

TO THE HOUR THAT WAS LOST, AND THE TRUTH THAT WAS FOUND.

He had not known, when he chose the site, that it was the fountain. He had known it afterwards, standing there on a wet morning in April with the surveyor, when something in the light on the water had gone through him like a word almost remembered. He had checked. Central plaza, fountain, north side. August 2105. Chen, M.; Reyes, E. Status: first meeting. The record agreed with him. He had not needed it to.

At two o'clock the readings began.

That was the Day of Testimony. It was not his idea, or anyone's in particular; it had been proposed in the street in December before anyone on the Commission had thought of it, and by the summer it had become a thing people simply did. For one hour, from fourteen hundred to fifteen hundred, anyone who wished could stand at the stone and say aloud what they remembered of one ordinary day. Not a grand day. Not the day of the Lost Hour, necessarily. Any day. What they had eaten. Who they had seen. What the weather was. A clerk from the Commission wrote each one down by hand, and the speaker signed it, and a second person who had been there, if there was one, signed beside them.

A tram driver read out her route for the fifteenth of November last year, stop by stop. A boy of about ten read out what he had had for lunch at school, and the crowd laughed, and he looked offended, and then pleased. An old man read the names of the people who had been in his shop that afternoon, and at the end of it said, "I don't know why I remember that. I just do," and sat down.

Marcus read his last. He did not read the upload. He read the night before it: the cottage, the stove ticking, the fog coming in off the lake, Kira's hand in his at the window. Involved parties: two. He did not say how the entry ended. He had never finished it for her. She was standing in the front of the crowd with a book held against her coat, and she knew.

On the dais behind the stone, with the rest of the Commission, Thomas Wei applauded with the others. When Marcus stepped down, their eyes met for a moment across the heads of the crowd. Wei nodded pleasantly, the nod of one colleague to another. Marcus nodded back.

He looked, once, along the edge of the crowd for a tall woman with dark hair pulled back hard. He did not see her.

Which meant, he thought, that she was there.

"Seven," said Alexei, and then, because nobody had laughed, "I counted. Dima's not the only one who can count."

"You counted wrong," said Dmitri. "There are eight."

"Seven of us." He threw an arm round Isaiah's shoulders, which was like throwing an arm round a lamp post; Isaiah bore it with the patient courtesy of a man being embraced at a press conference. "Seven of Wing C. In one place. Standing up. Nobody in a white room. When was the last time that happened?"

"It has never happened," said Dmitri. "Isaiah was on the upper level."

"Then it's the first time. Even better."

It was the first time. Alexei had worked it out on the train that morning and been quietly astonished by it. Marcus, Kira, the three of them, Tanaka, Isaiah. There had been a hearing and a Registry office and a hundred links on a hundred small screens, but never all seven of them on one piece of ground at the same time, with nothing between them but cold air.

Isaiah had flown in the day before, from the Sovereignty, on a Synthesis passport, because the SFW did not issue travel papers to guests whose asylum was still, in the word the mission used, conditional. He had not complained about the word. He had said, "It's very precise. I admire it," and folded his hands.

Tanaka had come across the lake that morning. She had brought news, which she delivered to Alexei in the tone of someone reporting a patient's stable temperature: the Chronos proposal on "salience therapy" for storm trauma had been withdrawn. "After the eleventh memorandum," she said. "I had begun the twelfth. I shall keep it." She looked, for her, almost smug.

And Nikolai was standing on Dmitri's other side with his own face on, no beard, no glasses, his hair cut short again, so that for anyone looking at the three of them there was, for the first time in a year, nothing to count wrong. Maya was holding his hand. Not pretending not to. Just holding it, in the cold, the way you hold something that is yours.

Alexei looked at the three of them, himself and his brothers, reflected in the dark glass of a shop front across the square, and found that he had nothing to say, which was unusual, and let it be.

At a quarter to three, Old Songs went up to the stone.

Kira had not known he was going to. Nobody had. He was sixty-nine now, still officially dead, still refusing, courteously, every clerk who offered to correct it. He wore a coat the resistance had given him and a scarf Alexei had given him, and he walked up through the crowd slowly, with his white ponytail over his collar, and the crowd let him through because he was old and it was the Day of Testimony and anyone could speak.

He did not speak. He stood by the stone, and put one gnarled hand on it, and sang.

It was the Lakeshore Air. Kira knew it from the first phrase, the way the whole of Wing C had known it, the tune that lifted at the end of every line like a question. The crowd knew it too. She saw it go through them: a woman near the front putting her hand to her mouth, a man lifting his chin. Half the border zone had been sung to sleep to that tune. They thought it was a lullaby.

It was not the lullaby.

"A people that has lost part of its past," sang Old Songs, cracked and thin and absolutely true, "will accept any past it is given —"

Kira's lips moved with his. She did not sing. She had never sung in front of anyone and was not going to begin now, in front of eleven hundred people and a broadcast camera. But she knew the words. She had written them down. They were on the first page of the book held against her chest, in her own hand, and on the page after them, under the ferry verse, was a line of short strokes rising, so that whoever read it would know that it went up.

"provided it is given gently,

and by someone it trusts."

The last phrase lifted, and hung, and stopped.

There was a silence. Then the crowd applauded, warmly, the way crowds applaud an old man who has sung them something lovely. Someone near Kira said, "My grandmother used to sing that." Someone else said, "Those aren't the words." Nobody in the plaza knew where the words had come from. Nobody knew they had come from a journal that had never existed, or that the old man had carried them for forty-four years so that they would not be forgotten, and had now given them away so that they would not depend on him.

Kira held the book.

It had thirty-one contributors now. Thirty-one hands, in thirty-one inks, each of them writing down what they had seen and what they remembered, and each entry checked against at least two others, and every disagreement written down beside it, in the margin, in the other person's hand. It had gone round the border zone and back twice. It was the first. It was not the only one. There were dozens now, in kitchens and back rooms and the offices of people who had once trusted nothing but the record, kept by people who had never met each other, checking and arguing and writing. Nobody had organised it. Nobody could have. That was the point.

And in the Commission's public archive, which anyone in Veridica could open from any terminal, every testimony ever given in the matter of the Lost Hour sat side by side with its witness's name and every point of agreement and disagreement marked. The forty. The three brothers. Sokolov. Lia Santos. The confession. The alibi.

Anyone could read them. A surprising number of people had.

Maya had gone to the prison that morning, before the ceremony, because the fifteenth was the day.

It was always the fifteenth. She had chosen it in January, without quite admitting to herself why, and had not missed a month since. The visiting room was clean and warm and had a window onto a yard with one tree in it. Her father was thinner. He was always a little thinner. He asked about her work and listened to the answers with the terrible, precise interest he had always given to everything, and asked twice about Nikolai, and seemed pleased.

Then, as he always did, somewhere in the second half hour, he told her about the letter opener.

He did not tell it as a confession any more. He told it the way he might have described an unusual result in a trial: the weight of it in his hand, the side entrance, her face looking up. It was colder than I expected. He said it with a kind of detached wonder. He remembered it perfectly. He always would.

In the first months she had argued with him. She had brought him the timings, the node logs, Kira's reading of the seam, everything she was allowed to tell him and a little she was not. He had listened, and nodded, and said, "Yes, I can see why you'd think that," and told her about the letter opener again.

She had stopped arguing in the summer.

Today she only listened. When he had finished, she said, "All right, Dad," and put her hand over his on the table, and he looked down at it with mild surprise, as if it were data.

On the tram back she had written the visit down in the book Nikolai had given her. Date. Time. What he said. He still remembers it. And under that, as she did every month: He didn't do it. She did not need anyone to countersign that line. She wrote it anyway, so that it would be written.

Nikolai waited until the crowd had begun to thin.

Then he drew the seven of them, and Maya, a little way apart, into the lee of the colonnade on the east side of the plaza, where two cameras overlapped badly and a third had been pointing at the same patch of pavement since the spring. He had checked it three times that morning. It was, as far as he could tell, the only honest blind spot in the square.

He took out a small tablet and turned it so they could see.

"It's a registry schedule," he said, very quietly. "Not ours. I'm not going to say whose, not here. I've been watching their maintenance tables since August, because someone had to, and three weeks ago this appeared." He touched the screen. A block of time. A window on a primary core, some weeks ahead. An owner field.

The field was blank.

Nobody said anything.

"That's all it is, so far," said Nikolai. "A booking with no name on it. But I pulled the structure of the reservation, and I sent it to Kira without telling her where it came from."

Kira was looking at the screen. Her face had gone very still, the way it went when she was most angry, so that she seemed calm.

"It's the same," she said. "The way it's laid out. The way it leaves no contradictions behind it." She did not say the name. None of them did. "It's the same hand."

"It's happening again," said Nikolai. He looked at his hands. "Somewhere else. I don't know what it's for. I don't know when."

Marcus had not taken his eyes off the blank field.

Then he looked up, at the seven of them, and Maya, standing close together in the only blind spot in the square, and at Kira with the book against her chest.

"Then we remember again," he said. "All of us." A pause. "Written down."

Yuki Tanaka saw the woman at twelve minutes past four.

She saw her the way she had once noticed things in Ashford's ordinary wards: at the edge of attention, while looking at something else. She had been watching Marcus, who was standing at the stone with Kira, talking to a clerk from the Commission, his glasses pushed up on his forehead. And at the far side of the plaza, beyond the fountain, by one of the pillars of the colonnade, a woman was watching him too.

Female. Approximately fifty. Gray coat. Reading glasses on a cord round the neck. She was standing very still, with her hands in her pockets, not looking at the stone or the Commission or the crowd. Only at Marcus.

Tanaka looked at her properly.

A tram went across the end of the plaza, between them, long and slow and gray. When it had passed, the woman was gone. There was only the pillar, and the colonnade, and a man with a dog.

Tanaka stood for a moment in the cold.

She thought of a case file on a secure link. A stab wound, a letter opener, a time of death during the unrecorded interval. An identification made by staff who had all been inside the seeded district. A cremation within twenty-four hours, at the request of a family nobody could find. No photographs of the face. Withheld for dignity.

She thought of the place where she kept the things she could not explain, and of the sentence she had put there eleven months ago. Nobody who can't be seeded has seen her body.

For most of her life she had kept things in that place and told no one. It was a very good place. Nothing she had put there had ever been lost.

She took from her coat the small notebook she had begun to carry in the spring, and a pen, and wrote, in her small precise hand:

15 November 2107. 16:12. Central plaza, colonnade, west side. A woman of about fifty, gray coat, reading glasses on a cord. Watching M. Chen. Not seen again. Witnesses: none.

She looked at the last word. Then she went to find Kira, so that it would not be none.

In a private hand, in a journal that no one else has read:

> Nov 15, 2107. The Doctrine is law. The reformers are grateful, and trusted. Chen knows, and says nothing; he has begun keeping records of his own, with many hands, all checking each other. That was not in the design. Neither was the old man. I find I want to see what they build.

History can be erased, but the truth endures in those who lived it, and in those who wrote it down.
