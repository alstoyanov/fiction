Chapter 26: The Speech

Friday, December 6, 2109. The farmhouse, the border zone

Since the morning of the fifth of October, in the yard, with two cups of tea that were not his, Mikhail Petrov had hummed nothing at Marcus.

Not the rising phrase, which meant I see you. Not the falling one, which meant no. Nothing. It was the longest he had gone without a tune for a person since Wing C, and it was deliberate, which the yes and the no had not quite been; the yes and the no came out of him; the nothing he held in, on purpose, the way you hold your breath, and it had cost him, because a man who has kept himself alive for forty years by humming does not stop humming at someone he loves without it costing him.

On Monday he had stood at the back of a crowd on the Justice Center steps, officially dead, in the open air of a city he did not go into, and heard a man confess with nothing attached. He had gone because a thing like that should be witnessed by someone who would not use it, and he was the only one of them who could stand in a crowd without being on a camera's list, being dead. He had heard the whole of it. I checked the record. I did not ask the witness. He had hummed nothing there either, at the back, under his hood, though his throat had wanted to.

He had been deciding ever since.

Friday he did ordinary work.

He mended the damper, which had come loose, and set it back to two fingers' width, so the crack in the stove door glowed and did not smoke. He peeled potatoes at the sink. The ordinary work was how he thought; it had always been how he thought; forty years of correction rooms and meal trolleys and dishwater had taught him that the mind does its real work while the hands are busy with something dull, that a tune comes while you are splitting wood and a decision comes while you are peeling potatoes, and that a man who sits still to think only frightens himself. So he did the work, and let the decision come, and it came the way they came, slowly, under the hands.

Vance sat at the table with her cup held in both hands and her right wrist against the table edge, and watched him do the ordinary work, and knew, he thought, what he was deciding, and did not ask, because Elise had never once in sixty years asked him a thing he had not offered, which was one of the reasons he had argued with her on Thursdays for two years and come across a marsh for her at seventy-one. She knew the shape of a man working toward a thing. She had watched him work toward things across a reading-room table when they were young, and she watched him now, across a farmhouse kitchen, old, and the watching was the same, and he was aware of it, and glad of it, because it is a rare thing at seventy-one to be watched by someone who has watched you since you were twenty-five and knows the look of you deciding.

In the afternoon Marcus came, by day, with Dmitri driving, to be back before dark. He had come to return the farmhouse book, which he had borrowed, to check the dates of his confession against the record of the week. Present in the kitchen: Vance, Elena, Kira at the far end of the table from Marcus, Dmitri, Alexei, and Nikolai, who had come out of his flat for the first time in three weeks and was pale and quiet and sat where he could see his brothers.

Marcus put the farmhouse book on the table and began to say something about the dates.

Mikhail stopped him with a hand flat on the table.

He spoke standing by the stove, plainly, without rhetoric, because he had never in his life been able to bear rhetoric, his own least of all. It was the one speech he would make in the whole of this, and he had decided, over four days, exactly what it was for.

"I kept my memory in songs for forty years," he said, "so that no one would ever have to trust one man. That was the whole of it. A song can live in many mouths. I set the dangerous things to the gentlest tunes I knew, so that a child could carry them and not know, and a whole town could carry them and not know, and if they took me there would be forty others humming it who had never read a word. I did it so that the truth would never again depend on one old man's head, because one head can be emptied, and I know, because they emptied mine, many times, and missed the songs."

He looked at Marcus, who stood by the book with his glasses pushed up and his coat still on.

"You trusted one record. Because it said what you were afraid of. That is not a failure of the system, Marcus. Do not let anyone tell you it was a failure, least of all yourself, because a failure is a thing that can be fixed, and this cannot. That is how the system works. It works on the best of us first. It does not need to fool the careless; the careless fool themselves, and the system does not care about them; they are not in charge of anything. It needs the ones who check. You were the one of us who checked. That is why it chose you. It found the man who would not believe a thing without a witness, and it gave him a record instead of a witness, and it waited, and it was right to wait, because when the thing you fear is written down in front of you, even you will stop asking who wrote it."

The kitchen was quiet. Nobody moved. Alexei had started to say something, at the stove word, and Dmitri had touched his sleeve, once, and he had stopped.

"You understood the falling phrase in August," said Mikhail. "Every soul at this table understood it. I watched your faces. You went anyway. I wrote it in the book. Not to shame you. So that it would be written, by a hand, where it could be found, because that is the only thing I have ever trusted and the only thing I ever will. He asked. We said no. He went. Three sentences. True ones. They will outlive me."

"The man you were lying to," he said, "sent me my wife's name and my daughter's name."

He said it levelly, which cost him more than anything else in the speech.

"Anya. Katya." He said them once, the two names, in the open, among the people who knew what a name was for. "Forty years I carried them in a song because there was no record that would keep them, and a man I never met went into his nation's holdings on his own time and found the hour they died and the number of the run that killed them, and copied it out by hand, and sent it to me, because you told him over a lunch that an old man sang. He gave me my dead. That same month, you were learning at this table how to be the thing he was missing, so that you could use the trust that made him kind enough to do it." He drew a breath. "I broke a cup. It was a good cup. It sat right in my hand. I have been angry once in my life that I let anyone see, and that was it, and I am not angry now. I am past it. But I will not pretend it did not happen, because pretending a thing did not happen is the one crime in this house."

He let that sit.

"I do not forgive you," he said. "It is not mine to give. Some of what you owe is owed to me, for Anya and Katya, and some of that I might give you one day, when I am less tired. But most of what you owe is owed to a man in a cell across the lake, who is seen every second and whom nobody sees, and that debt is his to forgive or keep, and you must not ask him for it. Elena will have told you that already. She is right. It is the one thing you have not taken from him. Leave it."

A pause.

"And I am not sending you away." He looked at Marcus, who had not moved, whose face was doing the thing faces do when they are being held very still against something. "Somebody has to keep singing at you. A man alone with a thing like this goes one of two ways, and I have seen both, in forty years of rooms, and the way he does not go is the way where someone keeps singing at him. So I will. Not yet. Not today. But I am not sending you away, and you should know that, because you have been sitting in that chair like a man waiting to be sent away, and you can stop."

Nobody spoke when he finished.

Elena looked at her hands. Alexei looked at the floor. Nikolai, pale, looked at Marcus, and then at his own brothers, feeling them, Mikhail supposed, the way he always felt them. Kira looked at Marcus for the first time that day. Vance, who corrected everything, corrected nothing; she sat with her cup held and her eyes on the old man and her right hand, for once, not trembling, because it had something to do, which was to be still for this.

Then Mikhail sang.

He sang "The Two Maps", whole, for the first time, all three verses, to the tune he had begun in October at the woodpile, the morning after the broken cup. He sang it to Marcus, standing by the stove, in his cracked old voice, and he did not soften it.

Two maps of the one river, one for either shore; one puts the ford by the willows, one by the mill-house door.

They fought for the ford forty winters, and neither would give an inch; the river ran on between them and never once asked which.

But who drew the maps for the fighting, and sold them the ink and the quill — and why is the hand in the corner the same on both maps still?

The second verse was Anya's lakeshore, the shore of his boyhood, the ford and the mill-house and the river that ran between the two nations and had never once been asked whose it was. He had not meant, when he wrote it at the woodpile in October, for the river to be his wife; he had meant it to be the lakeshore, the border, the thing two nations fought over; and he had found, singing it now, that it was both, the way the Lakeshore Air was both a ferry song and a lullaby and the hidden setting of a dead woman's essay, one tune carrying whatever the singer could not say any other way. That was what a song was for. It held the things that would not go into sentences, the way the farmhouse book held he asked, we said no, he went, three sentences that were true and would outlive him, and the way this tune held Anya and the border and the hand in the corner all at once, so that a man could sing the whole of it to the boy who had cost him all three and not have to choose which grief he meant.

At the third verse he watched their faces. Dmitri, who had read Principle Two aloud at this table, shut his eyes. Kira, who had built the knife, looked at Marcus and did not look away. Nikolai's lips moved, learning it. Vance's cup was quite still.

The last line lifted at the end, like a question, as all his tunes lifted.

He let it hang. He did not bring it down. Nobody in the kitchen answered it, because every one of them could answer it, because the answer was typed on pages five to twelve of a copy numbered seven that had gone back into a box in Chronos: let there be several Histologic nations, differing in method and agreeing in substance, and let there be enemies outside them all. The same hand in the corner of both maps. They all knew whose hand the song meant, which was no one's and everyone's, the hand that had drawn the river in two places so that two peoples would fight over the difference. Nobody said it. You did not say a thing a song had already said better.

Marcus left before dark, because he had to.

At the door Mikhail hummed. Very low. One phrase. It was the first sound he had made at Marcus since the fifth of October, and it was neither the rising phrase nor the falling one; it did not go up and it did not go down; it sat level, held, and stopped. Marcus stood in the doorway and heard it, and did not know what it meant, because it was not yes and it was not no and it was not forgiveness, and Mikhail did not know entirely what it meant either, except that it was a sound, made at a man, which was not nothing, and which after two months of nothing was very nearly everything.

Marcus went. The headlamps went down the track. Mikhail closed the cracked door of the stove on the fire, and the glow went out of the crack, and Vance said, from the table, into the dark kitchen, the first thing she had said all afternoon:

"That was well done, Mikhail."

"No," said Mikhail. "But it was true. It is the best I can do now. They are not always the same, and a man my age should stop pretending he can make them be."

He took the kettle off the cold stove and set it by the sink for the morning, and did not sing "The Names" that night, for the first time since the fourth of October he had meant to and had not been able; and the second time, this one, he could, and did, silently, for Anya and Katya and Kerr, the three of them, where they had always gone, and the tune lifted at the end, like a question, and he let it, and slept.
