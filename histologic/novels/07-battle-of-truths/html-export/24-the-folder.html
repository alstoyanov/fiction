Chapter 24: The Folder

Monday, November 25, 2109. Kovač's flat; the Commission's testimony intake; the flat by the old river, Veridica Capital

At twenty to four in the morning, Helena Kovač was reading her nephew's log, and she came to the garden.

She had not slept properly since the fifteenth, the day the log went public. She read it the way she read testimony, which was her profession now, Registrar of Testimony, intake on the ground floor; she read it in order, through the transcript layer the directorate had released with it, stopping where the text could not carry the thing, which was often, because a log holds a heart rate and not a heart.

She had read a great deal of testimony in her year behind the intake. She had learned, reading it, a thing she had half known at Ashford: that the record and the truth were cousins who did not always speak, that a thing could be in the record exactly and still not be true, and that the gap between them was where people lived and died. She had thought the lesson was about Veridica, about the factorepo, about the white rooms she had run a facility full of. She had not thought it was about her own cupboard. She read her nephew's published log, three hundred and eighteen hours of it, a boy she had driven to a checkpoint at nineteen and not spoken to since, and she read it as testimony, for the gap, and she came to the garden, and the gap opened under her at twenty to four in the morning. She had reached the fifteenth of August, the Customs House garden, the emergency session under the Lakes Talks' terms. Julian, gray, on a bench under a pear tree in leaf, saying to the Veridican commissioner:

I don't understand how they used the key. I've only had it since—

And then a woman's voice, cool, courteous, on a point of protocol. Senior Historian. The doors close at the half-hour.

Helena played the half-sentence three times.

Since.

She pushed her glasses up her nose and sat very still in her small neat flat at twenty to four in the morning, with the plant dying of kindness on the sill and the folders in the hall cupboard, and she thought: there was a letter.

It had come before the war. Before the lake post stopped running, which it had on the second of May 2108, so it had come in the winter or the spring before that. In handwriting she had not seen since he was nineteen, forward-leaning capitals, each one eager for the next word. She had not answered it. She had not known how. She had put it in a folder, because that was what she did, and she had not thought about it since, because thinking about it had meant thinking about the nephew she had driven to a checkpoint in 2093 and not spoken to properly in all the years after, and she had enough she did not think about.

She got up and went to the hall cupboard.

She kept her folders in boxes, because paper could not be adjusted; she had learned that at Ashford, in four years of keeping a folder of the patients the protocols went too far with, because on paper nobody could change in the night what a thing had always said. The first box was Ashford. The second was the Commission. The third box was family: a few letters, a photograph from 2093, a child's drawing she could not throw away. She knelt on the cold floor in her nightdress and her coat and went through the third box, and found it, in an envelope with a Chronos lake-post frank, one sheet, folded in three.

It was dated at the top, in Julian's sloping hand, to the day, the way he dated everything.

20 January 2108.

They've made me Senior Historian. I start in February. They've given me the old man's keys. I'm frightened of them, Aunt Helena.

Signed, Julian.

She read it at the table, in the lamp, three times.

They've given me the old man's keys. In January 2108. They've made me Senior Historian. I start in February. He took the office on the first of February 2108, then. He had received the keys in January, the month before. By his own hand, in his own letter, written to her, before the war began, before there was any reason on earth for anyone to lie about the date of a Chronos historian's appointment.

The Chronos Gap had been the thirteenth of December 2107.

So the key had not been Julian's at the Gap. He had not held it. He had received it, from his predecessor, seven weeks after the Gap, in January 2108. And the Archive's public roll said key-holder since 2104, and his own log said 2104, and every record in Chronos said 2104, and all of it was false, and she had a letter on paper in her own cupboard, franked and dated, that said 2108, and had had it for nearly two years, and had never read it closely, because she had been afraid of what it would cost her to answer, and had filed it instead.

She said it aloud, in the empty flat, against herself, in the flat briefing voice she used for things that were true and hard.

"I kept a folder. That's what I did. I kept a folder."

She had laughed when she said that once, at Ashford, with the recording off. It had not been funny then. It was less funny now, because at Ashford the folder had been a thing she kept instead of acting and told herself was a kind of resistance, a record for some later reckoning, paper that could not be adjusted; and she saw now that a record kept and not acted on was not resistance, it was only a cleaner way of doing nothing, a way of being able to say, later, that you had at least written it down. She had written Julian's fear down. He had written to her, I'm frightened of them, a boy asking his aunt for help in the only way he had left, and she had filed the asking in a box marked family and not answered it, because answering it would have cost her something, and she had told herself the filing was care. It was not care. It was a folder. She had done it twice now, with two of her brother's sons, and the second time a nation had been rewritten in the gap of it.

Her own failure, made plain, because it was true and she would write it first, before anything else:

Last November, in Book-closed December, she had told the Veridican commissioner, Marcus Chen, about her nephew: that Julian had never travelled since he was given the key, that Chronos never let the key out of the building. She had known that he had been given the key. She had not given a date, because she had put the letter out of her mind, and when she had looked at his career, the once-a-year reading of the public roll in a library carrel with her coat on, the roll had said what she assumed, since 2104, and she had not followed his career closely enough in those years to know better. She had read the record. Not her own folder. Not her own cupboard, where the truth had sat, franked and dated, the whole time.

She had made Marcus's mistake first. Months before him. She had checked the record and not the witness, and she the one person in two nations who held the witness, in a box, in her own hall.

She wrote that down first, on the top of a fresh sheet, in her own hand, at a quarter past four in the morning, because it was the truest thing in the business and she would not let it be lost under the rest.

At nine she went to her office, behind the testimony intake, as she did every weekday.

She worked by procedure, because procedure was the only thing that held when the ground moved. She laid it out on paper, in order, the way she briefed a committee.

The letter: the twentieth of January 2108. They've given me. The keys were already his in January 2108. He took office on the first of February.

The Gap: the thirteenth of December 2107, seven weeks earlier. So the key was not Julian's at the Gap. It could not have been. He did not have it.

The public roll, read again on the Commission's terminal through the Synthesis exchange: MERA, A. Senior Historian emeritus from 2104. KOVAČ, J. Senior Historian. Key-holder since 2104. And on the same roll, Mera's own line, which gave his appointment as Senior Historian in 2088. Twenty years. Both of the 2104 entries were false. Mera had held the key, as Senior Historian, from 2088; he had retired, by Julian's letter, in January 2108; and the roll said both men had somehow held the key since 2104, which was a year in which nothing had happened to either of them that anyone could name.

The old man. Dr. Augustin Mera, Senior Historian from 2088 to January 2108.

The Chronos public death record, which she pulled through the exchange, because Chronos published everything: Dr. Augustin Mera. Died 18 February 2108. Seventeen days after Julian took office. Cremated the following day.

She sat and looked at the death record for a while.

She had signed the form a hundred times, at Ashford, for the dead of Wing C. Remains processed according to standard protocols. No family viewing. She had certified ten false deaths herself and sat under an inquiry while a Deputy Minister's body went to the fire within a day. She knew what a death that ended in paper and fire within a day looked like. She knew it from the inside, from having done it, to save people, so she did not pretend to herself that a quick cremation meant murder; she had made quick cremations mean the opposite. But she knew the shape of a staged exit, because she had staged them, and this had the shape.

She wrote died in quotation marks, and nothing after it. She did not know who Mera had been: the Chronos hand, or someone's instrument, or an old man who had simply died in February as old men do, and whose record had been edited around him for reasons that had nothing to do with his heart. She did not guess. It was not her business to guess. It was her business to establish that the date on the roll was false, and she had established it, with a letter on paper, and the rest she left open, because the rest was not in her cupboard and she would not invent what was not in her cupboard.

She put the letter in a card folder, marked it with the date and her initials, and let nobody copy the original. She would take it herself.

At half past eight that evening she stood at the door of the Chens' flat.

Kira was away at the farmhouse; Mr. Chen opened the door alone, thin, in his shirtsleeves, with his glasses pushed up and his lists folded on the table behind him. He looked at her and knew that she had come to say something, and stood back, and she did not go further than the door, as she never did.

"Mr. Chen," she said. "I won't keep you. There is something you should have, and something you should know, and the second comes with the first this time."

She briefed him flatly, in order, the way she had learned to brief. The garden, the half-sentence, I've only had it since. The memory it had jogged. The letter. The date. The roll. Mera. The twenty years. The seventeen days. The fire. She did not soften any of it, and she did not spare herself; she told him that she had told him the wrong thing last December, that she had read the roll and not her own folder, that she had made his mistake first and months before, that she had had the letter for nearly two years and never read it closely because she had been afraid to answer it.

Then she handed him the letter.

He read it twice, under the lamp, holding it by the edges the way a historian holds a thing. They've given me the old man's keys. I'm frightened of them, Aunt Helena. 20 January 2108. She watched it go in, and find the place it was going, the way she had watched a hundred things go into a hundred people across a hundred desks.

He said, almost to himself, not to her: "Nikolai said, 'I know the key. I don't know the hand.'"

She did not know who Nikolai was. She did not ask.

Then he looked at her, and said the thing, in a voice with nothing left in it: "I checked the record."

"Yes," said Helena Kovač. "You did."

She took the letter back before she left. She would hold the original; she did not trust anyone else to; it was her folder and her failure and her nephew, and she would carry it herself, the way she had carried Singh's page out of a safe in another winter, the way their kind of people carried things, in a hand, on paper, not in the record, because the record was the thing that had done this.

At the door she stopped, with her hand on the latch.

"I'll read it myself," she said. "When he confesses. If he confesses. It should be read by the one who kept it instead of acting. That's mine to do." She pushed her glasses up her nose. "I kept a folder, Mr. Chen. Twice now. I don't intend to keep a third."

She went out before he could answer, and down the stairs, with the letter against her ribs the way the keeper at Kestrel Point carried a book, and did not look back, because she had done the thing she came to do, and the doing of it did not need to be witnessed by the man she had done it for.
