Chapter 27: By Many Hands

Monday, December 9 – Friday, December 13, 2109. The farmhouse, the border zone; by link, Ward 9S and six kitchens on the Chronos shore

Dec 9, 2109. Incident log. Population affected: approx. 150 million. Record: consistent with Veridica's on every contested event. The Judge (Chronos): confirms, 0.93, Nov 4. Unaided memory: present, but overruled by the log in every citizen who checks. Objective: a disagreement.

Kira wrote it at the farmhouse table on Monday morning and looked at it.

She had built the thing that did this. She was not going to be the one who built the thing that undid it; a thing undone by the hand that did it was just the same hand, moving again, and the whole of what they had learned was that the hand could not be trusted, any hand, including hers, including Marcus's, including the one that meant well. What had been done had been done by one source, from above, to a hundred and fifty million people at once. It could not be undone the same way. If she sat down and wrote Chronos's old history back into the true source from above, she would only have done the thing twice, and proved the design right twice, and there would still be one source and one hand and no witnesses.

So it could not be a restoration. It had to be a disagreement. Chronos had no Doctrine of testimony, the way Veridica now did; what Chronos had was the Judge's reading of the collective will, from patterns in the facts. So the disagreement had to become a pattern the Judge would have to read. Not a correction from above. A thousand small refusals from below, each one a fact, until the facts made a shape.

She had built the thing that did the harm, and she had built it narrow, and the narrowness had meant nothing, because the thing it went into was one piece. She thought about that a great deal, over the week, at the farmhouse table in the early mornings before the others were up. The lesson was not that she had been careless; she had not been careless; she had been as careful as a person could be, and it had made no difference, because the fault was not in the knife but in the belief that a knife could be narrow when the thing it cut was whole. There was one source. Whoever held it held the present. That was Principle Three, which they had published believing it meant something smaller, and it meant this: that there was no narrow way to touch a single record, because there was no single record, there was one record wearing many faces, and to touch any face was to touch all of them. The only thing that was not one source was the thing that had no source at all: paper, in many hands, in many kitchens, checked against itself, with no master to restore it from because it had never been mastered. That was the whole of what she had left to build with, and she was glad of it, because it was the one thing she had built that had held.

She laid the pieces out on the table, in rows, the way she laid out everything.

The six Chronos keepers from the Night of Copies. They held paper books, in Chronos hands, that still carried the old lakeshore, the Temporal War as Chronos had told it, the Gap as a Veridican attack. Their paper had not changed, because paper was not in the true source. Six books, six kitchens, on the far shore.

The 9S patients, whose lives had never been logged, whose memories had nothing in the true source to be rewritten. Twelve of them. Tanaka recorded their testimony each night in the twelfth notebook, dated and timed and signed, and read it over the link at eight o'clock, flat and exact, in her case-note voice.

Julian's published log. Since the fifteenth of November it was the most complete record of the operation in existence: every hour, the access given, the night of the twenty-fifth, three hundred and eighteen hours of it, uncut, in Chronos's permanent record, put there by the man it was done to.

And Julian himself. At maximum, so that anything he agreed to was public by itself, needing no channel, logged as he did it. Adrian brought his answer across the lake on the Monday: he would help. And he asked one thing, which Adrian delivered word for word, and which Kira delivered to Marcus word for word, because it was Julian's and she would not soften it: that Commissioner Chen not contact him. Marcus said, "Yes." He stayed away all week. It was the one thing he could do for Julian, and it was to do nothing, which Kira thought was probably the hardest thing she had ever watched Marcus do, which was to be useful by being absent.

She watched him stay away. He came to the farmhouse once, by day, and sat at the far end of the table while she read the counts off the link, and did not touch the comparison sheets, and did not ask after Julian, and left before dark. He had written a thing on his lists that she had seen upside down, in his small pencil hand, under HOPES, which was the column he guarded most: That staying away is a thing I can give him, and not another thing I take. She had not told him she had read it. She thought it was true, that the staying away was a gift and not a theft, and she thought that Marcus did not quite believe it was true, and that the not-believing was the whole of his punishment and the one part of it she would not have taken from him even if she could, because a man who had done what he had done ought to doubt his own gifts for a while, and she loved him, strained and silent across a kitchen, and that was one of the things love let her know about him without having to say it.

She designed the bridge on one sheet of paper. She called it the comparison. It had three steps, and she wrote them plainly, so that a keeper could copy them by hand into a book and pass them on.

First: write first. In your own hand, before you open your log, write what you remember of the lakeshore and the Temporal War. What you were taught as a child. What your aunt told you. What you believe. Write it before you look.

Second: then look. Open your log, and read what it says you know now.

Third: if they differ, sign it, and self-disclose the minute you signed. That minute of your own log goes onto the public record, by the protected right that no Administration could forbid, so that the difference between your hand and your log became a fact the Judge had to read, a fact on paper and a fact in the record at once, the two of them disagreeing with a name against the disagreement.

The keepers copied the sheet by hand and passed it on by the pairing method, each copy into two others, in different towns. There was no index. There was no centre. Nothing went out from a source; it was the image built backwards, a thing that spread by being copied instead of by being broadcast, that had no master and could not be restored from one, because there was no one for it to be restored from.

The count came in daily, logged from the keepers' tallies over the link.

Monday, two hundred and twelve. Tuesday, one thousand three hundred and forty. Wednesday, three thousand nine hundred. Thursday, seven thousand six hundred.

Many who wrote first and then looked signed nothing, because their memory yielded to the log at once, the way the registrar's had, the way the nurse's had; they wrote what they remembered and opened the log and decided, without pain, that they must have misremembered, and did not sign, and went on. Kira logged those too. They were facts as well: the people whose memory gave way were a count, and the Judge would read that count against the other, and the other was smaller, and that was the truth of it, and she wrote the small number down beside the large one and did not pretend the large one away.

Each night at eight, over the link, Tanaka read 9S testimony in her flat exact case-note voice: the ford, the ferry, which shore the lakeshore had been, whether the old pier had stood. She read it dated, timed, placed, signed, in the patient's own words, and she made, each night, the distinction her whole voice was built on, that she was recording not what had happened but what they remembered, which was not quite the same thing, and Kira, listening at the farmhouse table, thought that the distinction was the most honest sentence in the whole operation, because everyone else on both shores was certain, and Tanaka alone said not quite the same thing, and the not-quite was the only crack through which a disagreement could grow.

And while she read, Old Songs hummed the Lakeshore Air, low, at the stove, the whole time, under Tanaka's voice coming thin out of the receiver, and nobody asked him to stop. On the Wednesday a keeper on the Chronos shore, hearing it down the line, hummed it back, two notes of it, before she caught herself, and there was a silence on the link, and then she went on reading her book. Kira wrote that down too, in the incident log, though she did not know how to classify it: two notes of a tune returned across a lake, on a logged line, by a woman who then went on writing by hand. It was not a fact the Judge would read. It was not a signature. It was two notes, and it was the first thing in the whole week that had made Kira believe the disagreement would outlast them, because a thing that people hum back is a thing that has got into them below the level the record reaches, where the songs had always lived, which was the old man's whole life's argument, made once, faintly, down a wire, by a stranger.

Julian's part came only through the keepers, because he would not speak to Kira's circle directly, and Marcus stayed away.

On the Thursday he published, at maximum, a short statement: that his log of the twenty-fifth of October was complete and true; that he had given the access; that he had been used. He never named Marcus. It was the most-read entry in Chronos that day, because a man at maximum who chose to disclose was a thing Chronos had never seen, and because what he disclosed was that the man the determination had cleared had used him, which the determination could not say, because the determination read the record, and the record no longer held it. Julian put it back, by hand, into the one place it could go, his own log, where it stood against the record of his own nation, one man's disclosure against a hundred and fifty million concordant lives, a minority of one that would not reduce.

One cost, on the Thursday night. A Chronos keeper, the woman who had said wait, I'm writing in January, was visited by directorate officers again, in her kitchen, and she kept writing. Her kitchen was logged in the officers' own interfaces as they stood in it. Nikolai, who counted such things, over the link, said: "That's two more officers who have now watched a Chronos citizen write by hand. They'll remember it. They can't help remembering it; they're Chronos; they record everything. We didn't reach them. She did. By writing while they watched." It was small. It spread the way small things spread, which was slowly, and by hand, and without a centre.

Friday the thirteenth of December. Two years to the day since the Gap.

By six in the evening the count was eleven thousand. Alexei brought the last bundle of the day's comparisons across the Drowned Causeway in the fog, real fog, and came up the track wet to the knees, and put the bundle on the table, and said, "Eleven thousand people who wrote it down before they looked. In a country where nobody writes anything by hand. Eleven thousand." He said it without the grin. "It's not many."

"It's not many," said Kira. "It's not nothing."

At ten past ten, Chronos's Judge published its weekly reading of the collective will.

It was a long document, as Chronos documents were, and most of it reported a nation concordant with itself, which it was. But in it, in one line, in the small type the Judge used for patterns below the main finding, was a thing nobody at the farmhouse table had seen before, a thing that had not existed a week ago:

A minority pattern, 0.07, of citizens whose unaided recollection disputes the record.

Kira read it twice.

Zero point zero seven. Seven in a hundred. It was small. It was, she thought, the smallest true thing she had ever helped to make, and the hardest-won, and it was there, in the record of a nation that erased nothing, a pattern the Judge had been forced to read because eleven thousand people had written down what they remembered before they looked, and signed it, and disclosed the minute they signed, so that their disagreement became a fact the facts could not get rid of.

"It's not a restoration," she said, to the room, and to nobody, because the room had gone quiet. "We didn't put their history back. We can't; there's one source, and whoever holds it holds the present, and we are not going to be the ones who hold it, not ever again, not even to do a good thing. It's not a restoration. It's a beginning of a disagreement. That's more than they had on Monday. That's more than they've had since the twenty-sixth of October. A country that can disagree with its own record by seven in a hundred is a different country from one that can't disagree at all."

At the stove, Old Songs hummed the Lakeshore Air, and the phrase lifted at the end, like a question, and he let it lift, and did not bring it down, because it was the right shape for the thing they had made, which was not an answer, which was the permission to go on asking.
