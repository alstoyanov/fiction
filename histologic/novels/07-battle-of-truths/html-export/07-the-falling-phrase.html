Chapter 7: The Falling Phrase

Wednesday, August 28, 2109. Elena's farmhouse, the border zone

The stove had a crack in its door, and he had learned the crack.

Three summers now he had kept the damper open two fingers' width, no more, so that the fire glowed at the crack and did not smoke, and the kitchen stayed warm enough for an old woman's hands without becoming a thing you had to open the window against in August. He did it without thinking now. His hands did it. He stood at the stove in the late afternoon with the light coming level through the yard window, and hummed the Lakeshore Air under his breath, each phrase lifting at the end and hanging there, like a question nobody had answered yet.

Elise sat at the table's end with her cup held in both hands. Her right hand had been worse this summer; he had watched it, the way he watched everything, and said nothing, the way he said nothing about most of what he watched. She held the cup to keep the hand still. She read a newspaper folded to the puzzle and did not fill the puzzle in, and listened to him hum, and now and then she corrected the paper under her breath, which was how he knew she was content.

The others came in over the afternoon. Kira first, out from the capital with Dmitri driving, and then Alexei from the barn smelling of diesel, and then Nikolai, who came and sat at the far end and looked at his hands and did not take any tea. Elena was in and out, counting things. By five the kitchen was full, and Mikhail Petrov, who had learned forty years ago how to be furniture in a full room, went on humming at the stove and let them fill it.

He had learned, in forty years of rooms, to read a room before anyone in it spoke, by the small things: where people put their hands, who sat with their back to a wall, who would not meet whose eyes. This room was a room waiting for bad news it had already half heard. Kira had the look she had worn since the night Marcus came home from the Cabinet Office, a stillness like a held breath, and she sat where she could see the door. Alexei was too loud, which was Alexei frightened. Dmitri was too quiet, which was Dmitri always, except that today the quiet had an edge to it, the particular attention he gave a thing he had decided to oppose and had not yet decided how. And Nikolai looked at his hands, which was what Nikolai did when a thing was his fault and was not, when the bond carried a guilt that belonged to all three of them and settled on the one who could least put it down. Mikhail read all of it, and hummed, and said nothing, because the saying was not yet his to do.

He had the slip in his shirt pocket.

Alexei had brought it the night before, from the lakeshore paper run: a square of paper torn from the back of an order book, folded once, with a name on it in square capitals, the way a careful person writes who wants to be sure. He had not read it aloud yet. He knew what it was. A name went into the song in front of people, or it did not go in at all, because the whole of the song was that it was sung where people could hear it and say yes, him, I knew him. He had carried it against his chest for a day, the way you carry a thing that is owed.

Marcus came last, as the light was going.

He came in from the yard in his good Commission coat, thin, with his glasses pushed up on his forehead, and he did not sit. He stood by the stove, a little apart from the old man, and told them.

He told it standing, in order, the way he told everything: the seven dead, the child at Marram End, the slip and the water; the key on the Rewriting and the roll that said Julian had held it since 2104; the First Minister's office, and the transmitter units waiting in the wings, and the word Wei had given the thing, and the joint inquiry that would be the cover. He did not soften any of it. Mikhail, humming very low now, almost nothing, watched him not soften it and thought that this, at least, the system had not taken: the boy still put every fact on the table face up, even the ones that cut his own hand.

When he finished, the kitchen was quiet.

Elise set her cup down. Her hand began at once to tremble, and she laid it flat on the table and held it there with the other, and spoke into the quiet without any heat at all, in the dry exact voice Mikhail had argued with across a reading-room table when they were young.

"They designed you to be the restorer," she said. "This is the restoration."

Marcus looked at her. He did not answer.

"This is wrong, Marcus," said Kira. She said it very level, which was how she said the things she was most sure of. "This is what we fought against."

Elena said nothing. She stood by the door with her coat still on and counted the room, left to right, the way she counted a patrol, and then she looked at the table and not at anyone in it.

"If it stops the storms—" said Alexei, from the bench, and stopped, because he had heard where the sentence was going and did not like it, and could not finish it, and hated that he could not.

"I would ask for time," said Dmitri, formally, without a single contraction, from his place by the door. "That is all. Time to look at it again, with the tools in front of us, before anything is decided."

Nikolai, at the far end, said nothing. He looked at his hands. It had been his evidence, in February and again two weeks ago, that had put the key in Julian's name. Nobody looked at him, which was its own kind of looking.

They said their pieces, each of them, for their own reasons, and every reason was a good one, and Mikhail stood at the stove and did not say anything at all, because it was not his to say in words.

He thought about Julian Kovač.

He had met him once, on a marsh road in January, in the fog, a narrow man by a small gray car who had said nine minutes early, I didn't mind, and had driven three Veridicans into the permanent record of his own nation under his own name, and had told them so before the first step, so that they could turn back. Mikhail had watched a man do that with his eyes open, and had decided that it was either the bravest thing or something else, and had not been able to tell which, and had come down on the side of brave, because a man who warns you before he lets you into the trap is not setting one.

Julian had taken them to a sealed ward on the ninth floor on his Archive card, and had stopped three metres from the doors and said inside there I'd drop out of my own log, and someone would ask me why, and had turned and gone back down the lift alone, into the fog, into the country that forgot nothing, having brought Elise Vance's son's mother's rescuers to her door and kept not one second of the crossing out of his own record.

Mikhail owed him that crossing. He owed him Elise. A man did not often, at seventy-one, acquire a new debt of that size to a stranger, and Mikhail had acquired this one in a single January night, and had meant to carry it the way he carried the others, quietly, in a song eventually, when the man was dead and could be sung. The man was not dead. The man was across the lake, alive, in a flat with his desk turned from a window, being, this very month, made to love the person who would ruin him. You could not sing a living man into "The Names". You could only watch, and Mikhail had spent forty years watching, and had thought he had seen the whole of what the system did to people, and found that he had not, because he had never before watched it reach through someone he loved to destroy someone he owed.

And now the boy at the stove was going to use him. Was going to do to that narrow careful man the thing that had been done, once, to a believer at a café table by a fountain: find the place where he was open, and put a hand in it. For good reasons. Always for good reasons.

He did not speak.

He hummed the Lakeshore Air through once, whole, the way he always did, each phrase climbing at the end and hanging. The kitchen quieted to let him; they had learned, all of them, that when the old man took up a tune it was worth stopping for.

And at the last phrase, where every tune of his lifted, he took it down.

Low, and slow, the whole phrase bending downward and settling at the bottom, like a thing laid in the ground. It was not a tune anyone in that kitchen had heard him sing on this side of the lake. It was from the other side of a meal slot, from a wing of glass cells, where a hummed tune had been the only language that could pass a door and an orderly would never think to question. A rising third had meant yes. A falling last phrase, low and slow, had meant no.

Five people in the kitchen had learned it through a slot in a cell door at Ashford, three times a day, and their bodies knew it before their minds caught up. Marcus's face changed. Kira went still. Dmitri's jaw set. Alexei shut his eyes. Nikolai's head came up for the first time all evening. Elena and Elise had never learned it, but they read it in the five faces, and Elise, who had designed the wing those cells stood in without ever seeing it, set her cup down and looked at the old man for a long moment, and did not say anything, because for once there was nothing in the room to correct.

It was no.

It was the whole of what he had to say, and he had said it in the one language that the people he was saying it to could not argue with, because it had kept them alive. He could have made a speech. He had the words; he always had the words; he had spent his whole life keeping words in tunes, and he could have stood at the stove and told the boy, in plain sentences, every reason this was wrong, the war that was built, the man who was owed, the thing the design did to the ones who checked. He did not. A speech could be argued with. A speech gave a man something to answer, and Marcus was clever and desperate and would find an answer, and then they would be two clever desperate men trading reasons across a kitchen while a child lay dead at Marram End to weigh against a stranger across a lake. The falling phrase could not be argued with. It was not a reason. It was a thing their bodies knew from a wing of glass cells, before their minds could get hold of it, and you cannot argue with a thing your body knows. He gave them the thing their bodies knew. It was the truest no he had, and the only one that could not be turned into a conversation.

Marcus heard it. Mikhail watched him hear it. He stood at the stove a moment longer, thin, with his glasses pushed up and his coat still on, and then he said, "Thank you," which was worse than if he had said nothing, and took his car keys from the table, and went.

Kira did not follow him out to the yard. Through the window Mikhail watched the headlamps come on and go down the farm track and turn onto the lakeshore road and dwindle, and the dusk come down on the yard behind them.

At dusk he went out into the yard with the slip, and he sang.

Everyone who had stayed came out and stood in the yard, in the warm dark, by the barn. Elise came last, on Elena's arm, and sat on the bench by the door. Here, on this side of the lake, where he was free, he sang with words. He had sung "The Names" in this yard before, for the thirty-one of the first war, and the words of it were plain, and he sang them plainly.

Who went down to the water? Name them, and they stay.

He took the slip from his pocket and unfolded it and read the name on it once, to himself, silently, and then he put the name into the third line, where the song had room for one, and sang it down to the water with the thirty-one from the winter.

— went down to the water, name them, and they stay.

The record may forget them, the song will not forget; name them, and they stay.

He did not say the name aloud except in the line of the song, and in the dark, among the people who had known what a name was for, it went into the air and into them and was kept. There were six more dead since the winter whose names nobody had sent him. He would not sing a number; a number was the thing the record did, and he had never once agreed with the record about anything. So where the six names should have gone he hummed the place, six times, the bare tune with no words in it, and the tune lifted at the end each time like a question, and the people in the yard understood that it was asking for the names, and that they would come.

Elise listened to all of it with her hand steady on her knee, and when it was over she did not say anything, which from Elise was the highest thing she had.

Late, when the kitchen was empty and the others had gone to their beds or to the barn or to wherever Dmitri went to stand with his back to a wall, Mikhail banked the stove for the night and closed the cracked door on it, and took the farmhouse book down from the dresser.

It was the house's own book, the one Kira had started, that the people who passed through wrote in. He had written in it before. He had written a name in it once, in November, the first name he had ever set down on paper that he meant to keep, because a thing that is written down can be found, and for forty years that had been reason enough never to write anything. And he had written four lines into the back board of another book in the spring, the round that could not be sung alone.

This was the third thing.

He wrote it in his slow archivist's hand, in pencil, by the light of the one lamp, and did not sign it, because it did not need a name under it.

He asked. We said no. He went.
