Chapter 19: They Wrote Us

Friday, November 1, 2109. The Administration of Temporal Defence, and the directorate building by the harbour, the Chronos lakeshore city

At half past six on Friday morning Constance Aubry sat in her office, which had a desk and two chairs and a screen and nothing else, and read the overnight concordance report, and her deputy stood in the doorway and asked her, in good faith, what exactly the crisis was.

He was not being insolent. He was a careful officer, and the report was in front of both of them, and it said what the record said: that the lakeshore province was concordant on every contested event, that the Temporal War and the Gap and the Rewriting all now read consistently across the primary and the backups, that the Judge had confirmed it. From where her deputy stood, the war was over. Chronos's account and Veridica's account no longer disagreed. The thing Temporal Defence existed to defend, the integrity of the record, was, by every measure the Administration had, intact. So: what was the crisis?

Aubry thought in distributions. It was how she had always thought; she saw the world as a set of overlapping curves and spent her life working out where they crossed. She considered the probability that every contested record in a nation of a hundred and fifty million had moved, in one direction, in one night, by accident, or by honest reconciliation, or by any cause that was not a hand. It was not small. It was nil. There was no distribution in which that happened by itself.

And she considered the probability that she could prove it. That was nil too. Because the only evidence that the record had been changed was a memory of its having said something else, and a memory, in Chronos, was not evidence. It was the thing evidence corrected.

"The crisis," she said to her deputy, "is that we have won, and I do not believe we have won, and I cannot tell you why, and neither can anyone else, because the reason is not in the record any more. That is the crisis. You may go."

He went, puzzled, content, a man whose record was whole.

She kept paper.

It was Temporal Defence practice, and she had always privately thought it a vanity, a ritual left over from the founding years, when the Administration had not yet fully trusted its own systems: every directive she signed was printed and filed, in ink, in a drawer, in her own office. She had signed a thousand of them and never once needed the paper.

She needed it now. She opened the drawer and took out the printed order of the twenty-first of August, the one that had resumed corrective broadcasting in response to Veridica's second attack on the memory of Chronos. She had signed it herself. It was in her hand, with the date, printed on the morning she signed it. And the record's own copy of that order, which she had checked on Monday, now read otherwise: it read that the broadcasting had resumed in response to a border instability of undetermined origin, with no mention of Veridica, because in the new concordant history Veridica had attacked nothing, and an order could not cite an attack that had not happened.

On Monday the twenty-eighth she had submitted the printout to the Judge, formally, as evidence of discrepancy. The Judge had weighed one unlogged document, printed by one officer, against a hundred and fifty million concordant records, and had set it aside, with courtesy, as the Judge set aside everything that stood alone against the collective will. One sheet of paper against a nation. She had known it would. She had done it anyway, because it was the correct procedure, and because she wanted it on some record somewhere that she had tried, even a record that would then be told she had not.

She put the printout back in the drawer. It was the only thing she owned that remembered the war the way she did, and the Judge had told her it was wrong, and she believed the Judge about everything except this, and she knew that believing the Judge about everything except this was precisely the shape of the illness the Administration corrected people for, and she held both of those thoughts at once, coolly, because she was Constance Aubry, and holding two curves at once was her whole profession.

The Archive's record of the night, which she read in order, because order was how she read:

The reconciliation rooms had been opened at ten o'clock on Friday the twenty-fifth, under co-chair access, granted by Kovač, J., on Friday the eighteenth. A run from the true source had begun at twenty to three and finished at about six minutes past four. It had been accepted as legitimate, because by the record's own definition it was legitimate; it had carried a master authority, and the master was the master. And during the run, the Archive key's standing to sign in the true source had been revoked, which the record read as the Archive correcting a compromised key, protecting itself, doing its job.

Every line of it exonerated the system and implicated one man. The access was Julian's. The grant was in his hand. The key was his. The record, which could no longer be made to say that Veridica had attacked Chronos, said very clearly that a Veridican delegation had been let into the reconciliation rooms by the Chronos Senior Historian, who held the key, and that after that night the key was dead and the nation's past agreed with its enemy's.

She knew it was wrong. She knew it the way she knew the printout was right. And her own signature was on the thing that had let it happen: she had accepted the Joint Inquiry within two days, in August, because Chronos had wanted its key's misuse traced with Veridican witnesses present, and she had thought she was using the commissioner, again, as she had used him with MERIDIAN, and she had been the one who was used. She filed that against herself, coldly, in the drawer where she kept the things she had got wrong, which was a real drawer, with real paper in it, and went down to the interview.

At ten o'clock they brought Julian up from detention.

He had been held since the morning of the twenty-sixth, in the directorate building by the harbour, in the clean warm detention wing with its soft chairs and its window on the water and a camera in each corner. He came into the interview room in yesterday's cardigan, unshaven, gray, exact, and sat where she gestured, and folded his hands, and waited.

She was courteous. She was always courteous. She did not tell people what to do; she told them what was likely; it was the only honest thing an interrogator could offer, she had decided long ago, since everything else she might say was a lever.

She took him through the key, the logs, the hours with the Veridican commissioner. He answered everything. He did not evade. He had given the commissioner the access, he said, because he trusted him. He had not known what would be done with it. He had not done the thing himself; he had been asleep in his own bed, logged, and his log said so.

"The key," said Aubry. "How long have you held it?"

"Since 2108," said Julian. "I received it from my predecessor in January 2108. The keys in January. The office in February. I took up the post on the first of February." He said it plainly, without emphasis, the way a man states a date he is sure of. "I call him the old man. Mera. Dr. Augustin Mera. He had it for twenty years before me."

Aubry laid the record out for him, in order, on the table, because he was a historian and would want to see it, and because she wanted to watch him see it.

The Archive's public roll: KOVAČ, J. Senior Historian. Key-holder since 2104. The key's own signing history, one countersignature every night since 2104, under his name. His own log's system layer, which carried each of those nightly countersignatures, 2104 to the present, his. And her own printed MERIDIAN annex, from November 2108, a personnel sheet she had drawn up herself when the directive was running, which read, in a line she had typed and printed and filed and never once doubted: senior reconciliation key, held since 2104. Paper. Her paper. It agreed with the record, because she had copied it from the record, in 2108, a year before Veridica came, when there had been no reason on earth to edit the date of a Chronos historian's appointment.

"The roll says 2104," she said. "Your own log says 2104. My own paper, printed a year ago, says 2104. You say 2108. Mr. Kovač, I have one unlogged recollection of yours, and four records, including one on paper in my own drawer, all of which say otherwise."

"Show me the January morning," said Julian.

He said it quietly. He was a historian; he knew the one move left to a man against the record, which was to go to the record itself and find the place where it failed. "The morning in January 2108 when I remember the old man coming to my door with the keys. It was early. It was before the office opened. Show me my own log for that hour."

She had it brought up on the screen.

His log for that January morning showed him alone at his desk in the Archive, working, at the hour he said the old man had come to his door with the keys. No old man. No handover. No keys. A man alone, working, logged, breath by breath, at a desk, on a morning he remembered entirely differently.

He watched it twice. He was very still.

"That isn't what happened," he said.

"In Chronos, Mr. Kovač," said Aubry, not unkindly, "that is what happened means."

She believed the record. She believed it against him on the one point where it was lying, and she had, she thought, better reason to believe it than any interrogator had ever had, because she had four records and her own paper, and because the man across the table was offering her a memory, and a memory was the thing her whole Administration existed to distrust. She was not stupid. She knew that the record had moved in the night, everywhere, in the direction of Veridica. She considered the probability that it had moved on this one point too, the point that would clear him, as part of the same movement. And she weighed it against the four records and the paper, and she found that the probability the record was wrong in exactly the two places that would exonerate a man caught holding every key to the crime, was small. Smaller, she judged, than the probability that a guilty key-holder would say precisely this, and have a false memory to say it with, or the wit to pretend to one.

She said so. She was fair. She told him the number as near as she could, because telling a man the likely shape of his own case was the only kindness she had to give, and it was a cold one.

She remembered the garden.

She had read the twenty-second of November in his log, in the winter, four times, the day MERIDIAN was published. And she remembered, because she had been there, a morning in August at the Customs House, the fifteenth, when she had come out into the garden on a point of protocol and cut across a sentence he was saying to the commissioner on a bench. I've only had it since— She had not heard the end of it. She had not asked. She had been busy; the doors were closing at the half-hour; it had been innocent, entirely innocent, a thing she did a dozen times a day.

She had the end of the sentence now, eleven weeks late, in an interview room. Since 2108. And she did not believe it, because she had four records and her own paper, and because, she admitted to herself, coolly, in the private drawer, she did not want to believe it, because believing it meant that the one point on which her whole case stood was the one point the record had lied about, and that she had cut across the truth in a garden with a point of protocol and not asked, and that was a thing she would have to carry, and she was not ready to carry it, so she set it aside, with courtesy, as the Judge had set aside her printout.

"Either way," she said, "you are Veridica's instrument or Veridica's fool. The sentence is the same for both."

"Is the war over?" he asked her.

She considered the question honestly, which was the only way she considered anything.

"They didn't defeat us," said Constance Aubry. "They wrote us."

She told him the law, plainly, before she left, because she was fair and he had the right to know it.

At maximum recording, she said, his log would be streamed in full to the directorate, every heartbeat, nothing sealed. And the right of self-disclosure survived the sentence; it always had; a citizen could publish any part of his own log at any time, and no Administration could forbid it, even an outlaw, even a man at maximum. She said it to be fair. She did not say it as a hint. She had never in her life given a hint; she gave probabilities. She only thought it right that a man should know what rights he kept, even when the keeping of them seemed, to her, pointless, because nobody at maximum had ever used self-disclosure, there being nothing left to disclose that the directorate had not already seen.

She did not know that she had just told him the one thing he would do with the rest of his life.

At half past five she wrote the recommendation herself.

She did not delegate it. It was the maximum sentence for an Archive key-holder, and she thought the officer who recommended a thing should be the one who wrote it, in her own words, and own it. Recording forced to maximum. Removal from office. She wrote it, and read it once, and printed it, as she printed everything, and signed it in ink.

The Judge ratified it before evening.

She filed the printout in the drawer, beside the order of the twenty-first of August that the record no longer agreed with, and the MERIDIAN annex that said 2104, and the note that she had accepted the Joint Inquiry in two days. Four sheets of paper, in one drawer, in an office with nothing else in it. She closed the drawer.

She was not cruel. She had never been cruel in her life. She was certain, and certainty, she had always known, was a harder thing than cruelty, because cruelty could be ashamed, and certainty could not, and she went home that night certain that she had done the correct thing with the evidence she had, which she had, and that she had helped to destroy an innocent man, which she had, and she held both, coldly, in the two hands she had, because that was the whole of what she was.
