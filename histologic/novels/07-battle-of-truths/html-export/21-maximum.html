Chapter 21: Maximum

Monday, November 11 – Friday, November 15, 2109. The directorate building by the harbour, the Chronos lakeshore city

On the eleventh day at maximum, Julian Kovač woke and did not look at the time.

He knew it anyway, to the second, because he always did; but he had stopped scrolling to it, because scrolling to it was a thing you did when the time was yours, and the time was not his any more. Nothing was his any more. At maximum, his log streamed in full to the directorate as he lived it: every heartbeat, every glance, every word, every dream his eyes moved behind. The officer at the door had read what he had dreamed. There was no sensation to it. That was the thing nobody warned you of. It did not feel like being watched. It felt like nothing at all, and the nothing was the whole of it, because a man can brace against being watched and cannot brace against a thing he cannot feel.

He had been removed from office on the first. He was no longer Senior Historian. The key was gone; its standing in the true source had been revoked on the night itself, so that even if they had given it back to him it would have signed nothing. He was a private citizen, at maximum, in a clean warm room with a window on the harbour and a camera in each corner, and the fog far out on the lake where the far shore was.

They had given him back his handwritten journal with the sentence.

In Chronos, paper was nothing; a thing written by hand was a curiosity, a medical oddity; it carried no weight and broke no rule, and so there was no reason to keep it from him, and they had not. It had been returned with his other effects on the first. The joke of it, which he could appreciate, dryly, in the mornings, was that now his eyes recorded every line he wrote in it as he wrote it, so that the one private thing he had ever made, the book that was not in the log, was in the log after all, because the log had his eyes, and his eyes were on the page.

He kept writing in it anyway. He did not fully know why. He wrote in it at night, when the room was dark and the camera's little indicators were the only light, and he knew the directorate had it as he made it, and he made it anyway, because the making of it by hand was a thing the log could not have, even if the content could. The log could know that he had written something. It could not read the page. You had to read the page. He found that he liked that, still, more than he could explain.

On the twelfth he began the reading.

A citizen kept read access to his own log, even at maximum. It was the one thing the sentence left him. And he was a historian; there was only one thing a historian does with a record he has been living inside blindly; he reads it. So he read himself.

He started at the ninth of September, the opening of the inquiry, the day he had not been able to look at Marcus across the table. He read forward slowly, the way he read a source, pausing where the text could not carry the thing, going back to re-read a passage that did not sit right.

His log held what his eyes had caught without his noticing. Where Marcus had looked, in the half-seconds when Julian was not watching him. The sixteenth of September, the Archive steps, I hoped it would be you, and his own heart rate, which the log recorded, rising fourteen seconds after the reading-room door opened each morning, every morning, for six weeks. He had never scrolled to it while it was happening, out of a kind of modesty, and now he scrolled to it, every instance of it, and watched his own body give him away, day after day, in a record that had kept it all and would keep it forever.

The eighteenth of October. The access, given unasked, in the reconciliation rooms, with the chipped cup on the shelf. And Marcus's face in the half-second after, which Julian's log had caught because Julian had been looking at the terminal and his eyes had been pointed at Marcus's face and had kept it: a thing that was not quite success and not quite grief and was, Julian understood now, reading it for the fourth time, both. He watched it four times. He could not stop watching it. It was the face of a man who had just been handed a door and knew what the door was for.

The evenings in his flat. The journal page he had turned past too quickly, the entry headed M., which he did not open now either, because he still could not. The garden at Saltmarsh Crossing, talked about as a hope, in the leaf, properly, when this was over. The twenty-third of October, at his door, Marcus, I—, and the stop, and drive carefully, the fog's in, and Marcus's hand on his arm, for a moment, and then gone.

He read it all. It took three days. He was a historian; he was thorough; he did not skip the parts that hurt, because skipping the parts that hurt was how you got a history wrong.

On Wednesday the thirteenth, Adrian came.

He came across the lake again, by the route he never described, and was brought up to the family room, which was like the interview room but with softer chairs, and a camera in each corner, so that the brothers sat together in a room where everything they said went live to the directorate as they said it.

Adrian was easy and theatrical at first, prowling, taking in the room, making a joke about the chairs. Then he sat down across from Julian and was blunt, which was the other thing Adrian did.

"I knew," said Adrian. "In September. At dinner. When I asked if he was kind to you, and you said yes too fast. That was the odd look, little brother. That's what I couldn't say. I'd stood where he was standing. I did it to a woman I loved, years ago, in Veridica. I knew the shape of it from the inside the second I saw your face." He looked at his hands. "I didn't tell you. You were happy. I'd never seen you happy. I couldn't be the one to take it off you. So I went to her instead. To Kira. I told her to stop him." A breath. "She didn't tell him I came. And he didn't stop."

"It was real on his side," said Julian. He did not know if he believed it. He wanted to say it aloud, to see if it held, into the log, where it would be kept. "Some of it. It must have been. You can't—"

"It was real on mine too," said Adrian. "To the woman. That's the part that doesn't help. It was real and I still did it and it still destroyed her. Real doesn't undo the doing." He leaned forward. "I'll hate him for you. If you want. I'm good at it. I've got years of practice hating people on other people's behalf. Say the word."

"No," said Julian.

They talked about their father, who had stopped saying Julian's name eleven years ago and had not started again. They talked about Helena, who had driven Julian to the checkpoint in 2093 because their father would not. Adrian promised to come back. He did come back; he would be there, in the end, in January, beside Julian, in a room where nothing was private. But that was later.

On Thursday the fourteenth, Julian finished.

He read to the twenty-fifth of October, the Inquiry's closing session, and Marcus staying on afterwards to finish the draft report, which was in the log, Marcus's co-chair access opening the service door at two minutes past ten at night, which Julian's own log did not show, because Julian had been asleep, logged, breath by breath, in his bed, with his eyes shut, while five people he could not see came up into the Archive on access he had signed in his own hand.

And then he went, in the Archive's public bulletin, to the night of the twenty-fifth to the twenty-sixth, and to what Chronos had woken up believing, and he saw the whole shape of it at once, the way you see the shape of a proof when the last line goes in.

His trust had been the door. His key had been the lock. He had held the door open himself, signed the grant himself, given the access himself, unasked, so that Marcus would not have to take it through him, so that Marcus could see there was nothing behind it. There had been nothing behind it. That was true. There had been nothing behind the key at all; the key had only ever been a way in; and he had handed the way in to a friend, and the friend had used it to write Veridica's history into a hundred and fifty million heads, and his nation had woken up believing its enemy, and he was the man who had opened the door.

He said it aloud, at maximum, in the clean warm room, where it went live to the directorate as a confession, which was what they had wanted.

"I helped them erase my own nation."

He read one more thing, before he decided.

He went back, because he was a historian and a historian goes back, to the thirteenth of December, 2107, the night of the Gap. The night everyone in the province had scrolled to, a year ago, to find the seventeen minutes gone from their sleep. His said what everyone's said. 03:12:00 – 03:29:00. Asleep. Heart rate steady. He had looked at it a hundred times and taken it for what it was, proof that he had slept through the sacrilege like everyone else.

He looked at it now with different eyes, because now he knew what a log could be made to say.

And he went back further, to the January after, to the morning he had shown Aubry, the morning he remembered the old man coming to his door with the keys, which his log said was a morning he had spent alone at his desk. He had held both of those in his head for twenty months without ever setting them side by side, the way you hold two things you have never had cause to compare. His memory said: the old man, the keys, January 2108, the office in February. His log said: 2104, alone at the desk, the key already his for years. He had believed the log. Of course he had believed the log; he was a Chronos historian; the log was the record and the record was the truth and a memory that disagreed with it was the thing that needed correcting, not the record. He had carried the two for twenty months the way the people of Kestrel Point carried their two piers, both of them solid, neither of them examined, and he had told himself, on the rare mornings he thought of it at all, that he must be misremembering the January, that the handover must have been a formality after years of holding the key, that his memory had tidied a quiet ceremony into something it was not.

He saw now that it was the other way round. The log had been tidied, and his memory had not. He did not know when. He did not know by whom; the edit predated the operation, predated RESTORATION, predated everything he could see; someone had reached into the true source and moved the date of his appointment from 2108 to 2104, years ago, so that when the night came he would be the man who had held the key all along. He had spent twenty months deferring to the lie because deferring to the record was the whole of what it meant to be what he was.

He stopped deferring to it. He was perhaps the only man in the building who did. It changed nothing about his sentence. It changed everything about what he was.

On Friday the fifteenth of November, at two minutes before two, he sat in his room facing the window, with the fog far out on the lake, and the camera's indicators steady in the corners.

It was the Day of Testimony. At two o'clock, across the lake, in a plaza he could not see, people would come to a plain gray stone and read out ordinary days. It was Marcus's hour; the stone, the readings, the whole doctrine of testimony that Marcus had put back into the world. Julian had chosen it on purpose. He was a historian; he knew the value of a date.

The right of self-disclosure survived the sentence. Aubry had told him so, to be fair, not as a hint. Nobody at maximum had ever used it, because there was nothing left to disclose that the directorate had not already seen. But disclosure was not for the directorate. Disclosure was for the world, for the record of a nation that erased nothing, permanent, timestamped, beyond recall. And there was a thing the directorate had seen that the world had not, and it was his to give.

He would publish every hour he had spent in Marcus Chen's company. From the first day of the Lakes Talks, the twelfth of November 2108, to the closing session on the twenty-fifth of October 2109. Uncut. Three hundred and eighteen hours. His heart rate rising fourteen seconds after a door opened. The access given unasked. The garden. The bench. The thing he had almost said. Everything he had felt and never spoken, which the log had kept because the log kept everything, published now by the one man it had been used against, in full, so that it could not be cut, because cutting was what had been done to his log and to his country, and he would not do it, not to a second of it.

At two o'clock he spoke the formal words of disclosure.

It was instant, and permanent, and timestamped to the nanosecond, and it went to the world. The indicator at the door changed. Nobody came in.

He sat down, and opened the journal, by hand, to a clean page, where his eyes recorded that he had written something and could not read what, and he began to write.
