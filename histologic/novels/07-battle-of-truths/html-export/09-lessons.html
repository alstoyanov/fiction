Chapter 9: Lessons

Monday, September 2 – Monday, September 16, 2109. The farmhouse; the lakeshore checkpoint road; the Chronos Temporal Archive, the Chronos lakeshore city

September second, 2109. Nineteen hundred hours. Farmhouse, border zone. Involved parties: Chen, M.; Volkov, E. Purpose: instruction. Subject:

He could not write the name.

He sat at the kitchen table with the pencil over the page and the stove warm behind him and Elena across from him with her unwritten notebook, and he came to Subject and could not put the name there, because a subject was a thing an operation acted on, and he had sat at this table, in effect, from the other side, four years ago, as a believer at a café by a fountain who had been, without the word, somebody's subject. He left it blank. Elena watched him leave it blank and said nothing, and he understood that she had expected it, and had probably left the same field blank herself in 2105, in whatever record she kept in her head and never wrote down.

"The cover first," said Elena. "So you know what you have."

The cover was clean. That was the strange thing about it: the lie was true.

Maitland's office had proposed a Joint Inquiry into the Rewriting, co-chaired by the Veridican commissioner and the Chronos Senior Historian, sitting in the reading rooms of the Chronos Temporal Archive, to trace the misuse of the Archive's key. Chronos had accepted it within two days. Chronos wanted exactly that: its key's misuse traced, on its own ground, with Veridican witnesses present to be shamed by what was found. Aubry had signed the acceptance. The inquiry was real. It would sit, and take evidence, and produce a report, and the report would be honest, because Marcus meant it to be. The inquiry was not the operation. The inquiry was the room the operation would happen in.

The Veridican delegation sat under the Lakes Talks' Synthesis protocol, which meant Marcus would cross the lakeshore checkpoint by day, be escorted in public, carry no Chronos monitoring, and be logged by every Chronos citizen who laid eyes on him, like any visitor. Maitland's office had passed on one thing, which Elena made him write down, because it mattered: that when Chronos was asked whether Veridica had named its co-chair, Julian Kovač's first question in return had been whether Veridica had.

"He wanted it to be you," said Elena. "Before he knew it was. Write that down. Not because it helps. Because you should have to look at it."

Marcus wrote it down.

The lessons ran in the afternoons from the second to the sixth, before the inquiry opened, and then in the evenings from the ninth to the thirteenth, after the day's crossing, at the kitchen table with the back-room door shut. Old Songs and Vance did not come to them. Elena had asked for that, and Marcus had not argued.

She briefed. She did not explain. She had her notebook that she never wrote in, and she sat with her hands flat on the table and gave him the thing in pieces, each piece complete.

"You don't make him trust you," she said, the first afternoon. "You make it easy for him to choose to. A man defends a choice. He doesn't defend a gift. If you push, he feels the push and he braces, even if he doesn't know why. So you don't push. You stand where a man who wanted to trust you could reach you, and you let him reach."

"Find what he's missing and be it. Same hour, same chair. In Chronos everyone he knows is on file, every word anyone has said to him is in his log and theirs, readable. You are the one thing in his life he has to take on trust, because you are the one thing that isn't recorded against him. That is the whole of your value to him. It is also the whole of the wound."

"Tell him the truth about everything except the one thing. He will keep every word to the nanosecond; it is Chronos, he cannot help it. So lie as little as you can, and never where it can be checked. The one lie is that you are his friend. Everything around it is true. That is what makes it work, and that is what makes it unforgivable."

"Don't ask him for anything. Especially not the key. A man offers what he has never been asked for. If you ask, he gives you a thing he can later say he was asked for. If he offers, he gives you a thing he chose, and he will defend having chosen it long after he should stop."

"Let him see you almost say something. Then don't. A man will fill a silence you leave with the thing he most wants to be true."

She played Julian, in the exercises, out of his own published MERIDIAN log, which anyone could read, because Julian had published it himself in December.

She was very good. She was better than good; she was frightening. She took the narrow careful voice from the log and the way the man apologised for the seconds, and she set little traps in it, the way the real one would without meaning to. Watching her do it, Marcus understood for the first time, properly, with his whole body, what had been done to him in 2105. He had known it as a fact for four years: a woman had asked him for directions by a fountain and it had been technique, all of it, forty-seven interactions of technique, and he had reported her and been right about the facts and wrong about everything else. He had known it. He had not felt it until he watched Elena become Julian at the kitchen table, warmly, precisely, lethally, and realised that this was what had sat across a café table from him with milk and cinnamon, that the warmth had been exactly this warmth, manufactured, and that he had never once, at the time, been able to tell. He could not tell now, watching her, and he knew it was happening, and still could not tell. That was the thing. That was what he was learning to do to Julian: to be a warmth that could not be told from the real thing, by a man who would have no reason to look for the seam.

"You pushed your glasses up," she said once, breaking off. "You do that before you check something. He'll see it. Don't check him in front of him. Check him after, alone, where he can't watch you do it."

"How long did it take," Marcus asked her once, "in 2105."

She looked at him for a moment. "Forty-seven," she said. It was the number of recorded interactions. He knew it; it was in Story 01's record, in his own file, the one Kade had read to him. Forty-seven interactions over eight weeks, and at the end of them a man had reported the woman he loved because the facts required it, and had been right about the facts and wrong about everything else.

He was a good student. He hated that he was a good student. His false cover entries, the ones he wrote for the inquiry, the ones that stood in front of the real thing, got easier to write as the fortnight went on, and he noticed them getting easier, and filed the noticing, and went on.

On Saturday the seventh, between the afternoon lessons and the evening ones, he went into the capital, and passed the testimony intake on the ground floor of the Commission building, where Helena Kovač sat.

He stopped in the corridor. He could have gone in. He could have said, Registrar, a question, about your nephew, about when he was given the key. She had followed Julian's career for fifteen years. She would know the year to the month. It would take her a minute to tell him, and it would be a fact, checkable, in a witness's own voice, and it was exactly the kind of thing he had built his whole life on checking.

He did not go in.

He told himself it was delicacy. It was a hard thing to ask a woman about her nephew's guilt, and she had already had the state use her family twice, and it would be a cruelty to make her the witness against the second boy as she had been, unwilling, the thread that led to the first. All of that was true. He stood in the corridor and let it be true, and did not go in, and the reason underneath, which he did not write down, was that the roll already said 2104, and he did not want a witness to say otherwise, because then he would have to decide which to believe, and he had already decided.

The record had already told him. He did not want the witness.

Monday the ninth. The inquiry opened.

The reading rooms of the Chronos Temporal Archive were long and pale, white stone and clear glass, with the Archive's clocks set into the far wall in a descending row, each finer than the last until the last showed nothing a human eye could read. There were nanosecond clocks on the reading desks. Everything in the building kept time to a degree that made Marcus, who had learned to distrust his own memory of an afternoon, feel the way a man feels at the edge of a very deep drop: that there was a great deal here he could fall into.

Aubry opened the session for Chronos, coolly, courteously. "The probability that this inquiry finds what either of us wants," she said, "is low. I would like it on the record that I agreed to it anyway." She said it looking at Marcus, and he could not tell whether it was candour or a warning, and he had begun to think, with Aubry, that it was always both.

Julian sat across the table, gray and formal, and did not look at Marcus all day. Not once. He answered the procedural questions precisely and kept his eyes on the papers, and Marcus understood that the not-looking was its own kind of looking, that the man was holding himself still the way you hold a cup when your hand is not steady, and that he was doing it because Marcus was across the table and he did not trust his own face.

The reading rooms worked Monday to Thursday, which Marcus had asked for without seeming to ask, because it meant Fridays were free and he could cross home, and because a man who sat four days and rested three was a man a tired lonely historian would come to count on, four mornings a week, the same chair, the same hour, which was the whole of the method Elena had given him: the same hour, the same chair. He crossed the lakeshore checkpoint each morning in the fog and was escorted up through the Archive by a protocol officer and logged by every clerk and porter he passed, a known quantity, the Veridican co-chair, nothing hidden, everything on file, which was itself the cover, because a man doing a secret thing does not do it in a building where every step he takes is recorded by the nation he is doing it to. He had learned that from Julian, in a way. A thing done openly in a place that records everything looks least like a thing done in secret. It was the Chronos principle, turned against Chronos, and Marcus used it every morning, and hated that he was good at it, and was good at it.

Friday the thirteenth, the last of the evening lessons.

They had the conversation they had been not having for four years.

It came because Elena brought it. She had taught him for a fortnight how to be the thing a lonely man was missing, and at the end of it, with the back-room door shut and the stove low, she put down the thing she was holding and said, without preamble, "You should ask me the thing you've wanted to ask me since 2105. You'll do the operation better if you're not carrying it. And you're owed it."

So he asked.

The fountain. The blue dress. The café, seventeen forty-three. The milk and cinnamon. The flat with the surveillance blockers. The forty-seven interactions, the hundred and twenty-seven hours. The seven she had turned before him, and himself the eighth.

"Which parts," he said, "were real."

Elena was quiet for a while. Then she said, "Some of it was real."

She did not say which. He waited, and she did not say which, and he understood that she was not going to, and that this was not cruelty but the opposite; that there was a thing she could give him and a thing she could not, and that the thing she could not give him was the only thing he wanted, and that she knew it, and was sorry.

"I can't check that," he said.

"No."

"Give me one thing I can check. One fact. Something the chair took."

She considered it. "The dress," she said. "At the fountain. You lost the colour of it; it's in your file that you lost it. It was blue."

"Blue," said Marcus, and wrote it down, under CHECKED, Witness: Volkov, E., as testimony, not as memory, because it was not his memory any more and never would be, and a thing a witness told you was not the same as a thing you remembered, and he had learned, the hard way, in a white room, to keep the two apart.

At the door she said, "You'll be good at it. That's the part that costs. The ones who are bad at it get to think it was the job's fault. You won't have that."

Monday the sixteenth. The first true working morning.

The delegations had gone home over the weekend; the opening ceremonies were done; the working phase began, co-chairs and clerks, in the reading rooms, Monday to Thursday. Marcus crossed the lakeshore checkpoint at half past seven in the fog and was escorted up through the Archive, logged by every porter and clerk he passed, and came to the long pale reading room at two minutes to eight.

Julian was waiting on the steps.

He was alone, in the gray cardigan, with his hands in his pockets and his breath showing in the early cold. He had not been able to say it on the ninth, across the table, with the delegations there and Aubry beside him. He said it now, to Marcus, on the Archive steps, and his log recorded it, timed to the nanosecond, as it recorded everything, so that it would be there for as long as Chronos stood.

"I hoped it would be you," said Julian.

Marcus stood on the step below him in the fog, and heard it go into the record, permanent, timestamped, a gift given on purpose in the one place it could never be taken back, and he thought: he has done to himself, already, the thing I was sent to do. He has made himself impossible to protect. And he said, "Good morning," because it was the only true thing he had, and went up the steps past him into the Archive to begin.
