Chapter 1: Two Million Journals

Wednesday, August 14, 2109. The Justice Center, and Nikolai and Maya's flat, Veridica Capital

By noon the paper had come in, and Kira Osman counted towns.

She did it on the back of the session agenda, in pencil, in a column down the right-hand margin, while the great hall went on talking around her. A courier from the lakeshore had come through the east doors at 11:52 with a satchel, and the Commission's clerk had brought the first slips along the bench to her, because everyone in the building knew by now whose method it was. Each slip was a keeper's report, written that morning in a kitchen or a church hall or a ferry office and carried down the pairing lines from town to town until somebody with a car had driven it to the capital. Each said the same thing in a different hand.

Our book agrees with itself. It does not agree with the screens.

Kestrel Point. The farming town inland that held Kestrel's pairs. Two towns on the eastern shore. A village whose name she had to read twice. She wrote each one in the column and drew a short line beside it, and did not let herself count anything else. She did not count journals. Nobody could count the journals, and the number the hall had been given at eleven o'clock was not a number she could hold in her head without something in her chest closing up, so she did not hold it. She held towns.

Five.

At the technical bench, Thomas Wei was listening to a commissioner's question with his head a little on one side, the way he listened to everything.

Aug 14, 2109. 11:00. Great hall, Justice Center. Emergency session. Cameras: eleven.

She had counted them first, an hour ago, as she counted every room. The hall had always had nine. The two new ones were over the east doors on bright brackets that had not been there yesterday. Somebody had wanted the doors watched today. She noted it.

Minister Chen Zhao had opened the session with a glass of tea at his elbow and his hands folded, grave and warm. He had said that the Commission would hear the technical account first, and questions after, and that he asked for patience. We will know more tonight than we know now. Then Maya had stood at the technical lectern and given the account, and Kira had watched her give it and heard the fright under every number, because she knew Maya, and Maya was most frightened when she was most exact.

Between twelve minutes past three and twenty-nine minutes past, while the country slept, a little over two million journals registered with the Concordance had been altered. Not erased. Altered, and quietly, so that nothing looked wrong. The changes had come in through the Concordance's own provision for putting a damaged journal back from its sealed master copy. Maya had named the component by its number, 9.4, flatly, and had not looked at the commissioners' bench while she did it.

Kira had not needed her to. She had been seeing the floor of her own flat in January, near midnight, with six handwritten sheets laid out in a row on the boards because the table was too small, and Maya on her knees beside them in her coat, pointing with a pencil at the second sheet. And then sitting back on her heels and saying the sentence Kira had written in the journal afterwards and underlined: Restorable means editable.

They had said it in January. They had said it to the Commission, and to The Judge, and the Concordance had been made voluntary on the strength of it. A great many people had registered anyway, because they were tired and frightened and a master copy in the capital had sounded like safety. And last night someone had used the master.

The samples had gone up on the panel behind the lectern, one at a time, with names blacked out. Kira had read every one.

A debt, never repaid. A quarrel over a boundary. A light seen in a window that should have been dark. A face at a door, at a time, alone. Small things. Plausible things. The kind that would not make anyone gasp. The kind that would make a woman look at her neighbour across a fence for a week before she said anything, and then say the wrong thing. Under each, a row of countersignatures: real names of real neighbours, each with the small seal a registered countersignature carried on a screen, and none of them distinguishable on a screen from a signature given honestly.

Whoever had done it had read the journals first. That was what Kira kept coming back to. You did not write a lie that would turn a street against itself unless you knew the street.

Chronos had come in at noon, on the monitored broadcast relayed into the hall from the Synthesis channel.

A calm Chronos newsreader, a calm Chronos statement, read in the courteous flat way Chronos read everything. The witness books kept by the lakeshore networks on the Chronos side, the books the Administration had begun logging in March in its new witness index, had been altered in the night. The same seventeen minutes. The Administration of Temporal Defence described it as a second Veridican attack on the memory of Chronos.

Those were Julian's books. Julian Kovač's networks: the kitchens he had sat in all spring, countersigning what he had seen on the broadcasts, being felt sorry for. Kira did not look at Marcus when the statement was read. She felt him go still beside her, the stillness of a man who has just heard a name he was waiting for and wishes he had not.

Twelve minutes past three. Twenty-nine minutes past.

She had heard Maya say it at eleven and had not moved, and she did not move now. She knew those minutes. Everyone on the commissioners' bench knew them. They were the minutes in which, twenty months ago, four million people on the other shore of the lake had lost seventeen minutes of their lives from their logs. The Chronos Gap. Thirteenth of December, 2107. To the second.

It was a signature too, of a kind. Somebody wanted it read.

At the lunch adjournment she went up to the technical floor and read the operations log herself, standing at Maya's shoulder, for forty minutes. She was looking for one thing. She had looked for it before, in the Lost Hour, and in Chronos's own published analysis of the Gap, and in the deletion of a protocol she had entered herself on a November night four years ago and watched vanish: a chain of sealed blocks re-closed backwards from its far edge, so neatly that nothing showed unless you knew the hand. Wei's seam. She knew it the way she knew her own handwriting.

It was not there.

Nothing had been cut. Nothing had been re-sealed. Every alteration had come in at the front door, in order, carrying its authority, and been accepted. The master had been told what to say, and had said it, and had then put itself back into two million journals exactly as the Concordance had been built to do.

"It didn't break anything," Kira said.

Maya, typing, did not look up. "No. That's what's wrong with it."

Wei answered questions for an hour in the afternoon session.

He was calm and he was helpful. He was, very visibly, appalled. Kira had been watching him for two and a half years, on and off, the way you watch weather from a window you cannot leave, and she had never once seen him look appalled. She watched him do it now and could not find the seam in that either.

He took it on his department. He said so first, before anyone asked, standing at the technical lectern with his tablet in his right hand and his left resting easy on the wood, the thin pale line across its back faded almost to nothing. "The Concordance is my office's design. Whatever failed, failed under me. I'd like that on the record before anything else is." He said that the Commission would have a full account within the week. He said that every keeper whose journal had been altered would be written to, by name, and told how to compare it with their stamped paper. He said that Ms. Reeves had found it first.

"She was on the floor at a quarter to four," he said. "Before anyone else. She found it, and she called me, and she called the commissioners. I'd like that on the record too."

At the end of the commissioners' bench, a member from the eastern districts asked the question everybody in the hall had come to hear asked. How could a provision built to answer to one authority have answered to another?

Wei considered it. He was a man who considered questions; it was one of the things people liked about him.

"The path did what it was built to do," he said. "It restored what it was given."

Kira wrote the sentence down. She wrote it exactly, on the back of the agenda, under her column of towns, because it was the kind of sentence she had learned to keep. The first time she read it back it sounded like candour: a technician refusing to blame his machine. The second time it sounded like something else, and she could not have said what, and that was worse.

"And the authority it answered to?" said the commissioner.

"I don't know yet," said Wei. He said it simply, and looked straight at the bench. "When I do, you will."

He sat down. The hall let out a breath.

Undersecretary Iver Pell did not sit on the commissioners' bench. He sat on the Ministry bench across the aisle, in a good dark coat, and he had not spoken all day. He spoke now, rising as the Minister reached for his glass to close the session, and the Minister let him, because Pell was a member of the government and the cameras were on.

"I only want to say what everyone in this room is thinking," said Pell pleasantly. "In January this Commission told the country that unregistered journals were safe because they were on paper, and that registered journals were safe because they were sealed. The country believed the second half." He spread his hands. "The League of the Record has said one thing since December, to every town that would listen. I take no pleasure in being right. The journals were never safe."

He sat down. Nobody answered him. Kira watched the eleven cameras take it, and thought that by evening it would be on every screen in the capital, and her column of towns would not.

Seven, she wrote in the margin. A courier had come in during Wei's answers.

Helena Kovač was in the east corridor afterwards, under the tall windows, where the light lay across the floor in long bright panels and the air smelled of hot stone.

She was standing by a pillar with her hands in the pockets of her dark coat, which she wore in August as she wore it in February, as though the season were someone else's business. She was not waiting for anyone. Kira had learned that this was what Kovač looked like when she was waiting for someone.

"Ms. Osman."

"Registrar."

Kovač looked past her, down the corridor, at the commissioners coming out in twos and threes. Marcus was among them, slowly, with a clerk at his elbow and his glasses pushed up and his face closed.

"Julian's books too?" said Kovač.

"Yes."

Kovač pushed her glasses up her nose. She looked at the floor, at a panel of light, as though it were a document. Then she nodded once, and said nothing more, and turned and walked back towards the intake stairs with her hands still in her pockets.

Kira stood in the corridor and watched her go, and thought that she had never before heard the Registrar say her nephew's first name aloud.

Aug 14, 2109. 23:10. Nikolai and Maya's flat. Present: Volkov, N.; Reeves, M.; Chen, M.; Osman, K. Purpose: not stated. Purpose: obvious.

The kitchen table was the work table. It always had been. A lamp clipped to the shelf above it, a radiator under the window that was off for the summer and knocked anyway, three screens, and between them, squared to the table's edge, a gray card folder with NOT OURS on the front in Nikolai's small careful capitals.

Maya sat on the counter with her knees drawn up and a tablet on them, timestamping under her breath, a technician's rosary. 23:11:40. Opened. 23:12:05. Laid beside. She had not been home since five that morning.

Marcus was standing. He had been standing since he came in, by the dark window, with his coat still on, and Kira had seen Nikolai glance at him twice and decide not to ask him to sit.

Nikolai had his hands flat on either side of the keyboard.

"I want to say it in order," he said. "I said this once before, in February, in Marcus's kitchen, and I want it to be the same as it was then. Nothing more."

Kira sat down across from him.

He laid it out quietly, looking at his hands. Every alteration in the night had carried its countersignature, and they were all alike. A countersignature, he reminded them, was not a name. It was a set of habits, the small particular ways a key did its work, as individual as the slope of a hand. In February he had built a portrait of one key's habits from years of the Chronos Archive's public notices, and from a single printed slip that a man had once handed across a garden bench to show there was nothing behind it. Saltmarsh Crossing, garden, 13 Dec 2108. The slip was in the back pocket of the gray folder. It had been there since Christmas.

He put the night's countersignature on the left screen and the February portrait on the right.

He did not say anything for a while. He did not need to. Kira was not a reader of countersignatures, not the way he was, but she was a reader of patterns, and she could see it without being told: the same small habits, in the same order, every time.

"The senior reconciliation key," said Nikolai, "of the Chronos Temporal Archive."

Maya stopped timestamping.

"The same key that countersigned the Gap," said Nikolai. He did not look up. "I want to say this clearly, because I said it in February and I want it said the same way now. I know the key. I don't know the hand."

Marcus took his lists out of his inside pocket.

Kira watched him do it. He unfolded them on the windowsill, CHECKED and UNCHECKED and HOPES, the paper soft at the folds, and took the pencil from behind his ear and held it over the middle column, where she knew without looking what stood at the bottom. A date in February. A seventeen-minute interval. Two hands. A name, and after it, a question mark.

He held the pencil there for a long time. He did not write anything. He folded the lists again and put them back in his coat, and stood with his hand flat over the pocket, the way Ruth Calloway stood with her hand over a book.

Nobody spoke. The radiator knocked, though it was August.

Kira looked at the two screens, and at the column of towns on the agenda in front of her, eleven now, eleven towns whose paper had held, and found that the thing she could not stop seeing was not the key at all. It was the room it had been used in.

The Chronos Temporal Archive was on the far side of a lake. It held the backups of a nation that recorded every breath its people took. Its key belonged to that nation and opened that nation's past. And last night, at twelve minutes past three, it had walked into a store in the capital of Veridica, three floors below where they had all sat that morning, and been let in as if it lived there.

"How does a Chronos key," said Kira, "reach a Veridican master?"

Nobody answered her. Maya looked at Nikolai, and Nikolai looked at his hands, and Marcus looked at the window, where the lights of the capital went down towards the river and stopped, and beyond them there was only dark, and somewhere in the dark a lake, and another shore.

She wrote the question under the towns, and underlined it once, and did not know where it went.
