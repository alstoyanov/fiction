Chapter 15: The Key

Friday, October 18 – Thursday, October 24, 2109. The Chronos Temporal Archive and Julian's flat, the Chronos lakeshore city; the flat by the old river, Veridica Capital

October eighteenth, 2109. Sixteen-forty. Chronos Temporal Archive, reconciliation rooms, lower floor. Involved parties: Chen, M.; Kovač, J. Event: access.

Everything Elena had taught him worked.

He had been afraid it would not. He had sat at her table for a fortnight and learned to be the thing a lonely man was missing, and some part of him had hoped he would be bad at it, so that he could tell himself afterwards that it had not really been him, that the method had failed and the outcome had been Julian's own doing. He was not bad at it. He was good at it. He had been a lonely man himself, once, at a desk on the fourteenth floor, with colleagues and few friends and nobody to tell the day's facts to, and he knew from the inside exactly what the inside of this felt like, which was the thing that made him good at it, which was the part that cost, as Elena had said.

On the Friday, Julian took him down to the reconciliation rooms.

He did it unasked. That was the whole of it; Marcus had not asked. They had been working in the reading rooms all week on the key's authority history, and on the Friday afternoon Julian put down his pen and said, "You should see the rooms themselves. Where the key actually works. So you can see its history for yourself, and not have to take it through me." And he took Marcus down to the lower floor, to a long cold clean room with a reconciliation desk and a chipped brown cup on the shelf above it that Julian did not use and did not move, and he enrolled Marcus's co-chair access on the doors and the terminals, and signed the grant in the Archive's access ledger, in his own name.

"So you can see there's nothing behind it," said Julian. "Not through me. For yourself."

A man offers what he has never been asked for. Marcus heard Elena's voice say it, flat, at the kitchen table, and felt the thing she had described: the success and the nausea arriving together, in the same beat, indistinguishable, because they were the same thing. Julian had just handed him the door. Julian had signed, in his own hand, in the Archive's ledger, the grant that would, in a week, be the first thing Temporal Defence found when it asked how the Veridicans had got in.

Marcus said, "Thank you," and meant it in a way he could not have explained, and hated himself, and let Julian see none of it, because he was good at it now.

That night he told Kira, in one line: He gave me the access. Unasked. She wrote it into the refusal branch of the image without a word, because the access changed a technical thing, and because she was holding the knife and not the man and would not ask him how the access had felt.

The evenings, Saturday to Wednesday, at Julian's flat.

Julian cooked badly and cheerfully. He had a small flat with its desk turned side-on to the window, so that he never faced the lake, and he burned the first thing he tried to cook every evening and apologised for it every evening and they ate it anyway. Everything Marcus said in that flat went into Julian's log, so Marcus said almost nothing that was false. The lie was not in any sentence. The lie was the whole of it, the fact of a friend at the table, and it did not need any single false word to hold it up, which was the thing Elena had promised and the thing that turned out to be true.

The flat itself was a thing Marcus made himself look at, because looking at it was part of what he was doing and he would not let himself do the thing without looking at it. Two rooms and a narrow kitchen, the desk turned side-on to the window so that Julian never faced the lake, the bed on the inner wall; a man had arranged his whole life, fifteen years ago, so as not to look east, and had never said why, and Marcus knew why, because Marcus had read the file that said Julian had left Veridica at nineteen and never gone back, and a man who turns his furniture from a view turns it from a thing he cannot have. There were lists everywhere, in Julian's hand, on the walls, on the shelves, in a red-covered book on the desk that Marcus did not touch. A man who kept lists. Marcus kept lists. He had thought, more than once, in that flat, that he was looking at the life he might have had if the white chair had not taken the first half of his, a lonely precise man in two rooms with his records and no one to tell them to, and that the thought was exactly the kind of thought Elena had taught him to have, to be the thing a man was missing, and that having it honestly and having it as technique were now the same thing in him, which was what she had warned him of, which was the part that cost.

On the Monday Julian read him a page from the handwritten journal he had started in December.

"A network keeper," he said. "On the eastern shore. Her book was altered in the Rewriting, like all of them, and she sat down and rebuilt it, by hand, from memory and from her neighbours, over a week. I wrote down what she told me." He read it, in his sloping careful hand, a plain account of a woman at a kitchen table putting her street back together one countersignature at a time, and his voice was not steady by the end of it, and he did not pretend it was.

Then he turned the page quickly, too quickly, and Marcus saw, in the half-second before it turned, a date and a single initial at the head of the next entry. M. He did not see more. He did not ask. He filed it, under UNCHECKED, J's journal, an entry headed M., turned past, and did not know, and would not know for weeks, that it was the entry in which Julian had tried to write, and failed, the thing he felt about the man across the table.

On the Tuesday Julian told him about Adrian's visit, happily. The clock. Their father. "He asked about you," said Julian, and watched Marcus with the open look of a man sharing good news, and Marcus answered lightly, and thought of a lamp on Historian's Lane that he did not know about, and of a brother's odd look that Julian had scrolled back to and could not read, and said nothing.

They talked about the garden at Saltmarsh Crossing, the bench under the pear tree, which Julian remembered with a kind of helpless fondness, as the place where the worst and the best thing of his year had happened on the same morning.

"I'd like to sit there again," said Julian. "When this is over. In the leaf, properly, not in the fog. I never saw it in the leaf."

"Yes," said Marcus, and let it mean something, and it was the worst thing he did all week, worse than the access, because the access was a thing Julian had done to himself and this was a thing Marcus did to Julian: he let a man hope for a bench in the summer with a friend, knowing there would be no friend, knowing it while he said yes, letting the yes carry the whole weight Julian wanted it to carry.

Elena had told him about this, at the farmhouse table, in the last lesson. The hope is the cruellest part, and it is the part you cannot avoid, because the whole of what you are doing is making a man hope. You will want, near the end, to stop letting him hope, to go cold, to spare him. Don't. Going cold at the end is for your sake, not his; it tells him something is wrong, and then he spends the rest of his life knowing he was warned and did not listen, which is a second wound. If you are going to do it, let him hope to the last, and carry the knowing yourself. That is the only mercy left in it, and it is not much of one, and it is the one that costs you most. He had written none of it down. He did not need to. He heard it, on the bench that was only a word he had lost, every time Julian spoke of the summer.

Let him see you almost say something, and then don't. This time it was Julian who almost said something, over a file, on the Wednesday, when their hands brushed and neither of them moved, and Marcus who let him, and did not help him, and did not stop him, which was its own kind of cruelty, the kind with no fingerprints on it.

By day the inquiry was procedural. The co-chairs' draft report was due on Friday the twenty-fifth. Aubry sent a note of thanks, cool and correct, "for a very orderly autumn," which Marcus read three times, looking for the second meaning, and could not be sure there was one, which was how it was with Aubry.

Wednesday night, at the flat's door, twenty-two forty, Julian said, "Marcus, I—"

And stopped. And looked at him, in the cold, in the dark of the landing, with the fog coming in off the lake behind the building, and then said, "Drive carefully. The fog's in."

Marcus let the pause stand. He put his hand on Julian's arm, for a moment, and then took it away, and said good night, and went down to the car and across the checkpoint at eleven, through the floodlights and the fog and the accreditation check, and on the Veridican side he sat in the car at the edge of the barrier for ten minutes before he could drive, with his hands on the wheel and the engine off, doing nothing, before he could make himself go home.

Thursday the twenty-fourth. Kira finished the image.

They went through it at the kitchen table, the two of them, in the evening, courteous with each other, like colleagues. The scope list: two record sets, one revocation. The hard stop. The hour: 02:40, not 03:12. The refusal branch, for the moment Chronos's own backup would be offered a foreign credential and should, honestly, say no.

"If it refuses," said Kira, "I meet the refusal and we stop, and the records on both sides are half written, and we've made it worse, and that's the risk, and you need to have agreed to it before we go."

"I agree to it," said Marcus.

"If it doesn't refuse—" said Kira, and stopped, and he saw her decide not to finish the sentence, the way she decided not to finish sentences now. "If it doesn't refuse, it goes narrow, and it does the two things, and it stops at the hard stop. That's the plan. That's all the plan is."

"Yes," said Marcus.

She looked at him across the table. "You've agreed to everything I've said tonight without arguing one word of it," she said. "You did that last time too. It frightens me more than if you fought me."

"I know," said Marcus. "I'm sorry."

He was. It was true. It did not change anything, because the thing was decided, and the terms were only the shape of their consent to it, and they both knew it, and they went through the terms anyway because the terms were the only part either of them could hold.

Late, alone, after Kira had gone to bed, Marcus opened his lists.

He went to CHECKED, where he kept the things he could verify, and he wrote the one entry from the whole week that he could stand behind, the only fact he had that was not folded in cover or hope or the smooth place where a word should be.

He wrote it in his small historian's hand, in the format of desk 14-08.

Oct 24, 2109. Chronos Temporal Archive. Involved parties: Chen, M.; Kovač, J. Event: I used him. Verification: me.

He read it over.

It was the truest entry he had written in two years. There was no cover in it, no second meaning, no missing word. He had used him. He knew he had used him. The verification was himself, the only witness there could be to a thing that happened entirely inside the man who did it, and the entry stood under CHECKED because it was checked, by the one person who could check it, who would never be able to stop checking it, who had just written it down so that it would be in his own record, in his own hand, forever, the way Julian wrote things down, the way the whole series of them had learned to write things down, so that they happened, so that they could not be taken.

He thought, holding the pencil, of the first time he had understood what a fact entry was for. It had been in a white room, at the floor of himself, when the only way he had found to stay a person was to hold the entries of his own life in his head in the dull format of his old job, traffic incident, corner of Fifth and Main, fourteen-oh-two, because the format was too boring for the machine to want and so it survived. He had kept himself alive by making facts of himself. He had learned, afterwards, to make facts of the world, so that the world could not be taken from him either. And now he had made a fact of the worst thing he had ever done, in the same hand, in the same format, under the same heading, and he understood that the habit that had saved him was the habit that now condemned him, exactly, in his own writing, and that there was no version of himself that did not write it down, because writing it down was the whole of what he was, and a man cannot keep the parts of himself that saved him and refuse the ones that damn him; they are the same parts; that was the thing the year had taught him, over and over, and here it was again, in pencil, under CHECKED.

He had made it a fact. That was all he knew how to do with a thing. He had made it a fact, and filed it, and turned off the lamp, and sat in the dark at the table for a while with his hand flat on the folded lists, over the place where the entry was, the way Ruth Calloway sat with her hand over a book, the way he had seen himself do it, as if the thing inside could be kept from getting out by the weight of a hand.
