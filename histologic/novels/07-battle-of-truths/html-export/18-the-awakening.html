Chapter 18: The Awakening

Saturday, October 26 – Monday, October 28, 2109. The teaching hospital, the Chronos lakeshore city; Tanaka's flat

The handover on the storm ward began at half past seven on Saturday, and the night registrar read back his own casualty report from Friday afternoon, and it was wrong.

He was a careful young man, careful in the way the good ones were careful, and he read the report in his own hand off his own tablet, and it said what Veridica had always said about the lakeshore: that the far shore was Veridican, that the Temporal War had been Chronos's aggression, that the storms had come from Chronos's own broadcasts flung back across the overlap. He read it, and he paused, and he frowned at it the way the man at the tram stop had frowned, a flick of attention to the corner of the sight, and then he said, "I must have been tired when I wrote this up. I wrote it right, though." And he went on to the next patient.

Yuki Tanaka set her cup down on the nurses' station, very carefully, with both hands.

I wrote it right. He meant it. There was no distress in him at all. He had consulted his log, which told him that the thing he half remembered writing was not the thing his log said he had written, and he had concluded, instantly and without pain, that his memory was the thing at fault, because in Chronos the memory was always the thing at fault, the log was the self, and a man who found a difference between them did not doubt the log. She watched him do it and understood that she was watching the thing happen, the thing that had a name she did not yet have, and that it was happening to everyone in the building except her and one other man on the fourth floor, and that it did not look like damage. It looked like recovery.

The morning went on, and the ward desk talked about the lakeshore the way people talk about the weather.

It had always been Veridica's. Everyone knew that. The Temporal War had been Chronos's own fault, a war Chronos had started over its own grievance, which had cost both shores so much, and wasn't it a mercy it was over. They said it without heat, without shame, the way you say a settled thing. A nurse of about fifty, pouring tea at the station, said a sentence that Tanaka had learned, word for word, from a Veridican school primer forty years before, in a classroom in a country this woman had been taught all her life to think of as the enemy, and the nurse said it as a thing she had always known, and poured the tea, and did not see Tanaka go still.

The broadcast carried the detained key-holder's name through the morning, between determinations. Kovač, J. And the Judge's daily reading of the collective will reported the record concordant on the lakeshore for the first time anyone could remember, which was the Judge's way of saying that a nation of a hundred and fifty million now agreed with itself, and with Veridica, about a past it had not had on Friday.

Tanaka did not need to be told the plan to know what had happened. She had not been told the plan. She had known there was a plan; she had not known it could do this. But the Joint Inquiry had been sitting in the Archive for six weeks; a Veridican commissioner had held co-chair access to the reconciliation rooms; Julian Kovač was arrested; and the new past read like a Veridican textbook, like the primer in the nurse's mouth. She did the sum once, standing at the station with her cold tea, and did not finish it, because finishing it meant naming the people who had done it, and she knew their names, and three of them had held her head in a car and read the primer over her shoulder and taught her to fake her way through a white chair.

Morrison came in at noon and read the morning bulletin over her shoulder, the way he had read the stabilisation circular over her shoulder in the winter.

He was Veridican-born. His interface, like hers, received and did not record; he remembered what he remembered, and nothing had overwritten it, because there was nothing in him to overwrite. He read the bulletin, and the line about the lakeshore, and he went gray, the way he had gone gray at the fence, and he said, "Sorry," to nobody, to the room, to the bulletin, and went back to his desk and did not say anything else about it all weekend. His hands were steady. She checked. They had been steady for two years and they were steady now, which was somehow the worst thing, that a man could watch a country be rewritten around him and keep his hands steady, because the steadiness had cost him so much to earn that he could not spend it even on this.

Dr. Vale was on the ninth floor, and he agreed with the new record as calmly as everyone else.

She went up at the end of her round and found him at the window at the end of the corridor, and he said, "A sad business at the Archive," and meant the detention, and spoke of the lakeshore, when she led him to it, as a Chronos man now spoke of the lakeshore: as Veridican, as it had always been, a thing settled and old. He said it in the stooped, courteous, beautifully mannered way he said everything, and there was no seam in it, no flicker, no frown even, because Vale was a Chronos citizen and his interface recorded and had been rewritten, and his memory, where it disagreed, had yielded, the way everyone's had.

That frightened her more than the nurse or the registrar. Vale had stood surety for her. Vale had blocked two orders from the Administration and certified a dead woman who was not dead and sent her across a lake in a mortuary van. Vale had done all of that out of some conviction she had never asked him to name, and now the conviction's foundation, the history he had done it inside of, had been changed under him in the night, and he did not know it, and she could not tell him, because to tell him was to tell him everything, and she did not have the right to put that in a man's head against his will, having spent her whole life learning what it cost to put things in people's heads against their will.

It was the thing nobody warned you of about the Awakening, she thought, going down in the lift: that it took the good people exactly as it took the rest. She had half expected, without ever framing it, that a man like Vale, who had risked everything on a private conviction, would prove somehow proof against it, that conviction would armour a person. It did not. Conviction was a thing a person held; the Awakening did not argue with what you held; it changed what you remembered holding it about, so that the conviction stood, intact, pointing at a new history, and the man went on being brave and principled and kind in the service of a past that had been handed to him in the dark. There was no armour. The thing reached everyone who had a log, and a log was the price of being a citizen, and the only people it had missed were the ones who had already paid a worse price to be outside it: the never-logged, the paper-keepers, the officially dead. You could only escape the rewriting by having already lost the thing the rewriting worked through, which was a terrible kind of freedom, and the only kind on offer.

She did not ask him about it. She said good evening, and went down.

Saturday evening, at her kitchen table, she wrote the case note she had been circling all day.

Onset: between 02:40 and 04:10, per the Archive's own bulletin. No sensory event reported. No distress. No storm. Patients consult their records and report agreement. Memory, where it disagrees with the record, is discarded as error, without pain. Presentation resembles recovery.

She stopped there. She looked at the last line for a long time. Presentation resembles recovery. It was the most frightening sentence she had written in her life, and it was clinically exact, and she had learned, over forty years, that the most clinically exact sentences were the ones she least wanted to write, and she wrote them anyway, because they were true, and because someone ought to.

On Sunday she went into Ward 9S.

The sealed ward ran on paper and recorded nothing, and so its patients, whose lives had never been logged in here, had nothing to consult but their own memories, and their own memories had not been rewritten, because there was nothing of them in the true source to rewrite. The day-room screen carried the new history, calm, Veridican, and the long-term patients of 9S argued with it.

An old man, listed on the board as B.N., stood in front of the screen and told it it was wrong. When he was a boy, he said, the ferry to the other shore had meant going abroad; you took your papers; the lakeshore towns on the far side were Chronos towns, everyone knew it, his aunt had lived in one. The screen said otherwise, calmly, and he told it it was wrong again, with the particular stubborn clarity of a man whose only evidence was his own life and who had never in his life been asked to set it against a log. A woman two beds down laughed at the screen, short and dry, and reached up and turned it off, and said the people on it had lost their heads.

Tanaka stood in the doorway and understood that she was looking at the only Chronos memory left on this shore of the lake: twelve people on a sealed ward, and six keepers in six lakeshore kitchens with paper books, and nobody else. A hundred and fifty million people had woken up agreeing, and these twelve, and the six, remembered otherwise, because the thing that had rewritten everyone had not been able to reach the places that had never been written down.

She took out the twelfth notebook, the one she had started in 9S in the spring, and she began to take testimony.

She did it the way she did everything, in order, exactly. She wrote each entry dated, timed, placed, in the patient's own words, and she had each patient sign or initial it, the way the Kestrel keepers signed, because a thing a person said was worth more if the person put their name to having said it. And she made, in the first entry, the distinction her whole voice was built on, because it mattered more here than it had ever mattered:

I am not recording what happened. I am recording what they remember. That is not quite the same thing.

What the 9S patients remembered was Chronos's old history, which had itself been partly broadcast into them over a lifetime, which was not the same as the truth either, because there had perhaps never been a clean truth about the lakeshore for anyone on either shore. She did not try to resolve which version was true. It was not her business to say which was true. It was her business to write down that there had been two, and that these twelve people still held the one the whole of the rest of the country had lost in a night, and that the holding of it was the only evidence that there had ever been two.

It was still more than anyone outside this room had. It was, she thought, writing it down, perhaps the only thing outside Veridica's own records that disagreed with Veridica at all, now, anywhere on this shore. Twelve old people and a notebook that recorded nothing because it was on a sealed ward, and six kitchens, and a mortuary van's worth of the officially dead.

Monday afternoon, a woman came to the hospital asking for the Veridican doctor.

She was from a Chronos-shore town, middle-aged, steady, with a paper book in her coat. Tanaka knew her, or knew of her: she was one of the six keepers from the Night of Copies, the one who had said Wait, I'm writing to the directorate officers in January and kept writing. Her log said one history now. The book in her own hand, countersigned by her neighbours, said another: the old lakeshore, the Temporal War as Chronos had told it, the Gap as a Veridican attack. Her paper had not changed, because paper was not in the true source, because paper was the one thing the master could not reach.

She believed the book.

She asked Tanaka, quite steadily, sitting in the chair by the station with the book on her knee, whether she was ill. Whether a person who found that their own hand, countersigned by their neighbours, said a different thing from their log, and who believed the hand, was a person whose mind was failing.

"No," said Tanaka.

The keeper nodded, as if she had expected that answer and needed it anyway. She said she had spoken to the other five, by line, carefully, in the Chronos way that assumed everything was overheard. All six of them had books that disagreed with their logs. All six of them had kept their books. All six of them believed the books, and were afraid, and were not going to stop keeping them.

"We thought we were mad," said the keeper. "All six of us thought we were the only one. And then we weren't." She looked at Tanaka. "That's the difference, isn't it. Being the only one, and not being."

"Yes," said Tanaka. "That's the difference."

Late on Monday, alone at her fourth-floor desk, with Morrison gone home and the ward quiet, she wrote the case note she had been avoiding since Saturday morning.

She wrote it the way she wrote all of them, in italics, in her small precise hand, because the form was the only thing that let her say the size of the thing without being destroyed by it.

Population: approx. 150 million. Presentation: acquired certainty. Onset: overnight. No sensory event. No distress. Memory, where it conflicts with the record, discarded without pain. Prognosis: the certainty is self-sustaining, since each citizen's evidence for it is the same record that was changed, and the record now agrees with itself. The only contrary evidence is held by persons whose lives were never recorded, and by persons holding paper. Aetiology:

She stopped, with the pen on the page, and looked at the word, and at the lake through the window, which was very clear that evening, all the way across to the far shore, the Veridican shore, which had always, her log would now tell her if she had a log, been Veridican.

She finished the line.

Aetiology: my friends.
