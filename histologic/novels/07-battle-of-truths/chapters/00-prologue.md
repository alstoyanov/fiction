# Prologue: The Rewriting

*Tuesday, August 13 – Wednesday, August 14, 2109. Kestrel Point, the claimed strip*

---

The summer timetable was in the middle of the ferry-office window, where it belonged in August, and Ruth Calloway did not have to go back for it.

*07:40. 12:10. 17:40. 21:40.*

She had sold forty-one tickets that day and written them in the ledger, and the till had balanced to the coin, and the last boat had gone out at twenty to ten into a lake so flat and dark it looked poured. It was the hottest night of the summer. The heat had sat on Harbour Street since noon and had not moved, and now, at ten, it lay along the stones like something that had been put down and forgotten. Nobody had their doors shut. On the steps of the houses people sat in their shirtsleeves with their feet on the cobbles, not talking much, the way you don't on nights like that.

At the end of the street the quay ran out and stopped, and past the end of it there was water, and that was all. A year ago half the town would have argued about that. Nobody argued now. There had been no broadcasts across the lake since December to make them, and the things that had been put into their heads before December had settled, the way silt settles, so that you could see through to the bottom. Ruth still knew both piers. She expected to know both until she died. But when she walked to the end of the street, as she did most nights before she went up, she saw water, and so did everyone else, and they said so, and it was enough.

She locked the ferry office, and tried the handle, and went along to the chandlery.

---

The reading room had been rebuilt over the spring inside the chandlery's shell.

The walls were the old walls. Nobody had been able to afford new ones, and the stone had come through the fire black but sound, so the men from the boatyard had scraped what they could and painted the rest, three coats, white, and you could not see the char at all unless you knew where to look. On hot days you could smell it. It came out of the stone the way damp comes out of a cellar, faint and sharp, and anyone who had been on Harbour Street on Christmas Eve noticed it without wanting to and said nothing. There were new pine shelves along the back wall, still pale, still sticky where the knots had wept, and a long table that the schoolmaster had found in a school that was closing down the shore. The window had been reglazed. The door was new oak, with two locks.

Ruth went in to check the shelves, because she always checked them.

The Kestrel Book was not on them. The Kestrel Book was in her coat, against her ribs, as it had been every night since the twenty-fourth of December and most nights before that. It had forty-three contributors now. It lived on the long table by day, open, with a pencil on a string, and anyone could come in and read it and anyone who had seen a thing could write it, and at six the keeper took it home. That was the rule. It had not been the rule before Christmas Eve. It was now, and nobody had had to vote on it.

On the shelf, where it always stayed, was the other one.

It was thinner, and plainer, and it had a strip of paper glued down its spine with *PAIRS* written on it in the schoolmaster's capitals. It was the thing the pairing method made: hand copies of other towns' books, written into a Kestrel volume in Kestrel hands, so that if anyone burned one book, or spoiled it, the same days would still stand somewhere else, in other handwriting, checked by other people. Kestrel Point copied two towns, and two towns copied Kestrel Point. It was the most tedious work the book had ever asked of anyone, and in February eleven people had sat at this table in their coats for three evenings, before the stove was mended, and done it.

Ruth took it down and turned the pages without reading them, the way you touch a sleeping child's back to feel it breathe.

The first half was a farming town inland. The second half was Net Loft Row.

That was a street in Gullhaven, eleven kilometres up the shore: one row of tall narrow houses along the harbour, where the fishing families had dried their nets in the lofts for two hundred years. The Row had kept a street book since the May before last, one book for the whole street, which was unusual, and kept it well. In February two of its people had come down on the 12:10 with the book in a satchel and sat at this table with the Kestrel copyists for two days, reading every line aloud while it was copied, and checking the copy afterwards, line by line, with a ruler under it. Ruth had made the tea. The Row's book had gone home again on the 17:40 on the second evening, and its keeper had carried it down the gangway himself.

She put the pairs back on its shelf, and locked the door with both locks, and went up the hill.

---

She could not sleep.

It was the heat. She lay on top of the sheet with the window wide and the curtains not moving and listened to the town not sleeping either: a baby somewhere along the terrace, a man coughing, someone's radio turned low, then off. Wren's door was shut. Wren was fifteen now and slept through anything, and had told her mother so, at length, as though it were an achievement.

Ruth thought about nothing in particular. The coal order. The schoolmaster's knee. The Concordance terminal in the corner of the ferry office, which had been there since April, gray and new, with its crest on the casing, the open book under two joined hands. A Commission outreach woman had come down on the 12:10 to install it and stayed for the 17:40, sitting by it in a good blouse with a stack of leaflets, explaining to anyone who would stand still that registration was voluntary, entirely voluntary, but that a registered journal was sealed in a master copy in the capital and could never be lost to a fire again. She had said *fire* quite gently. She had not looked at the chandlery. She had not needed to.

About a third of Harbour Street had registered by the end of May. Ruth did not blame them. You could not stand on the slip on Christmas night and watch the black water take the last of the ash out of the harbour and blame anybody for wanting a copy kept somewhere else.

She had not registered. The Kestrel Book had not registered. Nobody had asked it to, and it had not offered.

She must have slept, because the hammering woke her.

---

It was 03:14 by the clock on the chest of drawers. The numbers were green and very clear. She was on her feet and halfway down the stairs before she knew she had decided to be, and the hammering went on, not loud, but not stopping either: the flat of a hand on wood, over and over, the way you knock when you have already knocked and nobody came.

She opened the door.

It was her neighbour from number 14, in her nightdress, with a cardigan pulled round her shoulders in spite of the heat. She was a widow of about seventy, small and neat, who had lived on Harbour Street longer than Ruth had been alive and had never once in Ruth's hearing raised her voice. Her face was the colour of paper.

"Ruth," she said. "I'm sorry. I'm sorry. It's the middle of the night. I didn't know who else."

"Come in."

"No. You come. Please. You have to see it. I can't—" She had hold of the door frame with one hand. "It's my name on it. It's my name on it, Ruth, and I'd never."

Ruth put her coat on over her nightdress. She did it without thinking, the way she did it every time she went out of the house, and she felt the book settle against her ribs, and was glad of it without knowing why yet. She did not wake Wren.

---

The kitchen at number 14 was very clean. It always was. There was a cloth on the table, ironed, and a vase with three marigolds in it, and on the end of the table nearest the window a terminal of the kind the Commission had been selling at cost since April, propped on its little stand, with its screen lit.

"I couldn't sleep," said the widow. "The heat. I came down at three for a glass of water, and I thought I'd read a bit. I read back, you know. In the night. I like to see what I wrote." She sat down in the chair in front of the screen, and then stood up again at once, as if it had been hot. "I was reading March. Last March. And it went—" She moved her hand across the air in front of the screen, from top to bottom, like a blind coming down. "It went like that. It drew itself again. As if somebody somewhere had pressed something."

"What time?"

"Twelve minutes past. I looked at the clock. It's what you do, isn't it, now. I looked at the clock and it said twelve minutes past three, and then I scrolled back up to what I'd been reading, and it was different."

Ruth sat down in front of it.

She had seen the widow's journal before. Everyone on Harbour Street had seen everyone's, by now, because people read each other's at the reading room and in each other's kitchens, and argued about them, and that was the point. The widow had kept a paper book from the spring of 2108, after the first storms. In April she had brought it down to the ferry office, all thirty-odd pages of it, and the outreach woman had scanned it in for her, page by page, very kind, and the widow had watched her own handwriting go into the gray box and come up on the screen as type. Since then she had written at home, at this terminal, in the evenings. She liked it. She said it was neater than her hand.

Ruth read.

It took her a while to see it. That was the first thing she would remember afterwards, when she tried to tell it in order: how long it took. Nothing on the screen looked wrong. It looked like a journal. Dates down the left, the time beside each, a few lines of plain writing, and under each entry the countersignatures, the names of neighbours who had seen the same thing and said so, each with its small seal, the way registered countersignatures looked on a screen. It looked like every page Ruth had ever read in this woman's hand, typed.

*Tue 3 Jun 2108. 19:30. Back fence. Words with No. 16 about the fence again. He says the post is on his side. It is not on his side. He was very rude. Countersigned:* and two names from the terrace.

"That's not right," said the widow, over her shoulder. "I never had words with him. Not about the fence. I've never had words with him in my life. He mended the fence. He came round with a hammer in June and mended it and wouldn't take anything for it."

Ruth scrolled.

*Fri 18 Jul 2108.* A loan. Thirty to the woman at number 9, *for the electric. Not repaid. Asked twice.* Countersigned.

"She never asked me. I never lent her a thing. She's never asked anyone for anything in her life, she'd die first."

Ruth scrolled.

There were others. Small things. A dog let off its lead on purpose. A remark about somebody's son. A light seen on in a house that should have been empty. None of them was much. None of them was the kind of thing you would notice if it was in your own journal and you were tired. They were the kind of thing you would notice if it was about you, and you read it in someone else's, with their name on the front and your neighbour's name under it.

And then:

*Mon 24 Dec 2108. 22:05. Harbour Street. Saw E. Varley at the chandlery door, 22:05, alone.*

Countersigned. Two names. Ruth knew both. One of them was the engineer's wife.

She sat very still in front of the screen.

"I never wrote that," said the widow. Her voice had gone thin and high. "I was in bed by ten on Christmas Eve. I was in bed. I heard the—I heard it, after. I didn't see anybody. I'd never write that about Emmet. I'd never write anything like that about anybody. It's my *name* on it."

"I know."

"Look at the names under it. Look. They'd never have signed that. They never signed anything of mine. I've never even shown her—" She stopped. She put her hand to her mouth. "Ruth, what is it? What's happened to it?"

Ruth did not know. She said so.

---

The widow had kept her paper book. That was the one thing. She had kept it after the scanning, in the drawer of the dresser, under the good tablecloth, because it was her handwriting and she could not throw her own handwriting away. She brought it now, with her hands shaking, and put it on the table under the lamp, and Ruth opened it at the back, where the writing stopped in April.

The paper stopped in April. Christmas Eve was in the paper. It was the last long entry before the scanning.

*24 December. Bed at ten. Woke about quarter past to the bells and shouting on Harbour Street. Saw the glow from my window. Did not go down; my legs. Heard after that it was the reading room. Lord help us all.*

That was all. No Varley. No chandlery door. Nobody seen at all.

Ruth sat with the paper book open on her knee and the screen lit in front of her, and the two of them said different things about the same night in the same woman's name, and the woman stood at the end of her own table in her nightdress and cried, very quietly, so as not to wake anyone.

Ruth knew where Emmet Varley had been at 22:05 on Christmas Eve.

He had been on the shore road with her. The reading had ended at half nine. She had taken Mrs. Aske home, because Mrs. Aske was eighty-one and walked with a stick and lived at the far end of the bay, and Varley had come along to carry the lantern, because the council lamps were out on that stretch and he had offered. They had walked slowly, because Mrs. Aske walked slowly, and talked about nothing, and at about five past ten Mrs. Aske had stopped to get her breath by the sea wall and said the lake smelled of weed, and Varley had held the lantern up so she could see her own front step. At ten past, the bells had gone.

It was in the Kestrel Book. Ruth had written it on Boxing Day, when they were writing down everything, every person, where they had been, because the town needed to know and because it was what the book was for. Mrs. Aske had signed it. Varley had signed it. It was in the book in Ruth's coat.

She did not take the book out. She sat with her hand flat on the outside of her coat, over it, and felt it there.

Emmet Varley was on probation. Everybody on Harbour Street knew it. Six entries in the Kestrel Book had red lines down their margins in Ruth's hand and his signature under each, *FALSE: countersignatures forged, confessed Dec 27, 2108*, and every entry he had written since needed three signatures and not two, and he had never once complained about it. He had brought biscuits. He carried Mrs. Aske's coal. He was a decent man who had done a stupid thing for money and been forgiven in a church hall by eleven hands, and he still could not look Ruth in the eye on some mornings.

Of all the men in Kestrel Point, he was the easiest one to believe a line like that about.

Somebody had known that.

---

By five there were people in the street.

They came out the way people come out after a storm, one at a time, in whatever they had on, and stood on their steps, and then on each other's steps, with their terminals in their hands, lit. It went from house to house faster than Ruth could follow. Somebody at number 9 had woken at three for the heat, as the widow had, and read back, and found a quarrel about a gate that had not happened. Somebody at number 22 had found a debt. Somebody had found her own son described, in her own name, as a liar. They stood in the gray light with the heat already coming up off the stones and held their screens out to each other, and read each other's, and the thing that Ruth would remember longest was how quiet it was. Nobody shouted. They read, and handed the screen back, and read the next one.

Every registered journal on Harbour Street disagreed with the paper it had been, if the paper had been kept. Nobody's registered journal agreed with anyone else's about anything that mattered. The countersignatures stood under all of it, neat, sealed, with their little marks, and people stood in their doorways and looked at their own names under things they had never seen, and then up at their neighbours, and then down again.

Ruth sat on the widow's front step under the street lamp, which was still on, and took the Kestrel Book out of her coat.

She did not know what she meant to do with it. She opened it. She turned to Christmas Eve. Her own entry, from Boxing Day. Mrs. Aske's signature, enormous and shaky, pressed so hard the pencil had gone through in one place. Varley's, small and round and eager. The engineer's, underneath, square, because he had been at the reading and seen them leave together. Then she turned back, and back, through the spring and the winter and the night of the pier, through margins full of other people's hands, *21:52 by my kitchen clock*, *there is no plaque*, *I remember a plaque*, the red lines down Varley's six, the vote, the cards pinned inside the back cover.

It agreed with itself. All of it. Where it disagreed, it said so, in the margin, with a name against each side. It did not agree with any screen on Harbour Street. It had not changed in the night. It could not have. Nobody had been near it but her.

People had begun to come and stand round the step. They did not ask her anything. They looked at the book open on her knee under the lamp, and then at their screens, and then at the book again.

"It's the same," somebody said. "Isn't it. The book. It's the same as it was."

"Yes," said Ruth.

Wren had come down. Ruth had not heard her. She was standing behind her mother's shoulder in an old shirt of her father's that she slept in, barefoot on the warm stone, reading the widow's screen, which the widow was holding out to nobody in particular with both hands, the way you hold out something that has bitten you.

Wren read the Christmas Eve line. She read it twice. Ruth could tell, because her lips moved on the time.

"Somebody wrote it in her voice," said Wren.

Ruth looked up at her.

It was the word. Not *changed*. Not *got into*, which was what the man at number 22 had been saying for half an hour, as if the screens had caught something. Wren had said *wrote*. She had said it the way she said everything, flatly, as if it were obvious and she was mildly surprised that it needed saying. Somebody had sat down somewhere and written it, as you would write an entry in a book, in somebody else's voice, and put somebody else's name under it.

Ruth did not say anything. She filed it, the way she filed the takings. She thought that Wren had been reading the Kestrel Book over her shoulder for a year and a half, and that this was what came of it.

---

At twenty past six she opened the ferry office.

There was no reason not to. The 07:40 would sail. The lake did not care what anyone's screens said, and the fish market across the bay would want its people, and the hospital its nurses, and somebody had to sell the tickets. She unlocked the door and put the float in the till and wrote the date at the top of the ledger.

Then she looked at the Concordance terminal in the corner, gray, with its crest.

She had never touched it. She had watched other people use it for four months, patiently, the way she watched them use the toilets. It was public. Anyone could read any registered journal that its keeper had marked open, and most people marked theirs open, because that was what the outreach woman had told them a good journal was for.

She had to know how far it went.

She sat down in front of it and did not know how to start, and then did. It was easy. It was built to be easy. She did not look at Harbour Street. She knew about Harbour Street. She typed *Gullhaven*, because it was the next town up, and because it was the town in the pairs, and because some part of her had been thinking about the pairs since four in the morning without telling the rest of her.

Net Loft Row had registered its street book in May. She had known that. The Row's people had talked about it on the boat: they had their copy in the Kestrel pairs and another in a farming town inland, and a third copy in the capital could not hurt, and the outreach woman had been very kind. Their keeper had carried the book down to the ferry office himself, here, to this terminal, because Gullhaven did not have one, and sat where Ruth was sitting while the outreach woman scanned it, page by page, and turned the pages for her when she asked him to.

Ruth scrolled down the Row's pages. Most of it she knew. She had poured the tea while it was read aloud.

*Sun 10 Jun 2108.*

A street meeting. She remembered there had been one; the Row had held them in the net loft at the end, on Sunday afternoons, when there was something to settle. This one was longer than she remembered. It ran down the screen, line after line, the way a meeting runs when somebody says something that changes it.

In it, a man stood up. The entry named him. In it, he *admitted*, in front of the whole street, that in November 2107 he had gone to Enforcement and given them the names of the households on the Row who had been sheltering people from the strip.

Two households had been taken that November. Everyone on the shore knew that. One of the men had come back corrected, quiet and pleasant and empty, and had not lived the next year out. The other had not come back at all yet.

Under the entry were three countersignatures.

She read the names, and then she read them again.

Two of them were dead. They had been brother and brother-in-law, the two oldest men on the Row, and they had gone down in the storms last autumn, on the same night, in a loft that had been one house in one history and another house in the other. Ruth had sold tickets to their widows for the funeral sailing. Their names were in the Kestrel Book, in the entry for that night, and on a slip somewhere in a farmhouse in the border zone, in an old man's song.

They had signed this. The screen said so.

She knew the name in the entry. Everyone on the shore knew it. She had known it for twenty years. She did not say it aloud in the empty ferry office, and afterwards she was never quite sure why not.

Outside, the sun had come up over the lake, white and hot already. The water lay flat to the far shore. She heard the engine before she saw the boat: a low steady note, coming down the bay from the north.

The Gullhaven launch came round the end of the harbour wall to meet the 07:40, as it did every morning in the season, low in the water with its first passengers standing in the stern. Its skipper was at the wheel in his shirtsleeves, with his cap pushed back. He saw her through the ferry-office window, as he saw her every morning, and lifted his hand to her.

Ruth lifted hers.
