# Chapter 25: No Remedy

*Friday, November 29 – Monday, December 2, 2109. The farmhouse; the flat by the old river; the Justice Center steps; the Commission, Veridica Capital*

---

On the Friday Marcus came to the farmhouse by day, with Dmitri driving, and had to be back before dark, and he brought a draft of what he meant to say.

It was four pages. He had written it over three nights, and it ended in a remedy: an apology from the Commission, in its own name; a petition for Julian Kovač's reinstatement; an independent inquiry into the operation; a fund, for the lakeshore towns, for the dead. It was, he thought, a decent thing. It admitted the wrong and set out to repair it. It was what you did when you had done a wrong: you confessed, and you brought the repair with you, so that the confession was not only words.

Vance read the object first and then the words. She held the four pages the way she held everything now, in her left hand, with her right flat on the table edge to keep it still, and she read them through once, slowly, and set them down.

"You watched the First Minister do this," she said. "On the eighteenth of December, last year. He confessed first, before anyone could expose him, in his own words, with the Concordance attached as the repair, and the country thanked him for it, and the Concordance was the thing that let them edit two million journals in a night." She tapped the fourth page, the remedy. "It worked. It was written to work. There is a page for it."

"Principle Six," said Marcus.

"I am not going to quote you the page," said Vance. "You don't need the wording, and the wording isn't yours to have yet. I'll tell you what it does, in my own words, because I was in the room when it was argued and I let it into the document." She looked at him. "When a thing is going to come out anyway, the design tells it first, before anyone else can, in its own words, with its own repair attached. Because the public forgives a system that confesses and mends. It never forgives one that is caught. A confession with a remedy attached is an offer. And the design accepts offers. It is built to. It will take your apology and your petition and your fund, and it will absorb them, and it will be stronger, because it will have confessed and mended in front of everyone, exactly on schedule, and you will have done its work for it one more time."

She took a pencil, and held it until her hand was still, and drew one line through the last page, the remedy.

"There," she said.

"Then what's left," said Marcus.

"The truth," said Vance. "And nothing to do with it. No apology in the Commission's name, no petition, no fund, no inquiry you offer them. Just the truth, said by you, with nothing attached that they can take. It is the one confession the design has no page for, because it offers the design nothing. It cannot be absorbed, because it asks for nothing to be done. It only sits there, true, in the record, having been said." She set the pencil down. "It will cost you everything and give you nothing back. That is how you will know it is the right one."

Old Songs was at the stove, and did not hum.

---

Saturday and Sunday, at the flat, the draft got shorter each time he rewrote it.

He took out the Commission's name. He took out the petition. He took out the fund and the inquiry, one at a time, and each time he took one out the thing got smaller and truer and harder to say, until it was a page, and then half a page.

Kira read one version, on Saturday night. She had come back from the farmhouse; they were strained, and together, and she read the half page at the kitchen table in silence and gave it back.

"Don't name us," she said. "Ours are ours. You did it. Say you did it. Don't put the rest of us in the record of your confession to carry your weight. If we want to say our own, we'll say our own." She looked at him. "That's the only note I have."

Kovač came on the Sunday, with the letter.

"It's my folder," she said, standing at the door with her hands in her coat pockets. "I'll hold it. And I'll read it, on the day, if you still mean to do it with the letter in it. It should be read by the one who kept it, not by the one it clears. That's mine." She pushed her glasses up. "You're sure there's no remedy in it."

"There's no remedy in it."

"Good," said Kovač. "The ones with a remedy are the ones that get used. I ran a facility for eleven years. I know an offer when I see one."

---

Monday the second of December, five to two, the Justice Center steps.

It was cold and clear. There was no lectern; he had asked for no lectern; a man who confesses does not need a thing to stand behind. The steps were wide and gray and the crowd stood on them and below them: press, Commission staff, the regulars who came to the Lost Hour stone on the second and the fifteenth, and at the edge, with placards, the League of the Record, who had come because Pell had told them to come, because a commissioner's fall was the League's whole argument made flesh.

Near the back, hood up, there was a white beard. Old Songs, officially dead, in the open air of the capital, who did not come into the capital, had come. Marcus saw him and started a fact entry about him in his head and did not finish it. He did not know how the old man had got there, or who had driven him, or what it had cost a man who had hummed the falling phrase in August to come and stand in a crowd and hear a confession with nothing attached. He only saw the white beard at the back, and did not finish the entry, and turned to the people in front of him.

At two o'clock he spoke.

"My name is Marcus Chen. Between the second of September and the twenty-sixth of October this year, I used the trust of Julian Kovač, Senior Historian of the Chronos Temporal Archive, to enter his Archive. He gave me his access because he was my friend. I let him believe I was."

He did not raise his voice. He did not have to; it was very quiet on the steps.

"We meant to cut one hand out of Chronos's record. One key, one misuse, the attacks on Chronos's memory. That is what I asked for, and that is what I believed we were doing. Instead we wrote our history into theirs. A hundred and fifty million people woke on the twenty-sixth of October believing a past that Veridica gave them in the night. I did that. Commissioner Osman built the instrument under protest, to make it as narrow as a thing like that can be, and it was not narrow, because the thing it went into was not what any of us believed it was. That is not her weight. It is mine. I asked for it."

He stopped, and went on.

"I checked the record. The record said Julian Kovač had held the key that did these things since 2104. I did not ask the witness. There was a witness. His own aunt works in this building, and she knew, and I did not ask her, because the record already said what I was afraid of, and I stopped checking." He said it flatly, because it was the worst thing and the truest and did not deserve any voice but a flat one. "I have spent two years telling anyone who would listen that the answer to a single record is many witnesses. And when it mattered, when a man's life was the question, I trusted one record, because it said what I feared, and I did not ask one witness. That is the whole of what I did wrong, and it is worse than the operation, because the operation was a mistake and this was a betrayal of the only thing I believe."

Helena Kovač stepped forward, at that, and read the letter.

She read it in her flat Registrar's voice, standing a little below him on the steps, holding the single sheet. *They've made me Senior Historian. I start in February. They've given me the old man's keys. I'm frightened of them, Aunt Helena. Twentieth of January 2108.* And she read the date twice, the way the method said you read a date that mattered, and folded the letter, and stepped back.

"He did not hold that key in December 2107," said Marcus. "His record says he did. I believed his record. He received it in January 2108, the month before he took office, from a predecessor who retired that January and died the next month. I will say nothing about who did hold it, because I do not know, and I have done enough damage with things I did not check." He looked out over the crowd. "I have no remedy. I am not asking you for anything. There is no petition. There is no inquiry I am offering you, no fund, no apology in anyone's name but my own. I am telling you what I did, so that it is written down. That is all. That is the whole of it."

He did not ask anyone to forgive him. He did not look at the white beard at the back.

---

The Judge weighed his words.

It weighed them against a record that now agreed with Veridica everywhere, so that half of what he had confessed to, the writing of a nation's history, was no longer in the record as a crime, because in the record it had not happened, because the record was the thing he had changed. He had confessed to altering a past that the past no longer showed altered. The Judge could only weigh the contamination of a man who said he had done a thing the record did not record, which was its own kind of contamination, the contamination of a man at odds with the record.

At fifty-two minutes past two, Kira read the public registry at the foot of the steps.

*CHEN, M. 0.57.*

Up from 0.44. It did not reach 0.60; it did not put him in custody; it rose to 0.57 and it stopped, and it was 0.57 at midnight, and 0.57 the next morning, and it stayed there. At half past three the government issued a statement of one sentence: *the Commissioner speaks for himself.* The back of the crowd was empty by then. The League had got what it came for and gone to write it up.

---

At twenty to five he was in the commissioners' room at the Commission, and Chen Zhao came to him.

The Minister had tea in a glass, and he was warm, and fatherly, and he put his hand on Marcus's arm.

"Very foolish, cousin," he said. "And very brave. The two usually travel together, in my experience. I have been foolish and brave myself, once or twice. It is survivable." He drank some of his tea. "You should rest. You look terrible."

Marcus had stopped keeping things back. It was part of the confession, he had decided, that he would stop managing what he let people see, and so he told Chen Zhao a thing he had not planned to tell him, because it was true and he had stopped holding true things back.

"The pages Julian found," he said. "In the Archive. They carried a copy numeral. Seven. Like the numeral on the pages that came to the intake last year, which was ten, and the one on the torn page from the Lost Hour, which was eleven. And the contents of Julian's pages list a Register of Contributors. A list of the people who wrote the thing. The eleven came out of the Ministry's own correspondence, years ago. So the copies run through the Ministry. I've come as far up as I can with it, which is to you."

Chen Zhao's face did not change at all. It stayed warm.

"Then let me help you," he said. "Let me help you trace these copy numerals, and find this Register of theirs. The Ministry's archive is sixty years deep, and it is at your disposal, cousin. If there is a list of names, we will find it together." He patted Marcus's arm. "You have earned the right to know who wrote the thing that was done to you. We will look for it."

"Thank you," said Marcus, and meant it, and accepted, because he had stopped holding true things back and because the Minister's kindness was real, which it was, and he showed Chen Zhao nothing, because there was nothing to show; the originals had gone back to a box in Chronos in September, and Marcus had only his notes and Kira's photographs, which he did not know Kira had.

The reader would know what Marcus did not. Marcus did not.

---

The flat, late.

He opened the lists. Under CHECKED he wrote, in his small historian's hand, the entry he had come home to make.

*Dec 2, 2109. Kovač, J.: innocent. Verification: a letter. Witness: Kovač, H.*

Then he turned to UNCHECKED, where *Julian?* had stood since the fourteenth of February, underlined on the fifteenth of August, carried through the whole of it. He moved it off UNCHECKED. He did not cross it out, because he did not cross things out; he drew a single line through it so it could still be read, and wrote beside it, *resolved: see CHECKED, Dec 2*, and the question mark, after all the months, was answered.

He held the pencil over the empty line where *Julian?* had been.

There was nothing to write in its place. He had carried the question for nine months, and it had been the engine of the whole year, and now it was answered, and the answer was *innocent*, and there was nothing that went in the space a nine-month question leaves when it is finally answered and the answer destroys you. He looked at the empty line for a long time. Then he folded the lists, and turned off the lamp, and did not write anything in the space, because some spaces a man leaves empty, as a record of the shape of what used to be there.
