# Chapter 2: Julian?

*Thursday, August 15, 2109. The Old Customs House, Saltmarsh Crossing, the neutral zone; the flat by the old river, Veridica Capital*

---

*Aug 15, 2109. 10:00. Old Customs House, Saltmarsh Crossing. Emergency session under the Lakes Talks' terms. Involved parties: Veridica; Chronos; Synthesis (Park, J.). Purpose: the Rewriting. Status:*

Marcus left the last field blank. He had written the entry in his head in the car, on the marsh road, with the window down and the insects loud in the reeds, and he had come to *Status* and found that he did not know what to put there, and so he had left it, the way he had once left another entry open for most of a year.

*The Rewriting.* The press had given it the name by noon yesterday, and by evening it was everybody's. He did not like it. It was accurate, which was the trouble.

The fog had burned off by nine. In February the Old Customs House had stood in it like a ship in a harbour, gray on gray, with its flag hanging wet. Now it stood on its spit of hard ground in full August sun, the slate of the roof gone green and warm, the stone the colour of bread, and the marsh all round it was loud and alive, reeds to the horizon and a heron going over and the air thick with small flying things. He had not known it could look like this. He had only ever seen it in winter.

Inside, it was the same. The shielded rooms with their pale panels behind the old plaster, the long table, the water jugs. And at the door, as the Chronos delegates came through, the same small pause: half a step, a hand lifted towards the place behind the ear and lowered again. A small deafness. He watched for it, and saw it, and was obscurely glad that something had not changed.

---

Professor Park Ji-won had come from the Synthesis in a day. She was seventy-five now and had flown overnight, and she poured the tea herself before anything was said, as she always had, and pushed the first cup across to the Chronos side and the second to his.

"I had hoped," she said, "not to see this room again for some time. Perhaps that was optimistic." She looked round the table with her round, lined, amused face, which today was not amused. "Perhaps we may at least agree on the date."

Nobody laughed. The Chronos delegation did not smile, which was their laughing. Neither did the Veridican.

Administrator Constance Aubry stated Chronos's position. She did it coolly and courteously, with her hands folded on the table and her gray eyes moving from face to face as though she were reading a column of figures and finding them all within range. In the night of the thirteenth to fourteenth, she said, the witness index of the Chronos lakeshore networks had been altered during an interval of seventeen minutes. The interval was the same, to the second, as the interval of December 2107. The alterations had been made under credentials of the Chronos Temporal Archive. Chronos regarded it as probable, at a level she would not state in this room, that those credentials had been obtained and used by Veridica, as Veridica had previously sought to obtain them by other means. Chronos reserved its response.

She said *previously* without looking at him. She did not need to.

Then it was his turn. He had the Commission's line in his case, drafted overnight by three people and checked by a fourth, and he gave it in order. Veridica's own registered journals had been altered in the same interval and in the same manner. Two million of them. The authority under which the alterations had been accepted was not Veridican. Veridica had been robbed in the same night, by the same hand, and had come here to say so.

Aubry listened to all of it with courteous attention. When he finished she said, "Thank you, Commissioner," and nothing else, and he understood that she had heard the same evidence he had and drawn the opposite line through it. Chronos's books had been altered under a Chronos seal: therefore Veridica had stolen the seal. Veridica's journals had been altered under a foreign authority: therefore the foreigner had done it. Each side held up the same night as proof against the other.

He noticed that this was so. He noticed it the way you notice a stair that is slightly the wrong height, and then he did not take it any further, because there was a session to get through, and because taking it further led somewhere he did not want to go before lunch.

---

Julian sat beside Aubry.

Marcus had not let himself look at him during the statements. He looked now, while Park was reading the secretariat's summary, and found that Julian was looking at the table.

He was gray. That was the first thing. Not pale; gray, the colour people go when they have stopped sleeping and started functioning instead. He wore the cardigan under the archivist's jacket, as in winter, in August heat, and he had not shaved closely, and there was a small cut on his jaw where he had tried. He sat very straight. When Park's summary reached the number of lakeshore network books altered in Chronos, he said, quietly, "Two hundred and eleven," before she could, and then, "I'm sorry. It's two hundred and eleven," and Park thanked him, and he looked at his hands.

They were his books. The kitchens he had sat in all spring. His networks' entries, in the Chronos witness index, had been altered in the night, and everybody in those kitchens had read their own neighbours' names under things they had never signed, and they had rung him. Marcus knew that without being told. Julian was the liaison. People rang the liaison.

The six keepers from the Night of Copies had rung him too, Julian said, in the afternoon session, when Park asked whether anything had held. Their paper books agreed with each other. They agreed with nothing in the index. He said it plainly, as a fact for the record, and Aubry, beside him, wrote nothing down.

He was still funny, once. Late in the morning, when a Synthesis clerk asked whether the Chronos delegation could confirm the exact time of the first alteration, Julian said, "Three twelve and no seconds. I'm afraid it was punctual," and the secretariat laughed, and then looked embarrassed for laughing, and Julian looked embarrassed for having made them.

Marcus did not laugh. He sat with his pencil and his blank *Status* and thought about February.

---

At the break Julian stood up and looked at him, and then at the side door, the way he had looked at it every break in the winter, and Marcus understood that it was only habit.

The aide was not there. There was no directive now; MERIDIAN had been published in December from Julian's own log, and Aubry had read it out herself, coolly, in this building. Nobody needed Julian in the garden any more. He went anyway, because his feet knew the way, and Marcus followed, because his did.

The garden was full of sun.

He stopped in the doorway. In winter it had been a gray room of fog with a bare tree trained against the wall like a set of empty shelves. Now the gravel was white and hot, and the roses along the path had leafed and bloomed and gone over and were blooming again, and the pear tree against the south wall was in full leaf, dense and dark, every flat branch furred with green. Along the wires hung small hard green fruit, dozens of them, the size of a thumb.

They sat on the bench. Everything Julian saw and heard went into his log, here as everywhere. Marcus knew it. In November he had chosen this bench for exactly that reason. Today he wished, for the first time, that it were otherwise.

"They want an account of the key," said Julian, after a while. He said it to the tree. "Temporal Defence. Every use. Every hour. Since—" He made a small gesture. "All of it. I've given them two days of it already. They're very thorough. I'd be disappointed if they weren't."

"Do they think it was you?"

"They think it was Veridica." Julian smiled faintly. "Using me. They're very polite about the difference. I don't think they've decided whether I'm a door or a doorkeeper."

He was quiet for a moment. A bee went past, heavy, and into the roses.

"I don't understand how they used the key," said Julian. "I've only had it since—"

The garden door opened behind them.

"Senior Historian."

Aubry stood on the step in the sun, with a tablet held against her chest and her face courteous and distracted, a woman with twelve things to do and this the ninth. "The mediator is recalling the delegations. The doors close at the half-hour. Protocol asks that we go in together." She looked at Marcus and inclined her head a precise few degrees. "Commissioner."

Julian stood up at once. He always stood up at once for Aubry; Marcus had noticed it in winter. He brushed the gravel dust from his trousers, and said, "Of course," and went in after her without looking back.

Marcus sat on the bench a moment longer.

He did not go after him. He did not, then or afterwards, at the door or at the gate, say *since when?* He told himself later that he had not noticed the sentence was unfinished. That was not true. He had noticed. He had heard it break off, and felt the shape of where it would have gone, and let it go, because finishing it would have meant asking Julian a question about the key, on this bench, into his log, and he did not want his voice in Julian's record asking that.

He would think about that, later, for a long time.

---

The February finding sat in him all afternoon.

He had not told Julian about February. Nobody had. It lived in a gray folder in Nikolai's kitchen and in one line of Marcus's own lists, and it said that the seal of the Chronos Archive had countersigned the seventeen minutes of the Gap. And last night the same seal had countersigned the Rewriting. And the man who held that seal was three places down the table from him, gray with exhaustion, writing nothing, looking at his hands every time the key was mentioned, the way he had in November.

Marcus looked at Julian's hands too. Long, and narrow, and still. He hated himself for looking, and looked anyway, and then looked away at the window, where the marsh shone.

At five Park closed the session. Nothing had been agreed. Chronos reserved its response. Veridica regretted that it had not been believed. Park said, "Perhaps," and did not finish.

At the gate Julian caught him up.

"I'll write," he said. "Through Park's secretariat. In handwriting. You'll have to read it." It was what he had said in February, and he heard it, and smiled a little. "I'm glad you came. I didn't know if they'd send you."

"They sent me."

"I'm glad," said Julian again, simply, and meant it, and went across the gravel to the Chronos car.

Marcus stood at the gate and looked back at the garden wall, over which the pear tree showed dark and heavy. He wanted to say the name of the fruit on it. Not *pear*. The young hard green ones that came before. He reached for the word and found a smooth place where it should have been. No edge to catch. He did not know if he had ever had a word for it, or if the white chair had taken it, and there was nobody on the gravel to ask.

---

*Train, 17:52. Involved parties: Chen, M. Subject: the key.*

He made the entries in order, as the lakeshore went past the window gold and flat.

*December 13, 2108. Garden, Saltmarsh Crossing. Kovač, J. showed the senior key, unasked. "So you can see there is nothing behind it." Status: CHECKED.*

*February 14, 2109. The seal on the Gap is the Archive's seal. Volkov, N.: "I know the key. I don't know the hand." Status: UNCHECKED.*

*August 14, 2109. The seal on the Rewriting is the same seal. Volkov, N.: same words. Status:*

He looked at the second half of Nikolai's sentence for a long time, at the window, with the train swaying. *I don't know the hand.* It was a careful sentence. It was a true one. It was the kind of sentence Marcus had once built his whole life on, the difference between what the record showed and what a person had done.

Somewhere between the second and the third station, without his deciding it, the second half fell away. He noticed afterwards that it had gone. He did not go back for it.

---

The flat was dark when he got in. Kira was asleep, on her side, with the journal on the pillow beside her, open. He did not read it. He stood in the bedroom doorway and listened to her breathe, and then went back to the kitchen and sat at the table in the light from the street.

Chronos published everything. That was the whole of Chronos. Its Temporal Archive kept a public roll of its office-holders, open to anyone in any nation, updated by the second, and the Commission's terminal could reach it through the Synthesis exchange in under a minute. He had never looked at it. He had never needed to. He looked now.

*MERA, A. Senior Historian emeritus from 2104.*

*KOVAČ, J. Senior Historian. Key-holder since 2104.*

He read it twice. Then he did the arithmetic, because he could not help doing it, the way Julian could not help the seconds. 2104. The Gap was December 2107. Three years and more. Julian had held the key at the Gap. He had held it alone, for years before, and every year after, every night, under his name.

He thought of Helena Kovač.

She would know. She had followed her nephew's career for fifteen years from across the lake, in library carrels, with her coat on. She was in his own building, behind the testimony intake, two floors down, every weekday from nine. He could ask her what year the boy had been given the key. He could ask her tomorrow.

He did not want to make her a witness against Julian. He told himself that. It was a delicate thing to ask a woman about her nephew's guilt. It was kinder not to. He told himself that too, and it was true, and it was not the reason.

He took the lists out of his coat and unfolded them on the table under the street light.

At the bottom of UNCHECKED, where it had stood since the fourteenth of February in his small precise hand, was the line, and at the end of the line the name, and after the name the question mark.

*Julian?*

He looked at it for a long time. He had the pencil in his hand. He did not cross it out.

He underlined it.
