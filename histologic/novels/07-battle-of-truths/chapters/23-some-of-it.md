# Chapter 23: Some of It

*Friday, November 22, 2109. The farmhouse; Nikolai and Maya's flat; the central plaza, Veridica Capital*

---

Elena Volkov counted the kitchen left to right, the way she counted a patrol.

Alexei at the stove, too loud this morning. Dmitri in the doorway, too quiet. Kira at the table with the courier schedules, not looking up. That was three. Nikolai was not there, had not been there in a week, and she wrote nothing down about it, because she did not write things down; she carried a notebook she had never once written in; she remembered everything and set nothing on paper, because paper could be taken, and because a thing she had set down was a thing she had to defend, and she had learned long ago to defend only the people and never the record.

It was a week since Julian Kovač had published his log, and the circle had broken along the lines each of them had always stood on.

"We did what was necessary," said Alexei, from the stove, too loud. He said it to the pan. "Seven dead and then none. That's what we did. I'll take it."

Elena knew that voice. It was the voice Alexei used when he had decided a thing was true because he could not afford for it to be false. He had driven a lead car through a storm once and seen a van go over an embankment in his mirror and been unable to turn back, and he had said *they'd have done it to us, keep driving* in exactly this voice, and had believed it because the alternative was to stop the car in the middle of a manufactured storm and die with everyone in it. He said *I'll take it* now the same way. He would take it. He would carry the seven saved and refuse to carry the nation rewritten, because a man cannot carry everything, and Alexei had learned, young, to choose what he would carry and let the rest fall, and it was the only way he had found to go on being the one who went back to the fence.

"We became the thing we were used by," said Dmitri, from the doorway, without a contraction, flat. "That is also what we did. Both are true. You do not get to take only the first."

Neither of them gave ground. The bond was plain to see, to Elena, who had watched these three since they were boys arguing on a parapet: a weight in the room, a pull, two of them leaning against each other through a thing that had no words, and Nikolai absent at the far end of it, in a flat in the capital, a cold hand they could both feel and neither could reach.

---

Marcus came at noon.

Dmitri had driven him out, by day, because at 0.44 a night logged here would do what nothing had yet done and carry him over, and he had to be back before dark. He came into the kitchen in his good coat, thin, and Kira got up from the table without a word and went out into the yard.

Elena watched her go. Then she followed her, because there were things you said to Kira that you did not say to Marcus, and because Kira was the only one of them Elena could speak to straight, both of them being women who had loved men who had lied to them and done it for reasons.

Kira was at the fence, looking at the field.

"He let him hope," said Kira. She said it level, which was how she said the things that had gone deepest. "It's in the log. All of it. Julian published it. The bench. The thing Marcus wouldn't let him say, and then let him almost say, and then—" She stopped. "He let him hope about a bench in the summer. Knowing. That's in the log too, if you know how to read a man's heart rate at nanosecond resolution, which the whole world now does."

"That's the work," said Elena.

"You taught it to him."

"Yes," said Elena.

That was all. Kira did not say anything else, and Elena did not, and they stood at the fence and looked at the field, and there was nothing to add to it, because it was true, all of it, every clause, and neither of them was going to pretend otherwise to make the morning easier.

---

At half past four she went into the capital, to Nikolai's flat.

Maya opened the door on the chain. Behind her the flat was dim, and Elena could see the gray folder on the kitchen table and a plate of something barely touched beside it.

"He won't come out," said Maya, through the gap. "He's been like this since the fifteenth. Since the log. He's barely eaten." She looked at Elena with the flat prickly directness that was hers. "He keeps saying the same thing."

Through the door, very quietly, from the dark of the flat, Nikolai said it.

"I said it in February. 'I know the key. I don't know the hand.' Everybody heard the first half."

Elena stood at the door with her hand on the frame.

The bond was not hers. She had watched it her whole life, from the outside, since the three of them were small and one person in three bodies; she had seen it hold through correction and isolation and a storm; but it was not hers, and she could not reach Nikolai through it the way his brothers could, as weight, as a cold hand warmed. She could only stand at a door.

"Kolya," she said. Once. It was all she had.

He did not answer. Maya closed the door, gently, not in Elena's face but between them, and Elena stood on the landing for a moment and then went down, because there was nowhere to go through a door that a man had shut from the inside, and she had learned that too, a long time ago, from a brother in a study with the lamp on and the door shut, who had come out white and said nothing and gone away.

---

At twenty to six she went to the central plaza, because that was where his habit put him.

Marcus walked through the plaza most evenings. He had done it in 2105, before any of it, a lonely believer taking the last of the autumn sun past the fountain, and he did it now, and she had read it in his file before she ever met him, the file that told her where a man would be and when, so that she could be there too, by the fountain, as if by accident, being terrible with directions.

It was dark now, and cold, with the lamps laid along the water of the fountain and the Lost Hour stone pale a few steps from it. She waited in the blind spot of the colonnade, where the camera arcs did not reach, counting them, because Marcus was at 0.44 and an outlaw recruiter at his elbow on camera was exactly the thing that had put him in a white room the first time, and she was not going to be the one who sent him back.

She had her opening line ready. She always had her opening line ready; it was the oldest habit she had; *I'm terrible with directions*, which had been the first thing she ever said to him, by this fountain, four years ago, when he had been a target and she had been doing the work.

She did not use it.

She said his name instead. "Marcus."

It was the first time in four years she had come to him without technique. She heard herself do it and did not take it back.

---

He came into the colonnade, out of the camera arcs, because he knew the blind spots too now; she had taught the whole family to count arcs, one way or another.

They stood by the fountain, a few steps from the Lost Hour stone.

"You have the dress under CHECKED," she said. "Blue. From September. In my name. You don't have to ask again." She looked at the water. "I'll give you one more. You never asked for it, which is why you can have it. The dinner, three days after the café, at the restaurant over the river. The dress was green."

"Green," said Marcus. He did not say he remembered it. He could not; the chair had taken the colours for good; he would never remember either dress; he had only her word for them, testimony, not memory, which was all he could have of that autumn and all he would ever have.

"The part that was real," said Elena.

He waited. He had learned, from her, how to wait for a sentence.

"At the dinner. Over the river. You told me about your parents. Your father the enforcer, your mother who taught history and marked essays in green ink. And I reached across the table and said, *they'd be proud of you.*" She said it flatly, looking at the water. "That wasn't in the plan. The plan was to agree with you, to be the thing you were missing, to let you choose to trust me. I wasn't supposed to tell you things. I said it because it was true, and because I wanted it to be true for you, and it was the only thing I said in eight weeks that wasn't work." She was quiet for a moment. "That's the part that was real. You asked which part, in September, and I wouldn't say. That's the part."

---

She had not meant to tell him any of it. She had come to the plaza meaning to say one thing, the warning about forgiveness, and go. But he stood at the fountain in the cold with the lamplight in his glasses, thin, at 0.44, having done the thing she had taught him to do and done it well, and she found that she owed him the rest of it, because she had opened the door in September with *some of it was real* and left him four years to stand in the gap of it, and a person who opens a door like that and does not finish owes a debt, and Elena paid her debts, because she carried people instead of records and a debt was a kind of person.

"What did it cost," said Marcus. Not *you*. He left the *you* off. He had learned that from her too, probably.

"I couldn't do the rest cleanly after that," said Elena. "Once I'd said one true thing, the rest of it had a true thing standing next to it, and I could feel the difference the whole time, in every one of the forty-seven, in every one of the hundred and twenty-seven hours, and I remember each of them, because I remember everything, because I write nothing down." She turned the cup of her hands over, empty, a gesture left over from holding something. "In my correction cell, when they ran me, I had to hide the thing I most wanted to keep, and the thing I most wanted to keep was that dinner, you and the green dress and *they'd be proud of you*, and I hid it in the dullest thing I knew, which was the operation's own log. I hid the one real thing inside the record of the work. I've never known why I kept the device in that drawer, Marcus. I've never known whether I left it where you'd find it. I don't claim I did. I don't know."

She stopped.

"And the seven before you," she said. "I still don't know whether I saved them or destroyed them. Either. Both. I know all seven of their names and I've never written one of them down, and I never will, because a name written down is a name that can be found, and I would rather carry them than risk them. That's what it cost. Carrying people instead of records. For the rest of your life."

---

"What he'll carry," she said, and this was the thing she had come to say, "is the same. You'll remember every hour. The way I do. Julian's hours are written down, for everyone, by the man they were done to, which is worse in one way and better in another, because at least his are outside him and yours will be inside you, and a thing outside you can be put down and a thing inside you cannot."

Marcus did not say anything. He was looking at the water, and his glasses had the lamplight in them so she could not see his eyes, which she was glad of, because she did not want to see them do what she knew they were doing.

A camera arc was coming round. She heard it, in the way she heard such things, as a pressure to be out of a place by a certain second, and she stood up from the stone edge of the fountain.

"You'll want someone to forgive you," she said. Low, and hoarse, and direct for once, with no technique in it at all. "Everyone who does this wants that. Don't ask him. It's the one thing you can't take from him too. You've had his trust and his nation and the hope about the bench. Leave him the not-forgiving. It's all he's got, and it's his."

She walked out of the colonnade's shadow, ahead of the arc, and did not look back.

He stayed at the fountain. She knew he would. She had known him for four years, longer than she had meant to, and she knew that he stayed where things had happened, the way she went back to files, the way they all of them went back, over and over, to the places where they had done the thing or had it done to them, because that was the only kind of record their kind of people could trust: not the log, not the facts, but the standing in the place, alone, afterwards, with your hand over the thing, keeping it from getting out.
