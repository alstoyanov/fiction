# Chapter 3: Gullhaven

*Monday, August 19 – Tuesday, August 20, 2109. Kestrel Point, the shore road, and Gullhaven, the claimed strip*

---

On the fifth day the Commission said, on every screen, that entries had been altered, and that it could not yet say which ones.

Ruth heard it in the ferry office at eight in the morning, from the little radio on the shelf above the till, in a woman's careful voice. *Keepers of registered journals are asked to compare their entries with their stamped paper originals where these exist, and to report discrepancies to the Concordance offices. The Commission asks the public to treat any registered entry concerning another person with caution until further notice.* Then the weather. Then the price of fish.

*With caution.* She wrote the phrase on the back of the float slip, because she wanted to remember the exact words, and looked at them.

People had been treating the entries with caution since Wednesday. That was not the trouble. The trouble was that caution did not choose. A woman who had always suspected her brother-in-law of stealing from their mother read a registered line that said he had, in her own sister's name, countersigned, and she treated it with caution. She read the line that said she was a fool for trusting him with caution too, and then she stopped speaking to him. People believed the altered entries that said what they already feared. They did not believe the ones that didn't. Nobody had told them to. Nobody had needed to. It was only what people did.

On Harbour Street the man at number 16 had not been round to number 14 since Wednesday, though the widow had gone round herself with her paper book in her hands and shown him. He had read it on his step, and nodded, and said he believed her. He had not come round. It was not that he didn't believe her. It was that the line was still on the screen, and his name was still under it, and he could not look at her without seeing it.

The 07:40 sailed with twenty-six. The Gullhaven launch came round the harbour wall at twenty past seven to meet it, as it always did in the season, with its engine low and steady.

There were three passengers in its stern. There were usually nine or ten.

Its skipper brought it alongside the steps and held it there while they got off, and he did not lift his hand to Ruth through the window. He did not look up at all. He looked at the water by the steps, at the rope in his hands, and when the last of the three was up he pushed off without a word and turned the launch in the harbour mouth and went back up the shore, alone, with nobody in the stern.

Ruth watched it go. She knew why there were three.

She had read the Row's page on Wednesday morning at twenty past six, on the gray terminal in the corner, and had told nobody. She had told herself she was waiting until she knew more. She had known enough. She had known the name and the dead men under it, and she had stood at this window and lifted her hand to him, and he had lifted his.

---

It was the real thing underneath that made it hold. She understood that by Monday, the way you understand the set of a current by watching what it carries.

In November 2107, two households on Net Loft Row had been taken by Enforcement in the night. That had happened. Everybody on the shore knew it. There had been people from the strip sleeping in the lofts that autumn, two families, the kind who came across in the bad weeks with what they could carry and went on when they could, and the Row had fed them and said nothing. Then one night the vans had come, and they had known which doors to knock on. They had known which lofts.

Somebody had told them. The Row had lived with that for nearly two years. They had not said it aloud, because there was nobody to say it to, and because once you said it you had to say who.

One of the men had come back from correction in the spring. Quiet, pleasant, empty, as they came back. He had sat on his step in the sun all summer and said good morning to everybody, and in the autumn he had died, of nothing anyone could name, in his sleep. His wife still lived on the Row. The other man had not come back at all.

And now there was a page that said who. In a dead man's handwriting, as it were. Under a dead man's name.

That was why it held. A lie on its own would have floated. This one had been tied to a grief the Row already carried, and the grief kept it down.

---

At 17:40 the launch did not come.

Ruth sold the tickets for the evening boat and watched the harbour wall, and at ten to six she watched it harder, and at six the 17:40 went out with nobody from the Row on it because there was no launch to bring them down. She told herself it was the engine. It had been the engine once in July.

At ten past six a woman came along Harbour Street from the north end on foot, in a headscarf, with her shoes in her hand and her feet gray with road dust. Ruth knew her by sight: a Gullhaven woman who sold eggs at the Kestrel market on Saturdays. She had walked the eleven kilometres of the shore road because there was no boat, and because, she said, standing at the ferry-office counter drinking the water Ruth gave her, she did not want to be on the Row tonight.

"They've asked him to stay in," she said. "They went to his door at four, a few of them, and asked him nicely to stay in his house. He's been out on the launch every day, as if nothing. They don't like that. They're meeting at seven, in the loft at the end. To have it out." She put the glass down. "He's got the book, you know. The Row's book. He's kept it since the first spring. He told them it would show. They said he'd have written it himself."

Ruth stood with her hand flat on the counter.

The Row's book. The paper one. The one he had carried down this gangway in May and sat with at that terminal while the outreach woman turned its pages. He had it in his house, and he would hold it up, and it would say what he said it said, and nobody on the Row would believe a word in it, because he had kept it. Of course they wouldn't. She wouldn't, in their place. A man's own book was a man's own book.

But there was another copy.

---

It was in the reading room, on the shelf, behind two locks, in Kestrel hands.

Ruth knew exactly what was in it, because she had made the tea. In February, two people from the Row had sat at the long table for two days and read their whole book aloud, page by page, while the Kestrel copyists wrote it into the pairs volume, and afterwards they had gone back through the copy with a ruler under each line and checked it against the original, word for word, and initialled every page. Nobody on the Row had been accused of anything in February. Nobody had had any reason to change a line. It was the one copy of the Row's book that its keeper could not have written, because he had not been in the room.

She did not have the key. The reading room's keys went to the day keeper, and the day keeper this week was the schoolmaster, who lived at the top of the hill. It took her eleven minutes to get there and back. She knew because she looked at the clock when she left and when she came in, and she wrote both times down on the float slip without deciding to.

She opened the reading room and took down the pairs.

Then she did a thing she would think about for the rest of her life. She did what the book had taught her. She sat down at the long table and checked before she carried.

It was the method. You did not take a page to a quarrel until you had read it against the hands that signed it. She found the Row's pages. She found Sunday, the tenth of June, 2108. She read the street meeting, line by line, the way it had been read aloud at this table in February.

There was a meeting. It was about the drains. There was a dispute about whose turn it was to clear the gully at the end of the Row, and a long discussion of a broken gate, and a vote. There was no admission. There was nobody standing up. There was nobody's name at all, except the names of the people who had been there, and among them his, saying that he would see to the gate.

Under it, the countersignatures, copied in Kestrel hands from the original, and beside each one, in the margin, the initials of the two Row people who had checked it in February. She read the two dead men's names. One of them had added a line in his own hand in the original, and the copyist had copied it faithfully, with a note that it was in the margin there: *Rain all afternoon. Gully overflowing again.*

It took time. She knew it was taking time. She could feel it, the way you feel a boat drifting off its mooring while you look for the rope. It was twenty-five to eight when she shut the book.

---

There was no car on Harbour Street that could be had at that hour, and the 21:40 went the wrong way. There was the shore road, eleven kilometres north along the lake, and her feet.

Wren was in the doorway when she came out of the reading room with the pairs in her coat beside the Kestrel Book.

"No," said Ruth.

"You'll want someone to countersign," said Wren. "When you read it."

Ruth looked at her. Fifteen, in her father's old jacket, with her hands in its pockets and her chin up. She had heard everything at the ferry-office counter. She always did.

"Get your boots," said Ruth.

---

The fog came off the lake at dusk.

It came as it came every evening at the end of summer, cold out of the warm water, rolling in low across the road from the left until the lake was gone and the fields on the right were gone and there was nothing but a strip of gray tarmac and the white line down the middle of it, running away into nothing. It was weather. Ruth said so to herself once, at the start, out of habit, and then did not need to again. There had been no storms since December. Her head was her own. The fog was only fog, and it was only slow.

They walked on the white line. They did not talk. Wren kept up without being asked. Somewhere past the halfway post Ruth's left heel began to bleed into her shoe, and she let it.

She thought about four o'clock, when they had asked him nicely to stay in. She thought about seven, in the loft. She thought about the eleven minutes for the key, and the hour at the long table with a ruler, and every line she had read twice. She did not think it had been wrong to read them. She would have done it again. She would do it again tomorrow. That was the worst of it.

---

Gullhaven was dark except for Net Loft Row.

The Row was lit. Every lamp in every window, every door open, at twenty to midnight, and people standing in the narrow street between the tall houses in twos and threes, not talking, not looking at one another. The fog lay over the harbour at the end like a lid. Ruth came down the Row with Wren beside her and the books against her ribs and felt the street turn to watch her, without anyone moving.

A man she knew from the market told her, standing in his doorway with his arms folded and his face like stone. He told her as if he were reporting the weather and it had nothing to do with him.

They had met at seven. He had come to the loft, though they had asked him to stay in. He had brought the book. He had held it up in front of them and said it would show them, and someone had said he would have written it himself, and he had said he had not, and someone else had said something about November. Then they were all outside, on the slipway, in the dusk, with the fog coming in. Nobody had touched him. The man in the doorway said that twice. Nobody had laid a hand on him. Somebody had taken the book out of his hands, and somebody else had thrown it into the harbour.

He had gone in after it.

He had gone down the slipway into the black water in his clothes, after a book, in the dusk, in the fog, and they had stood on the slip and watched, because nobody had believed he would. And then they had believed it, and a boy had gone in with a rope, and two men after him, and they had found him and brought him up the slip at about twenty to eight. He had the book. He had it in both arms against his chest and they had to open his hands.

A doctor had come from up the coast at nine. He was in his house now, above the slipway, on his own bed. He had not woken properly.

"The book's ruined," said the man in the doorway. "Pulp. You can't read a line of it." He unfolded his arms. "Not that it would have proved anything."

---

Ruth climbed the steps to the house above the slipway.

The door was open. The lamp in the front room was lit, and through the doorway beyond it she could see the end of a bed, and a pair of boots on the floor beside it, and the doctor's coat over a chair. She did not go in. That was not where it was to be done.

She turned on the top step, under the lamp, with the street below her. People had come along the Row behind her and stood at the foot of the steps, looking up. More came. She did not count them. The fog was in the street now, thin, and the lamplight made rooms of it.

She took the pairs out of her coat.

"This is your book," she said. "The Row's. Copied at Kestrel in February, in our hands, and checked by two of yours, line by line, before anybody here had a reason to change a word." She heard her own voice, flat and plain, the ferry-office voice. "I'm going to read you the tenth of June."

She read it.

The drains. The gully at the end of the Row, and whose turn it was. The broken gate. The vote. And his name, once, saying he would see to the gate. And under it the countersignatures, and she read the names aloud, all of them, the two dead men among them, and then she read the line in the margin, in one dead man's hand: *Rain all afternoon. Gully overflowing again.*

Nobody spoke.

She held the book open and turned it so that the lamplight fell on the page, and the people at the foot of the steps came up them, one at a time, and looked, and went down again. They knew the hands. They knew whose initials were in the margins, from February. They knew who complained about the gully.

At the back of the crowd a woman sat down on somebody's front step, as if her legs had gone. Ruth knew who she was. Her husband had been taken from the Row in November 2107, and had come back pleasant and empty, and had died last autumn in his sleep. She sat on the step with her hands in her lap and looked at nothing.

---

Wren wrote the entry.

Ruth had meant to. She had the Kestrel Book out and the pencil in her hand, and Wren took the pencil from her, without asking, and sat down on the top step under the lamp with the book on her knees, and wrote. It was her first. She had countersigned for a year and a half, and had written in the margins, and had once written a line about a crate of seedlings. She had never written an entry of her own.

She wrote it the way the method said. Date first, then time, then place, then what happened, then who was there. Plain words.

*Monday 19 August 2109. 23:40. Net Loft Row, Gullhaven, on the steps of the house above the slipway. R. Calloway read aloud the entry for 10 June 2108 from the Kestrel pairs copy of the Row's street book, made Feb 2109 and checked by two of the Row. It is a meeting about the drains and a gate. There is no admission in it. Heard by: about forty people of the Row.*

Then a line of her own, under it, in the same plain hand.

*We came four hours late.*

She turned the book round and held it out. Ruth countersigned.

Then a man came up the steps from the street, and then a woman, and asked if they could sign it too. Two of the Row. Ruth let them, and watched them write their names under Wren's line in their own hands, and then she wrote in the margin beside their names, *They asked*, and initialled it, because a thing like that should be in the book.

---

He died in the night, at about ten to four, without waking.

The doctor came out onto the step and told her, because she was still there. She and Wren had sat on the bottom step of the house opposite since midnight, against each other, in the fog, with the books in Ruth's coat. Nobody had asked them to go. Nobody had asked them in.

---

At first light the fog lifted off the Row, slowly, the way it lifted off Harbour Street, and the street was a street again: tall narrow houses, nets drying in the open lofts, a gull on a chimney. People came out and stood on their steps. Nobody spoke to anyone.

Ruth took out her order book, the little one she carried for the coal and the paraffin, and tore a slip from the back of it. She wrote his name on it in square capitals, the way engineers write, slowly, so that every letter was plain.

There was an old man who sang the names of the dead. She had never met him. She had heard him once, on the broadcast from the plaza in March, singing two names to a verse, one from each shore, and had stood in the ferry office with a ticket half torn and not moved until he finished. The book's couriers knew where he was. This was where the names went.

She folded the slip once, and gave it to the courier who came up the Row at seven with the morning bundles, and asked him to see it carried.

It was the first death of the second war. She did not know that yet. Nobody did.
