# Epilogue: The Coast

*Friday, January 10, 2110. Chronos; Ward 9S; the farmhouse; the Commission; the Sovereignty; the Atlantic coast of Veridica*

---

In Chronos, in the morning, Julian Kovač wrote in his journal by hand.

He was recorded at maximum; every heartbeat went to the directorate; his eyes recorded the page as he made it, so that the log knew he was writing and could not read what. He wrote slowly, in the sloping hand, the capitals leaning forward together, eager, out of a habit he had not lost. He wrote the one thing no log could hold, the thing that was true precisely because it could not be kept by the machine that kept everything:

*I am seen every second and nobody sees me.*

Adrian sat beside him and did not read it. Julian let him stay. Adrian came across the lake most weeks now, by the route he never described, and sat in the family room with its camera in each corner, and was theatrical for a while and then quiet, and the quiet was the part Julian had come to need, because it was the only quiet in his life that was not also an absence. At maximum, a man is never alone and never accompanied; the directorate is always there and never there; and Adrian, who sat and said nothing and did not read the page, was the one presence in Julian's days that was a presence and not a watcher.

In the back cover of the journal, folded, was a letter, a reply, in a hand he had not seen in years, that had come across the lake by courier in December, nearly two years after he had written to ask for it. He did not take it out to read it again. He knew it by now; it was not long; it said less than he had hoped and more than he had expected, which was the shape of most things worth keeping; and it was signed *Aunt Helena*, which was the thing in it he read when he read it, more than the words. He closed the journal before the thought of the man who had used him could finish forming, the way you close a door on a cold room, and Adrian, beside him, in a room where nothing was private, pretended not to see him do it, which was the only privacy there was, and was enough.

---

At midday, on Ward 9S, Yuki Tanaka took testimony from a patient who had never been logged.

He told her where the old ferry had run, and whose shore the lakeshore had been when he was a boy, and that the old pier had stood, a hundred metres of it, with lamps. She wrote it in the twelfth notebook, dated, timed, placed, in his words, and he signed it, a shaky initial, Kestrel-style. The count from December had grown, a little; the minority pattern had not reduced; seven in a hundred had not become six. She wrote the new figure down and did not say it aloud, because the number was not the point; the point was that there was a number at all, where in October there had been none.

*Patient: [initials]. Presentation: remembers. Onset: always. Aetiology:*

She left the line unfinished. She had left a great many lines unfinished this autumn. It had stopped troubling her. There were some lines a clinician should not finish, because finishing them was a claim, and she had learned, over a long year, exactly how much a claim could cost, and that the honest thing was sometimes the unfinished line, the colon and the space after it, left open for whoever came to read it to finish or to leave.

---

In the afternoon, at the farmhouse, Elise Vance took out the page she would not give anyone.

Old Songs watched her from the stove. He did not see where she kept it; she had never shown anyone where she kept it; she took it out from wherever it was and read it, once, with her right hand holding the edge to keep it still, except that this winter the cup and the edge and the holding did not quite steady the hand any more, and the page trembled a little as she read it, which it had not done in the autumn. He saw that. He filed it where he filed things. She read the page, whatever was on it, no heading he could see and no numeral, and folded it, and put it away.

Old Songs asked nothing.

He hummed the Lakeshore Air, low, at the stove, and it lifted at the end like a question he was not asking, and she heard it, and looked at him once, and neither of them said anything, because they had said the things that needed saying in a sealed ward in January, and a man and a woman who have said those things to each other do not need to say anything else at a stove in the winter; they need only to be in the same warm room, one who built the prison and one who survived it, with a page between them that one of them would not give yet and the other would not ask for.

---

In the evening, at the Commission's testimony intake, Helena Kovač filed a letter.

She had written to Julian at last. The letter had gone by courier across the lake in December, to answer one dated the twentieth of January 2108, which she had kept in a folder and not answered for nearly two years. She did not know what she had said in it that was worth the two years; she had read it over a dozen times before she sent it, and it had seemed thin, a thing nearly two years too late; but she had sent it, because she had decided, on a Monday in November, kneeling on a cold floor in her nightdress, that she would not keep a third folder instead of acting.

She put his original letter into a new card folder, and labelled it, in block capitals, in her own hand: *ANSWERED.* She pushed her glasses up her nose. She looked at the shelf of folders she had kept over the years, instead of acting, the patients the protocols went too far with, the certifications, the family box, and she put this one at the end of the shelf, where the acting folders went, of which there was, now, exactly one.

She sat for a while at the intake counter after the staff had gone, in the empty hall with its six writing desks and its pens on chains and its steel slot in the wall through which anyone in Veridica could put anything at any hour. It was hers to keep. She had insisted on paper, on a ledger, on signing for releases herself, because paper could not be adjusted in the night so that it had always said something else; she had learned that at Ashford, in four years of keeping a folder and doing nothing with it; and she had thought, when she took the Registrar's post, that she had learned the lesson. She saw now that she had learned only half of it. Keeping the record on paper was the easy half. The hard half was acting on it while there was still time, and she had failed the hard half with Julian's letter for nearly two years, and a boy was at maximum across the lake who need not have been if she had read her own cupboard instead of the roll. She had answered the letter now. It was late. It was not nothing. A folder marked *ANSWERED* was a different thing from a folder marked nothing, and the difference was the whole of what she had to show for the year, and she found, pushing her glasses up, that it was enough to go on with, which was all she had ever asked of anything.

---

In the Sovereignty, a letter came for Isaiah from the asylum office.

His review had been *brought forward*. He read the phrase and admired its precision, as he had once admired *conditional*, and with the same diminishing pleasure, because he knew now what the precision was for. He filed the letter in the study, with the date. He did not write a question under it, because he knew the answer; a man whose column had been printed uncut and quoted in a war brief, and who had then begun to ask, in the one channel he trusted, who wanted a war, was a man whose conditional asylum was about to be reviewed, and the review would find the condition unmet, and he had known the terms when he took them, and had taken them anyway, because they had been the only terms on offer to a man with no state.

His coat hung by the door, with the lining still sewn, copy VII and his notes inside it. He did not take it down. He sat in the parlour of the memory palace for a while, among the true warm things, and did not look at the woman at the head of the table, and let the fire be the only thing he watched.

---

On the Atlantic coast of Veridica, at dusk, four people stood on a winter headland.

It was Veridica's own coast, the former East Coast, gray sea and a hard wind off it and salt on the glasses Marcus kept wiping. It was real weather, all of it, cold and ordinary and true, the one kind of truth no nation had ever learned to write. Sokolov had brought them out to see the ground, because the lakeshore was not where this war would come, and a commissioner who was going to understand the next war had better stand on the beach it would come to, in the wind, and see how a door is not a wall.

Marcus, Kira, Dmitri, Sokolov. Alexei was at the farmhouse; Dmitri noted his absence once, in the way he counted people, and did not say it, and did not know, could not know, that it was the last winter all of them would be alive to be counted. Kira stood close to Marcus, not touching. Sokolov gave them the coastline in a report's register: the landing beaches, where a fleet would come ashore if it meant to; the roads inland, which you held because you could not hold the beach; the distance to the capital, which was the number that mattered. She gave it flatly, and did not interpret it, and let them interpret it, because interpreting was their work now, not hers.

Sokolov walked them along the headland and showed them the ground the way she showed everything, by naming what she noticed and interpreting none of it. Here the beach shelved steep, so a landing craft could come close; there it shelved shallow, so it could not. The road inland ran through a cut in the cliff, which was where you held, because you could not hold the beach. The distance to the capital was a number she gave twice, once in kilometres and once in the hours a column would need, and the second number was the one that mattered, and she let them work out why. She had drafted a coastal plan nobody had asked her for. She did not say so. Dmitri, who had served under her for four years, watched her point along the sand and thought, Marcus saw him think it, that she was showing them a thing she had already decided and would not say she had decided, which was her whole way, and had been since a checkpoint in the fog three winters ago when she had cleared a barrier and turned her face away.

Beyond the horizon, somewhere, across a great deal of water, was a fleet of people no broadcast could reach. No storm would turn them. No seeding would touch them. Every weapon the four of them on the headland knew how to use, the record, the storm, the fact, the doctrine, the many hands, the whole of what two books had been about, was a weapon against people with something behind their ears, and the people coming had nothing behind their ears, and would come, Marcus thought, wiping the salt from his glasses, into a war that could not be fought with any truth at all, only with blood.

"No storm will turn them," said Dmitri, who had spent four years running storms and the two since being officially dead because of them.

"No," said Sokolov.

Marcus thought of the clean sheet on his kitchen table, and the new heading, and the one question under it that he had not answered and would not answer tonight. *Who is invading whom?* He thought of Isaiah's line, in his pocket still, folded. *Someone in Veridica wants to be invaded.* He thought that he had spent a year learning, at a cost he could not yet count, that the record was not the truth and the witness was not safe and the restorer was the one the design chose first, and that none of what he had learned would turn a single soldier on a single beach, because the soldiers were coming to fight a war his kind of people did not know how to fight, with a weapon his kind of people had forgotten the look of.

The light went. The sea went gray to black. The wind came up off it, cold and real and true, and nobody had written it, and nobody could.

Marcus turned up his collar, and said it to the three of them and to the sea, because it was the honest end of the year, and because a thing said on a headland in the wind at dusk is at least a thing said where the record cannot reach it.

"We won the Battle of Truths," said Marcus Chen. "Now comes the Battle of Blood."
