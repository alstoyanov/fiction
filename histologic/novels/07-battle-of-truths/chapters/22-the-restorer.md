# Chapter 22: The Restorer

*Friday, November 15 – Monday, November 18, 2109. The Sovereignty of Free Will*

---

Julian Kovač's disclosure reached the Sovereignty on Friday evening, and the SFW press ran it before Isaiah had read a line.

It came across the world the way things came, on the long relays, and by the time it reached Isaiah's screen the paper's own translators were already on it, and the evening edition carried the first extracts, and the headline over them said that a Chronos official had exposed a Veridican plot with his own record. It was three hundred and eighteen hours of one man's life in a friend's company, published by the man himself, timestamped to the nanosecond, uncut.

Isaiah did not read the extracts. He read the thing itself, over three nights, in order, the way he read anything that mattered, and he filed it in the study by date, as he filed everything: the Lakes Talks in the winter; the garden in August; the Archive steps in September, *I hoped it would be you*; the access given on the eighteenth of October; the evenings in the flat; and the heart rate, rising fourteen seconds after a door opened, every morning, for six weeks, which Julian had published along with the rest, because Julian had published all of it, because cutting was the thing that had been done to him.

What the log showed was what Isaiah had been taught to see in Wing C, in a building where seven people had been emptied and one of them had been given a woman to grow attached to so that a man with a clipboard could watch what grew. Protocol R-2. An attachment figure placed in front of a lonely mind. He had seen it done. He had been three cells along; he had made a census of the seven and turned them into a question; and he had understood, before most of them, what the pairing across the atrium was, line of sight, privileges synchronised, a thing grown on purpose in people who had been hollowed out so that something could be grown in them.

He had written about it, once, in the article that had got him out, and he had withheld one thing from that article, the one time in his life he had edited the truth: he had not named the pairing, had not written that the believer and the architect had been placed in front of each other on purpose, because he had decided, alone, with the archive going out through a channel and a man with a clipboard tied up on the floor behind him, that two people deserved to not have that printed about the thing between them. It was the only time he had put his own judgement above the record, and he had never been sure it was right, and he was a little less sure every year, because he had learned since what it cost to decide, even once, even kindly, what another person was allowed to have known about themselves.

He did not withhold anything now. He read the log, and he saw R-2 turned outward, performed by the one man in the world who knew exactly what it did, because it had been done to him, and he did not edit it, because this time the man it had been done to had published it himself, uncut, and the withholding was not Isaiah's to do, and a man who has learned the cost of deciding for others does not decide for a man who has already decided for himself.

---

What he did not know, and was careful not to claim, he kept in a separate place.

He did not know the operation's name. It was not in Julian's log; Julian had never heard it; the log held three hundred and eighteen hours of a friendship and not one word of what the friendship was for, because Julian had not known what it was for until it was over. Isaiah did not know who had built the rest of the thing, the part that was not Marcus, the part that had taken a narrow restoration and turned it into a nation's new past. He did not know what Chronos had believed on Friday morning compared with Saturday. He knew only what the log showed and what Chronos had published: a night run in the Archive, a nation that now agreed with Veridica, a key-holder at maximum. He wrote all of that in the study, and beside it he wrote the things he did not know, and he did not let the one list leak into the other.

---

He had Principle One and Principle Two.

Kira had sent them on the thirtieth of September, copy VII, pp. 5–12, and he had not published them, and his prints of her photographs were sewn into the lining of his coat, where his notes had been sewn since Wing C. *Let there be enemies outside them all.* If he put that page beside this column, "The Restorer" would stop being a column about one man's betrayal of another and become a thesis about a design, and it would be a better column, and a truer one, and it would be the wrong column, because a piece about what one man did to another should not be carried on the back of a page about what the world was built to do. The two were both true and they were not the same size, and you did not make a man's betrayal into an illustration of a theory, not when the man was in a cell at maximum having published his own heartbeat to the world. Isaiah left the pages in the lining.

---

Monday, the eighteenth, six o'clock in the morning, in the parlour of the memory palace, he wrote the column he had to write.

He wrote the headline where he always wrote headlines, on the lace runner, among the true warm things, and it came almost at once, because it was the only honest one, and because it was a word he had used before.

*THE RESTORER.*

It was the Warning's word. He had published the Warning last December, the handwritten page, *reformers into its own restorers*, and he used the word now deliberately, because it was the word the design itself used for the thing Marcus had become. He did not know, as he used it, that it was also, nearly, the name of the operation; the echo was his own coinage, out of the Warning, and he would not learn for a month that there was a folder across the lake with the word on its cover.

He wrote the column in his editorial voice, every unnecessary word struck.

The man who had refused, in Book-closed secret, to weaponise a lonely man's trust, in a garden where that man's own log would keep the refusal, had now done the thing he refused. *In one night, Veridica wrote its history into a hundred and fifty million people, and called it a restoration.* He quoted Marcus against himself, the line from the Reply, from February, that Marcus had written in his own hand: *Trust no restorer, including us.* And he ended, as he ended everything, on a question he admitted he could not answer:

*He told us not to trust him. Why is it that we only believe a man when he stops meaning it?*

At the head of the parlour table Aunt Sarah watched him write it, as she had watched him write everything, and said nothing, and he did not look up at her, because he could not, that morning, bear to be watched even by a thing in his own head.

---

He filed it at four minutes past ten, and he knew what came next, because he had known it for three years.

In the Sovereignty, a column of his was held for a day. Then a call came from the managing editor, cheerful, with the reading glasses pushed up into the white hair, and three paragraphs were cut, for tone, for space, for the house's sense of what its readers wanted, which was the Histologic world condemned but not explained, the no without the argument. It had happened to the Doctrine column, which they had spiked entirely. It had happened to every column since, a little, the last paragraph, the question at the end, the thing that made it his.

At fifty-one minutes past ten, "The Restorer" ran.

It ran uncut, within the hour, on the front of the afternoon edition, with his byline larger than it had ever been. There had been no call. The managing editor passed his desk, jingling something in his pocket, and said, "Important work," and said it the way people say *useful*, and went on. In three years this had never happened. Not once. His columns were improved, in the house's view, by being shortened; the last paragraph went, the question at the end went, the thing that made it his; it had happened to the Doctrine piece, which they had spiked whole, and to every column since, a sentence at a time, so steadily that he had stopped expecting otherwise and started writing the important thing into the middle where it would survive. This one had been improved by being run whole and fast and large, which meant that this one said what the house wanted said, exactly, at the length the house wanted it said, so that nothing of it was cut, because there was nothing in it the house did not want cut, which was a different thing from nothing being wrong with it.

Isaiah sat very still at his desk with his hands folded.

He thought about the shape of it. A man's column is cut when it says more than the house wants; it is run whole when it says exactly what the house wants; and he had written, this morning, a column condemning a friend, and the house had run it whole, which meant the house wanted a friend condemned, at length, now, with a byline on it that the readers trusted. He had not written it for the house. He had written it because it was true and he could not not write it; Julian had published the log and the log showed what Marcus had done and a witness does not look away from what the record shows, not when the record, for once, is a man's own honest account of his own ruin. But the house had taken his true thing and made it theirs, by running it whole, and he could not tell, sitting at his desk with his hands folded, whether a true thing that is run whole because it serves someone's war is still only a true thing, or whether it has become something else in their hands, and he filed the question, because he did not know, and a man should not pretend to know a thing he does not.

---

By evening it was everywhere.

Reprinted across the Sovereignty, translated for Synthesis, quoted on the broadcasts. People he did not know stopped him in the corridor to thank him, to grip his hand, to tell him it was the truest thing anyone had written about the Histologic world. His asylum was still conditional, and for the first time in three years he found himself wondering, precisely, what the condition was, and admiring the word *conditional* a great deal less than he had in December.

At half past seven he sat in his rooms over the shop that sold notebooks, with the radio on, and the SFW Council's evening session came on, a debate on the Council's posture towards Veridica.

A member he did not know rose and read a sentence aloud to the chamber, as evidence, as grounds.

*In one night, Veridica wrote its history into a hundred and fifty million people, and called it a restoration.*

His sentence. The member stopped there. The member did not read the question that had followed it in the column, the question Isaiah had ended on, *why is it that we only believe a man when he stops meaning it*, because the question was the part of it that was his, the part that admitted it did not know, and a war brief has no use for a sentence that admits it does not know. The chamber applauded.

He listened to the whole of the debate with his hands folded in his lap and the radio turned low. He had spent twenty years listening to how people said words, and he listened now to how the chamber said *Veridica*, and it was not the way people say the name of a rival, or even of an enemy they fear; it was the way people say the name of a thing that has already been decided on, a thing agreed, a conclusion for which the evidence is now being read into the record so that the record will show that there was evidence. The member who read Isaiah's sentence did not read it to persuade the chamber. The chamber was already persuaded. He read it to furnish the decision with a reason, and Isaiah's name was the reason's signature, a Veridican, a known witness, a man the readers trusted, saying the thing in words the chamber could repeat.

Isaiah sat with his hands folded and listened to his own words come back to him with the question cut off, in a chamber debating a posture that he could hear, in the way the members said *Veridica*, was no longer a posture, and he understood, exactly, without knowing whose it was, that he had become a witness for somebody's case.

He did not know whose. He wrote that in the study, late, on a card, under the column and the number and the line he could read and could not read: *A witness for whom?* And he did not cross it out, because he did not know, and because a man who does not know a thing should leave the question standing where he can see it, until it tells him.
