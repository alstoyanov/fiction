# Chapter 14: The Image

*Monday, October 7 – Monday, October 14, 2109. A sub-level room of the Commission building; Historian's Lane; the flat by the old river*

---

*Oct 7, 2109. 08:00. Sub-level two, Commission building. Personnel: Osman, K.; Volkov, N.; Reeves, M. Objective: a narrow restoration image. Scope: two record sets, one revocation. Nothing else.*

The room had no windows. It had been an archive store once, on sub-level two, and it still smelled of the dust of paper that had been taken somewhere else, and it had a steel door and a lock that only the three of them had the key to, and Kira had chosen it for all of those reasons. If she was going to build the thing, she was going to build it where she could put a wall round it.

Wei brought the tools down himself, in a case, in his right hand, kind and unhurried.

"They're yours, Kira," he said, setting the case on the bench. "Change whatever you like. If there's a line in there you don't trust, cut it. I'd rather the whole thing were yours than that one line of mine went across the lake unread." He did not sit. He never sat in a room he had not been asked to stay in. He put the case down and inclined his head and went back up, and left her with it, which was exactly what she had asked for and felt, obscurely, like being handed a loaded thing by a man who wanted to watch what she did with it.

She opened the case. The manifest on the top sheet was headed, in plain capitals, *RESTORATION*. She had seen the word before, on a folder label Kira had carried out of a data centre at Ashford in 2106 and never been able to explain, *RESTORATION / XI*, and she filed the manifest and did not say anything, because Nikolai and Maya were in the room and there was no good in saying it aloud.

---

The design was narrow. She had made it narrow; it was the one thing that was hers.

Two halves. The first took away the Archive key's standing to sign anything in the true source, so that the key that had countersigned the Gap and the Rewriting could never be used again, by anyone, Julian or whoever had stood behind Julian. The second put back Chronos's own records of the two attacks, the Rewriting and the Gap, as they had actually happened, from Chronos's side, so that Chronos's Judge would read the truth and the storms would stop. Nothing else. She wrote the scope list on a card and pinned it above the bench: *two record sets; one revocation.* She built a hard stop into the thing, so that if the count of what it touched ever went over the card, it would halt itself and wait for her. She built a branch for the one moment she was most afraid of, the moment when Chronos's own backup, offered a foreign nation's credentials, would do what any honest system should do and refuse them, and demand its own key, and stop. She wrote the refusal branch herself, by hand, so that when it came she would be ready to meet it.

She worked for a week. She worked the way she had always worked, which was to treat the thing in front of her as a system under attack and herself as the attacker, to assume the worst about every part of it and make it prove otherwise; it was the discipline that had built the transmission-layer standards of the Spire, and it was the discipline she now turned on a thing she herself was building, because she trusted her own hand least of all. And all week the tools fit Chronos's architecture the way a key fits a lock that was cut for it.

That was the first wrong thing. It was not wrong in any way she could point to; it was wrong the way a thing is wrong when it is too easy. Wei's tooling, which he had said would need adapting to Chronos, needed almost no adapting at all. The two nations had spent forty years telling each other their systems were nothing alike, incompatible, built on opposite principles, Chronos's obsessive and continuous, Veridica's spare and verified; it was a thing everyone knew, a thing the war had been partly about; and the tooling slid into the Chronos architecture as though the opposite were true, as though underneath the forty years of difference the two were the same machine wearing two coats. Her refusal branch, the careful thing she had written for the moment Chronos would say no, sat there with nothing running through it, because in every rehearsal Chronos did not say no. She rehearsed it a dozen times, the moment a Chronos store should challenge a foreign nation's credentials and demand its own, and a dozen times the challenge did not come, and she told herself a dozen times that she had built the rehearsal wrong, because the alternative was a thing she was not ready to think.

"Either someone sloppy built this," said Maya, on the third day, timestamping, not looking up, "or someone who never needed the refusal. One of those."

Kira built the refusal branch again anyway, stronger, because a thing that had never been used was a thing she trusted least.

---

On the fifth day she found the time.

The tooling had a scheduler, a thing that set the hour at which an operation would run, and it had a default hour already in it, sitting in the field as a suggestion, the way a form comes with a date already filled in. The default was 03:12.

She sat very still and looked at it.

Twelve minutes past three. The minute the Gap had begun. The minute the Rewriting had begun. The minute two million journals had started to redraw themselves while a widow at Kestrel Point watched her own page come down like a blind. It was in the tooling as a default, a suggestion, the hour the thing expected to be run, the way a thing expects the hour it has been run before.

She changed it to 02:40. She did it without a word, and did not explain, and Maya, across the bench, timestamped the change without being asked, *14:06:22, scheduler default altered*, and did not ask why either. Nobody in that room needed to be told why the number 03:12 should not be in a thing they were building. They needed, all three, not to say it, because saying it led somewhere none of them had the stomach to go on a Wednesday afternoon with the thing half built.

---

Nikolai mapped the Chronos store against the Veridican one, which he knew from the inside, because he had been inside it, as Pieter Lang, on Wei's team, two winters ago.

He did it quietly, over two days, with three copies of every stage of his work, because he kept three copies of everything. On the Thursday he sat back from it and said, uneasily, as a figure of speech, not as a finding:

"Their backup structure is like looking in a mirror."

He said it the way you say a thing that unsettles you and that you do not want to have meant. He had built the February match. He had put the key in Julian's name. He kept three copies of every build and said, for the first time Kira had ever heard him say it, that he wished he didn't.

Kira looked at the two structures side by side on his screen, the Veridican and the Chronos, and saw what he meant, and did not say what she thought, which was that a mirror is not two things that resemble each other, a mirror is one thing and its reflection, and that a reflection is not independent of the thing it reflects. She did not say it. She wrote *mirror* in her notebook, under *too clean*, under *03:12*, and underlined none of them, because to underline them would have been to admit she had a list, and she was not ready to admit she had a list.

Maya said the last of it on the Friday, holding a component up to the light, turning it over the way she turned everything over.

"Tools this good," she said, "are built for the job they're used for. My father's cradles were like this. Beautiful. Nothing wasted. You don't build a thing this clean to leave it in a drawer." She put it down. "Somebody built this to do exactly what we're about to do with it. Before we thought of doing it."

Kira narrowed the image again, after that. She took out everything that was not the two record sets and the one revocation, and she made the scope list shorter, and the hard stop tighter, and she told herself that if the knife was narrow enough it would not matter who had forged the blade.

It was the reasoning of a person who still believed the thing could be narrow. She would recognise that, afterwards, as the error under all the others: not that she had been careless, she had been the opposite of careless, but that she had spent the week making the knife as fine as a knife could be made, and had never once asked whether the thing she meant to cut was the kind of thing a knife could touch. She had assumed a record was a record, a page you could change one line of without changing the next. She had built the verification layer of the Spire; she knew records; she knew they came apart into lines. She did not know, that week, in a windowless room on sub-level two, that the thing on the far side of the lake did not come apart into lines, that it was one piece wearing the look of two, and that a narrow cut in a thing that is all one piece is not a narrow cut at all. She would hear, before the operation was over, exactly how much the narrowness was worth, which was nothing, and she would carry the nothing for the rest of her life, because she had held the knife, as she had said she would, so that it would only cut what it should, and it had cut everything, and the holding had been the proof of her care and the measure of how little her care could buy.

---

On Monday the fourteenth, at half past nine at night, she went to Historian's Lane, and Adrian was there.

She had not arranged it. She had gone to the Lane because she went there sometimes, to the place where a block of stone had come loose above a doorway four years ago and a man had pulled her clear of it, and where, two weeks later, she had stopped being someone who believed the system. She went there the way you go back to the scene of the most important thing that has happened to you, not often, and never on purpose, and always on purpose.

He was under the lamp, where the loose stone had been. He had the scar above his left eyebrow and the blue eyes, the Kovač eyes, and he was older, and he did not come towards her and he did not touch her. It was the first time she had seen him since December 2105, since the window, since the night she had walked to the Enforcement Tower and got her ninety-two per cent and let herself be taken rather than run.

"Kira," he said.

"Adrian."

"I had dinner with my brother," he said. "Three weeks ago. Across the lake. First time in eleven years." He said it steadily. "He's happy, Kira. He's happier than I've seen him since we were boys. There's a man he trusts." He looked at her. "Whatever your Commissioner is doing to my brother, I did to you. I know exactly what it looks like from where I'm standing, because I stood where he's standing. Don't let him finish it."

Kira did not say anything quotable. She had learned, over four years, to say nothing quotable when she most wanted to say everything, because everything she wanted to say was in a drawer with 7.3 and a bridge and a warehouse, and if she opened the drawer she would not get it shut again before the operation, and she needed it shut.

Adrian looked at her for a moment longer, and then he nodded, once, and went, back up the Lane into the dark, the way he had always gone.

---

At the flat Marcus was asleep over his lists at the kitchen table, his glasses pushed up on his forehead, the pencil fallen from his hand.

Kira stood in the doorway and looked at him. Then she went to the shelf and took down her journal, and sat at the other end of the table, and wrote, in her own hand, because a thing that was written down could at least be looked at later and not denied:

*Oct 14, 2109. 21:30. Historian's Lane. Adrian. "Whatever your Commissioner is doing to my brother, I did to you. Don't let him finish it."*

She looked at it. Then she closed the journal, and did not wake Marcus, and did not tell him, that night or any night, that his lover's brother had stood under a lamp and warned her, because she had decided, somewhere on the Lane, that it was hers to carry and not his, the way Adrian was hers to carry and not his, the way the drawer was hers.

She went to bed, and lay in the dark, and the specifications started up in her, *layer one, key exchange, rotation interval ninety seconds*, and she let them, because for once the people she loved were not what the reciting was hiding. For once it was hiding the thing she had just built, in a windowless room, with her own hands, that was too clean, and fit too well, and defaulted to 03:12, and looked, Nikolai had said, like a mirror.

She counted the specifications until she slept.
