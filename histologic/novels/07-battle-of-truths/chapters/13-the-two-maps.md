# Chapter 13: The Two Maps

*Friday, October 4 – Saturday, October 5, 2109. The farmhouse, the border zone*

---

The Joint Inquiry did not sit on Fridays.

It sat Monday to Thursday, in the reading rooms of the Chronos Archive, like the Lakes Talks before it, so that the delegations could cross home for the weekend and the Synthesis secretariat could have its Fridays. That was why Marcus could be at the farmhouse on a Friday afternoon, and why he came, in his good coat, with an Archive envelope in his hand and a look on his face that Mikhail had learned to read since the morning in August when the boy had stood at this stove and heard the falling phrase and gone anyway.

"From the Archive," said Marcus, holding out the envelope. "It's for you."

Then he went out to the woodpile to split wood, because he could not stay in the room. He had not met the old man's eyes since August. Mikhail let him go. There was a kind of courtesy in letting a man do a thing with his hands when he cannot do the thing with his face, and the wood did need splitting.

Mikhail took the envelope to the bench by the stove and sat down with it, and did not open it at once.

He had learned that from Kerr, sixty years ago, in a reading room with a green-shaded lamp and the smell of floor polish and old paper: you read the object before you read the words. Weight. Paper. The hand that had closed it. A thing told you half of what it was before you ever broke its seal, and the half it told you was the half nobody had meant you to have, which was usually the true half. He had read ten thousand objects before he read a single word of one, and he read this one now, turning it in his gnarled fingers in the firelight, an old man taking his time because taking his time was nearly the only freedom a man kept at seventy-one.

Heavy Chronos paper, good, the kind that lasts a century because the people who made it had decided a century was the least a record deserved. And clipped to the front, a card, in a sloping hand he had seen once before, on a card in the Reply that had come across the lake in January: *For Chronos: Record less. Remember more.* The capitals leaned forward together, eager, as if each wanted to reach the next word first.

*For the old gentleman who sings. I was told of your wife and your daughter. I found this in our holdings and thought it was yours to have. I am so sorry.*

It was not signed.

He put the card down on his knee and opened the envelope.

---

Inside were two sheets.

The first was an extract from the Chronos Archive's sealed operational series, copied out in the same careful hand, with a note that it was copied faithfully from the original. A single line of record, the kind of line Mikhail had catalogued ten thousand of before he ever read one for its meaning.

*Mon 11 Jun 2074. Lakeshore sector, far shore. Calibration run 14. Coordinated with V. Border Broadcast Division. Onset 15:00:00. Duration 41 min. Outcome: recorded.*

The second sheet was the Archive's mirror of the Veridican death notices for the twelfth of June, 2074, the day after: a town on the Veridican lakeshore, a short list of names, copied in the same hand. Two of the names were:

*PETROV, A.*

*PETROV, K.*

---

He read it twice.

He read it the second time slowly, the way you read a thing when the first reading has gone through you and left no mark, because it was too large to take a mark all at once. *Onset 15:00:00.* On the hour, to the second, the way the manufactured ones came, the way they had always come, with no half-hour of warning the way the old natural storms of his boyhood had come. He remembered the natural ones; he was old enough to remember the world before the record, which was the whole of why they had corrected him so many times and never emptied him; and the natural ones had announced themselves, a pressure that built across half an afternoon, so that a mother had time to call her children in off the shore. This one had come on the hour. A child on a shore at three o'clock in the afternoon of a June day, with no warning at all, because the thing that made it had a clock and kept to it.

At three o'clock on the afternoon of the eleventh of June, 2074, he had been in a schoolroom on the lakeshore, marking copybooks, his pen going down the margins in the red ink the school gave its teachers, correcting sums. He had never known where they were when it came. He had only known where they were found: Anya on the shingle below the ferry steps, and Katya a little way along, where the water came up over the stones. He did not let himself see the finding, even now; he had spent forty years not seeing it; he had a door for it and he kept the door shut. Katya had been seven. She had sung the ferry verse wrong all her short life, the same wrong note in the third line, *lanterns are low on the pier*, flattening the word *low* where it should have lifted, and he had never once corrected her, because it was hers, and a thing a child does wrong and loves is a thing you leave her, and he had been right about that, and it was the only thing in the whole of it he had been right about.

*Calibration run 14.* He understood the words. He had understood Principle Five since last November, when the pages had come to this table: *border regions of broadcast overlap should be preserved, not resolved; they are where a population's tolerance for contradiction may be measured, and raised.* He had understood that the border had been kept open on purpose, as a place to measure what a mind could bear, and to raise it. He had not understood, until this sheet, that his wife and his daughter had been in the measuring. *Run 14* meant there had been thirteen before. *Coordinated with V. Border Broadcast Division* meant that both shores had kept the timetable, that the two nations which told their peoples opposite histories had sat down together and agreed the hour of a storm, and that somewhere there was a schedule, in two hands, with a date on it, and his family in the column marked *outcome*.

He did not think in numbered principles. Eleven days ago Dmitri had read *agreeing in substance* aloud at this table, formally, without contractions, and Kira had looked at the word for a long time, and Mikhail had watched them look at it. He thought now, sitting on the bench by the stove with the two sheets on his knee, in the only words he had ever had for it:

*Two maps. One river. The same hand in the corner of both.*

---

He picked up his cup.

It was his own, the brown-glazed morning cup, the one he had kept beside the stove for three summers because the handle sat right in his hand. He picked it up, and he broke it against the iron of the stove, below the cracked door, in one motion, hard, so that it went in pieces to the hearth.

That was all. He said no words. He had sung the dead for forty years and he had no words for this, and he knew better than to reach for any, because words for a thing this size came out as sermon, and he had never once in his life been able to bear a sermon, his own least of all.

In the yard the axe stopped. Then, after a moment, it started again, which was Marcus deciding not to come in, which was the right thing, and the kind thing, and Mikhail was obscurely grateful to him for it and did not want to see him.

Kira had come to the doorway. She stayed in it. She did not come in and she did not go away. She stood the way she had once stood at a glass wall, counting, and she let him sit with the pieces of the cup on the hearth and said nothing.

After a while Elise came through from the back room.

She did not ask what had happened. She had heard the cup, and she had seen the two sheets on his knee, and Elise Vance did not need a thing explained to her twice, or once, if she had the paper. She sat down on the bench beside him, close, and she picked up one of the broken pieces of the cup from the hearth and held it in her right hand, so that the hand, which trembled, had something to hold and was still. She did not apologise. He was glad of that, more glad than he could have said. An apology would have been a thing he had to answer, and he had nothing to answer with, and she knew it, because she had designed the border those storms fell on and she had never once, in all the months at this farmhouse, offered him an apology for any of it, because she understood that some debts are not paid by the word *sorry* and that offering it only makes the creditor do the work of refusing it.

They sat inside the silence until the stove burned down to red. Kira stayed in the doorway a while and then went, quietly, and the kitchen emptied around the two of them without his noticing it empty. He did not hum. Elise did not speak. She had never been a woman who filled a silence; it was one of the things he had loved in her across a reading-room table, that she could sit inside a quiet for an hour and then say one exact thing; and she sat inside this one with him and said nothing at all, which was the exact thing, this time.

He thought, while they sat, about the word *coordinated*. He turned it over the way he turned an object. It was a committee's word, a word for minutes and agendas, for two parties sitting down on opposite sides of a lake and settling a time that suited them both. *Coordinated with V. Border Broadcast Division.* Somewhere there had been a meeting. Somewhere there was, or had been, a paper with the eleventh of June on it and a time, fifteen hundred hours, and two sets of initials, and his wife and his daughter had been, to that paper, the word in the last column. *Outcome: recorded.* Not *killed*. Not *lost*. *Recorded*, because to the people who made the paper the deaths were not the point; the point was the measurement, how much a mind could bear, raised a little, run by run; and Anya and Katya had been instruments of the measuring, the way a thermometer is an instrument, the way he himself had been an instrument for forty years, humming in correction rooms so that the songs would not depend on one head. He had always thought of the storms as weather that men had learned to make. He understood now that they were not weather at all. They were arithmetic.

That night, for the first time in thirty-five years, he did not sing "The Names", not even silently, for Anya and Katya and Kerr, because he could not put his wife and his daughter into a song on the night he had learned the hour of their deaths had been set by a committee. He lay in the dark in the little room off the kitchen and listened to the house settle, and did not sing, and found that the not-singing was a kind of mourning he had not done in thirty-five years, because for thirty-five years the singing had stood in the place where the mourning should have been, and tonight the singing would not come and the mourning came instead, bare, with no tune to carry it, and he let it.

---

Saturday, first light, at the woodpile.

It came the way the tunes had always come, out of his hands as much as his head, while he was carrying an armful of split logs across the frosty yard to the kitchen door. He stopped in the middle of the yard with the logs against his chest, and it was there, low, going up by steps.

Here, on this side of the lake, where he was free and on his own shore, it came with words.

*Two maps of the one river,*
*one for either shore;*
*one puts the ford by the willows,*
*one by the mill-house door.*

He stopped there. The last line lifted at the end, like a question, the way all his tunes lifted. He stood in the cold yard and sang the four lines again, and they held; they were right; they would carry. There was more coming, he could feel it, two more verses standing behind the first the way the far poplars stood behind the near ones in the fog, but they were not ready, and he did not force them. A song that was forced came out as sermon. He let the first verse be the whole of it for now.

---

Kira brought him tea at the woodpile, two cups, and he noted that neither of them was his, because his was in pieces on the hearth, and that she had noticed too, and had not said anything about it, which was the right thing.

She told him plainly, standing in the cold with the cups, where the envelope had come from.

"Julian Kovač," she said. "The man from the marsh road in January, who drove you to the ninth floor. Marcus told him, over lunch, about an old man who sang for a wife and a daughter lost in a lakeshore storm in '74. And Julian went and looked in the Archive's sealed series, on his own time, and found it, and copied it out by hand, and sent it to you. Through Marcus. Because he thought it was yours to have."

Mikhail took the tea.

He had known it was Julian. He had known from the hand on the card, the forward-leaning capitals, the same hand that had written one line in the Reply. He had only not let himself finish the knowing until Kira finished it for him.

He knew, now, two things at once, and he held them the way the people in Kestrel Point held their two piers. Julian Kovač had given him his wife's name and his daughter's name, a gift he had wanted for forty years and never had, the hour of their deaths and the fact of the schedule, out of nothing but kindness, on his own time, from across a lake, for a man he had never met. That was one.

And the boy across the yard, stacking split wood very neatly because he could not come into the kitchen, was that same month using Julian's kindness, learning at this table how to be the thing Julian was missing, so that he could walk into the Archive on Julian's trust and cut a hand out of a nation and end the man who had sent the gift. That was the other.

The gift had been paid for with Mikhail's own grief. Marcus had spent Anya and Katya as conversation, over lunch, to be the thing Julian was missing, and Julian had answered the conversation with the one kindness that would open Mikhail's heart, and had sealed, by the kindness, his own ruin.

---

Across the yard Marcus was stacking the wood.

He stacked it very neatly, bark side out, the way a careful man stacks who does not want to look up. The frost was going off the woodpile where the sun reached it.

Mikhail looked at him for a long moment, across the yard, with the tea in his hands.

He did not sing. He did not hum the Lakeshore Air, rising at the end, which would have been *I see you*. He did not hum the falling phrase, which would have been *no*. He hummed nothing at all. He stood in the cold with two cups of tea that were not his, and looked at the boy who had been the one of them who checked, the one the system had chosen because he checked, and he made no sound, and he let Marcus feel the no-sound across the yard, which was worse than either the yes or the no, because the yes and the no were things you said to a person, and the nothing was the thing you did when there was a person-shaped space where a person used to be and you were waiting to see whether they came back into it.

Marcus's hands slowed on the wood. He did not look up. He knew. Mikhail watched him know, and drank the tea that was not his, and went in.
