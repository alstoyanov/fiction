# Chapter 10: The Brother

*Thursday, September 19 – Friday, September 20, 2109. The Temporal Archive and Julian's flat, the Chronos lakeshore city*

---

Julian Kovač woke at 06:11:52 and knew it to the second, because he always did.

He lay still with his eyes closed and let the night come up in the corner of his sight where the log waited. The lamp off at 23:52. Asleep by midnight, more or less; the log would tell him exactly, if he scrolled, and he did not scroll. He had slept badly since August. The log knew how badly. Everyone who had operational access to his log knew how badly, which since the Rewriting was a great many people, and the knowledge sat on him in the mornings like a hand on the back of the neck.

He read, as he read most mornings, the one entry he knew by heart without scrolling to it.

*13 December 2107. 03:12:00 – 03:29:00. Asleep. Heart rate steady.*

He had looked at it a hundred times. Everyone in the province had, in the week after the Gap; four million people scrolling back to the same seventeen minutes to find the place where their record had gone quiet. His said what everyone's said. He had slept through it, in this bed, breathing, while somewhere someone took seventeen minutes out of the world. It proved where his body had been. He had thought, every time he looked at it, with a small shame he did not examine, that it did not prove where his key had been, because his key was not in his bed, it was in a cabinet in the Archive, and a thing in a cabinet does not have a log.

He did not look at it this morning. Temporal Defence had questioned him twice about the key since August. He had given them two days of its use. He was tired of the key. He got up and made tea and went to work.

---

The old man's habits were still in the building.

Julian had never met the old man. Dr. Augustin Mera had retired the January before Julian took the office, and Julian had come to a department that still ran on Mera's habits the way a house runs on the habits of whoever built it. There was a chipped cup on the shelf above the reconciliation desk, a plain brown cup with a bite out of its rim, that nobody used and nobody moved; the junior staff said it had been Mera's, and that moving it was bad luck, and Julian, who did not believe in luck, left it where it was because it would have felt like presumption to touch it. The sealed boxes in the lower holdings were shelved fore-edge out, labels turned to the wall, which was not how anyone was taught to shelve and was how Mera had always done it, so that you could not read a box's label without pulling it, which the juniors said was Mera's way of making you handle a thing before you judged it. The lamp at the end reading desk was left burning at night. Julian had asked, his first week, whether it should be turned off, and had been told, gently, that it was always left on, that Mera had always left it on, and he had let it burn.

He noticed the three of them this morning, the cup and the shelving and the lamp, the way you notice the furniture of a room you have lived in for a year and a half. He thought about none of them. They were the old man's, and the old man was gone, retired to the coast and then dead within a month of it, as old men were, and the department kept his habits the way departments keep the habits of the dead, out of a kind of courtesy. He had never met Mera. He had come to the Archive and found the office already shaped by a man who had just left it, the keys handed over in a cold week, the cup on the shelf, the boxes turned to the wall; he had stepped into a life still warm from someone else, the way you step into a coat someone has just taken off; and he had never thought it strange, because a man who inherits an office inherits its habits, and the habits had become his so gradually that he could no longer have said which were Mera's and which his own. He shelved a box fore-edge out without thinking about it, which was Mera's way, and went up to the reading rooms.

---

Marcus came in at eight.

Julian's log would show, if he scrolled to it, which he did not, that his heart rate rose at 08:00:14 each morning, when the reading-room door opened and Marcus came through it with his glasses pushed up and his coat over his arm. He knew it rose. He did not scroll back to watch it do so. There were things you could know about yourself in Chronos and still decline to look at, and he had become, over this one autumn, a connoisseur of them.

He did not name the feeling. He had a list for feelings, in the annotation layer, *List 47, Things to tell Adrian*, which he had not added to since 2098 and which he was not going to add this to. He let it be unnamed. He worked beside Marcus through the morning on the key's authority history, the long dull record of every reconciliation the key had ever countersigned, which the inquiry was supposed to be tracing, and which Julian found, to his own surprise, that he was glad to be tracing, because it was a thing he and Marcus were doing with their heads bent over the same table, and because for the length of a working morning the question of who had used the key in the night was a professional question and not a thing that could end him.

---

On Tuesday, over lunch, Marcus had mentioned the old man who sang.

It had come up sidelong, the way things came up with Marcus, in a gap. Marcus had said that there was a man in Veridica, an old man, officially dead, who sang the names of the dead every night, and had been doing it for a wife and a little daughter he had lost in a lakeshore storm in the summer of '74, and who had never, in forty years, had their names in any record, only in a song.

Julian had written it in his annotation layer. *Summer 2074, lakeshore storm, Veridican side. The old man who sings. His wife and his daughter.* He had written it because it was the kind of thing he kept, a thing lost from the record and held somewhere else, and because it had moved him, and because Chronos had the record Veridica did not. Chronos recorded everything, even its weather, even its storms, even the ones that fell on the far shore and were logged by the Border Broadcast Division as a matter of course. If there had been a storm on the lakeshore in the summer of 2074, it would be in the Archive's sealed operational series, with a date, and a duration, and whatever else the Division had thought worth keeping.

He meant to look, when he had an hour. He did not tell Marcus he meant to. It would be a gift, if he found it, and you did not announce a gift.

---

On Thursday night, the nineteenth, he was in the sealed holdings alone, late, tracing the key's authority history down into the older boxes, when he found the one with no catalogue entry.

It was at the end of a shelf, in the lower holdings, among the boxes from the sixties, shelved fore-edge out like all of Mera's boxes. It had no catalogue number on the spine. It had only an accession number, inked on the end, 204117, and nothing else: no subject, no date, no series. An uncatalogued sealed box, in a building where everything was catalogued, where being uncatalogued was itself a kind of record, the record of a thing someone had wanted kept and not found.

He slid it out. Inside was a card folder, tied with archival tape, old tape, gone soft. Through the thumb-cut in the folder's edge he could see the lower right corner of the topmost sheet, and on it, small and typed, a numeral. A Roman numeral.

*VII.*

His hands went cold. He had seen a numeral like that before, this year: a photographed page that had gone round three nations in December, with a different numeral on it, *X*, and the same kind of type, uneven, with a bite out of every lowercase *e*. He had read about it in the Reply. He had not thought he would ever touch one.

He did not untie the tape.

The rule was absolute and he knew it absolutely: an uncatalogued sealed item could be opened only with a second officer present, and both their logs would record the opening. He was alone. He entered the find in the holdings ledger in his own hand, *box 204117, uncatalogued, sealed, found in lower holdings, Senior Historian*, and locked the box in the Senior Historian's cabinet, and went home, and did not sleep, and did not tell himself why he was not going to follow the rule.

---

Friday evening. Adrian came across the lake.

He came the way he came, by a route he never described, and knocked on the flat's door at nine o'clock, and Julian opened it and saw his brother's face for the first time in eleven years, and could not speak for a moment, and then said, "Your log's got you now," because it was the only thing in his head.

"It had me already," said Adrian, coming in, looking round the small orderly flat with its desk turned side-on to the window. "You mirror Veridica's outlaw notices. I've been in your Archive's files for years, little brother. You just never looked."

It was awkward for an hour. They had not been in a room together since Julian was twenty-three and Adrian twenty-two, on a railway platform, in an argument neither of them had ever mentioned since, which was first on a list in Julian's annotation layer that he had never sent. They talked about nothing. Adrian prowled. Then Adrian took the back off the kitchen clock, the way he had taken the back off Aunt Helena's clock when he was six, to see what was inside it, and Julian laughed, for the first time in weeks, a real laugh that surprised him, and after that it was not awkward.

They talked about their father, who had not said Julian's name since he went across the lake. They talked about Helena, who had driven Julian to the checkpoint in 2093 because their father would not, and whom neither of them had spoken to properly in years. They did not talk about what Adrian did, or where he lived, or how he had come across the water. Some things you left.

Near the end, Adrian asked, with an odd look that Julian could not read:

"Chen. The commissioner. Is he kind to you?"

"Yes," said Julian, too fast.

Adrian looked at him for a moment longer than the question needed. "Good," he said, and let it go.

Later, after Adrian had gone back into the dark, Julian scrolled back to that second, 21:47 and some, and watched his brother's face say *good*, and still could not read it. It was not a thing the log could help him with. The log could show him Adrian's face to the nanosecond and could not tell him what was behind it. He filed it in the annotation layer, *Adrian's look, 21:47, unread*, and left it.

---

At 23:40 he went back to the Archive with his own keys.

He had never broken the two-officer rule in his career. He broke it now. He told himself it was because the box was his responsibility, and because he could not open it with a second officer present without a second officer's log recording a thing he did not yet understand, and because he wanted to know what it was before he reported it. All of that was true. None of it was the reason, and he knew the reason, and did not say it to himself: he wanted to be the one who knew first.

At the end reading desk, under the lamp that was always left on, he untied the soft old tape.

Nine typed sheets. The type uneven, a bite out of every lowercase *e*. The numeral VII in the lower right of each. The top sheet was a title page, and under the title a list, the way a book lists itself.

*THE FOUNDATION OF CERTAINTY.*

*A design for the governance of a species that cannot agree on what happened.*

He read the title twice. Then he read the first words of the list under it, and stopped, and sat in the pool of lamplight at the end of the long dark room with the nine sheets in his hands and the whole Archive silent and listening around him, every clock keeping time to a degree no eye could read.

He did not read the rest, not that night. He read the title, and the first lines of the contents, and understood that it was old, older than the nation, and that it was a design, and that it had a numeral on it that matched a numeral that had gone round the world in December.

He decided, sitting there, to give it to Marcus.

He decided it the way he decided most things, completely, in a moment, and then spent an hour checking that the decision was sound. Marcus was the one person in either nation he trusted. Marcus kept his own record; Marcus had kept the entries of his own life in his head in a white room so that they could not be taken; Marcus, of all people, would know what to do with a thing that had been kept out of the record on purpose. He would give it to Marcus, and ask him to read it first and say what it was, and take it back for the box, because the box's contents belonged in the box, in the record, where they could be found.

His log recorded the decision. For once, he wanted it to.

He retied the tape, and locked the folder in the cabinet, and walked home along the harbour in the cold with the lake black on his left and the far shore somewhere beyond it, too far to see, where a man he trusted was asleep, and did not know that Julian had found the thing that would be the end of him, and meant to hand it over, as a gift, because that was the kind of thing you did for the one person you trusted.
