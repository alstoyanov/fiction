# Chapter 12: The Diplomat's Page

*Monday, September 30, 2109. The Sovereignty of Free Will*

---

The link came in at eleven minutes past eleven, which in the capital was eleven minutes past four in the morning, and Isaiah supposed again that Kira had not been to bed.

It came the long way round, as everything from Veridica came: borrowed nodes, a Synthesis relay, half the world, a two-second delay on every sentence so that each arrived finished, with nothing to be done about it. He sat at the desk with his hands folded on one knee and waited through the crackle while the images assembled on his screen in bands, like blinds coming down.

Eight pages. Typed, photographed flat under a lamp, a clean margin round each. He counted the page numbers at the foot as they came: *5, 6, 7, 8*; then *9, 10, 11, 12*. In the lower right corner of each, small, the same Roman numeral. VII. In the type, in every lowercase *e*, the bite he had sat on a cellar stair in his head and looked at for most of a night, two winters ago, in a photograph of a torn page.

At the foot of page eight, a mark: *HV·01.* At the foot of page twelve: *SO·04.*

Then a line in the plain field under the images.

*Copy VII. Insurance. He doesn't know I've sent them. — K.*

She did not name him. She did not need to. There was only one *he* this autumn.

---

He read them twice, the way he read an editorial: first for what they said, then for what they had been built to do.

*Singular truth, mechanically verified, universally enforced. All other principles derive from this necessity. Democracy failed because it assumed humans could collectively determine what happened. We remove the assumption.*

*A system without opposition appears tyrannical; a system with uncontrolled opposition becomes unstable. Let there be several Histologic nations, differing in method and agreeing in substance, and let there be enemies outside them all. Peace would raise questions. Total war would end the system. Controlled conflict answers every question before it is asked.*

*Enemies outside them all.*

He sat back. Outside the window the courtyard lay in the hard clean light, the fig tree, a man from the print room smoking on the step. None of it recorded. He had lived here for nearly three years and still noticed, every day, the way a man who has been ill for a long time goes on noticing that he can breathe: that he could sit in a room, in this country with no interfaces, and have no part of what happened in it kept, unless he chose to keep it himself, by hand, on paper, in a palace in his own head. He had come here with his life sewn into a coat lining, written in newsprint margins with a burned match, because in the country he came from everything was kept and nothing was his. Here nothing was kept and all of it was his. He had thought, the first winter, that this was freedom. He had learned, by the second, that it was only a different arithmetic: a place where a thing was true if a man chose to remember it, and a man could be made to choose, by hunger, by fear, by an asylum that was *conditional*, which was a word he had once admired.

*Enemies outside them all.* He was sitting in one. The Sovereignty of Free Will, which had rejected the Histologic system in 2067 and built itself on the rejection, which gave asylum to the system's outlaws and used them, which had taken him in when he had no state. Here, in his own hand, on a page typed before the Founding, was a sentence that said the Sovereignty's whole existence, its rejection, its hostility, its place at the edge of the Histologic world as the thing to be feared, had been written for it. Not predicted. Prescribed. *Let there be enemies.* The no the Sovereignty had said to the Histologic world had been a line in the Histologic world's own design.

He did not know yet what to do with that. He knew what it would do, in the wrong hands, and he had met the wrong hands at a lunch table in August.

---

He filed them in the study, by copy, not by page, because the copies were the story.

The Introduction, with no numeral. Principle Three and Principle Five, copy X. The torn page thirty, copy XI. And now One and Two, copy VII.

Four copies. Four sets of hands. One document, typed on one machine with a chipped letter, carried in four different numbered copies, surfacing a fragment at a time across three nations over three years. *One fragment is a curiosity*, he had written to Kira two winters ago, in the resistance cipher. *Two is a pattern.* Two had been a pattern for a year. Four was not a pattern. Four was a map, and he had most of it, and he did not know whom to give it to, because everyone who wanted it wanted it for a war.

He laid them out in the study the way he laid out the structure of a long piece: the Introduction at the top, with its *seventeen contributors* and its relief, a frightened woman's relief at having found an answer; then the two principles, One and Two, cold and sure and granting every objection before it could be raised; then, below, the torn page and the sung essay, which he had carried longest. And he saw, laid out, what the thing was doing, which was the question he always asked of a document: not what it said, but what it was built to do. It was built to be found a piece at a time. Each fragment survived on its own, meant nothing fatal on its own, could be called a forgery on its own. Together they were a confession older than the nation. Whoever had let them out, and someone had let them out, in November and December and again now, had let them out slowly, each one when it would do the most work, and Isaiah had spent twenty years watching men release documents and he knew a controlled release when he filed one, and this was a controlled release, and the only question was by whom and for what, and he did not have it, and he wrote *by whom, for what* on a card and put it in the drawer where the unanswered questions went, which was fuller this autumn than it had ever been.

---

At noon he was called into the small conference room at the paper.

The managing editor was not there. The attaché was. He stood by the window with his hands behind his back, in the civilian suit cut for a uniform, and he turned when Isaiah came in and inclined his head, courteous, unhurried, a man with all the time in the world and a use for every minute of it.

On the table were photographs.

Isaiah looked at them from the doorway before he came any further in, because he had learned to read a table before he sat at it. Eight pages. Typed. The bite out of the *e*. The numeral VII in the corners. Principle One and Principle Two.

The pages he had received three hours ago. The pages he had shown to no one.

"Mr. Okonkwo," said the attaché. "We would value your authentication of these. You know the material. You have, I think, made something of a study of it."

Isaiah came in and looked at the photographs properly, and saw that they were not Kira's.

He knew Kira's photographs. He had been receiving them for three years; he knew the lamp she used, flat and even, and the way she squared a sheet to a dark surface before she shot it, and the grain of the farmhouse table under the paper. These were shot under a different lamp, harder, from a little to one side, so that each page threw a faint shadow along its right edge, and the surface under them was pale, and at one corner of one sheet there was the shadow of a thumb, a reader's thumb, holding the page down.

Someone had photographed the originals. Not Kira's photographs of the originals. The originals themselves, the nine typed sheets, under a different lamp, held down by a different thumb. Copy VII was in Chronos, in a box in the Archive. Someone had had it under a lamp.

He did not say any of that. He folded his hands.

He came in and looked at the photographs properly, and did not touch them, and folded his hands behind his back, which was what he did when he most wanted to pick a thing up.

"I authenticate what I publish," said Isaiah, "when I publish it. I haven't published these. I haven't published anything."

"We understand that these have reached you independently."

"Then you understand more than I do about how they reach people." He let that sit. "Where did yours come from?"

"Channels," said the attaché.

Isaiah thought of a gray folder with a red border, and a line he could read and could not read, *Phase: justification*, and a figure said at a lunch table a day before the dead were counted. He did not say any of it. He said, "I'll decline, with respect," and the attaché inclined his head again, as if Isaiah had corrected a point of grammar, and made a note, somewhere, in whatever file a man like that kept, which was not on paper and not in his head but in some third place that Isaiah was beginning to think of as the real archive of the world.

"Your position remains conditional, I think," said the attaché, pleasantly, as Isaiah reached the door.

"It does."

"A good word."

"It is," said Isaiah, and went out, and admired the word's precision a little less than he had in December, which he noted, because a man ought to note when he has begun to be worn down.

---

He drafted a line to Kira that afternoon and did not send it.

*Someone else has your pages.*

He sat with it on the screen for a long time. The trouble was that he did not know what it meant, and he would not send a thing whose meaning he did not know. If the attaché's photographs had come another way, from the Archive itself, from the box, from some hand inside Chronos, then the link between Isaiah and Kira was still the one clean channel in the world, and he should not foul it with an alarm he could not stand behind. If the photographs had come through the link, if the link was open somewhere he could not see, then the link was not clean, and a message saying so would go straight to whoever was reading it, and tell them that he knew.

He did not know which. He would not claim more than he knew. He deleted the draft, and wrote in the study instead, on paper, where nobody could restore it: *The SFW has copy VII, pp. 5–12, under a different lamp. Not K.'s photographs. Source: not established. Do not send this on the link.*

---

Evening. The parlour of the memory palace, among the harmless true warm things: the fire, the long table with its lace runner, the pepper soup always just about to be served, his first byline framed over the fireplace.

He read Principle Two aloud, there, among the true things, because a thing read aloud among true things could sometimes be weighed better. He read it to the room, in his own voice, and when he came to *differing in method and agreeing in substance*, Aunt Sarah, at the head of the table, went very still.

She had always sat there. When he was six the table had seemed as long as a street, and she had sat at the head of it, sharp and elegant, already talking about the diplomatic service and the languages it would want of him. She had sat there when he was eighteen and brought home his first byline, and read it at the table in silence, and handed it back with one word underlined. She had not called after his arrest. In all the years he had kept the palace, through every room he had built and emptied and furnished again, she had never once said a word.

She said none now. She sat in the firelight with her hands folded on the cloth in the way he folded his own, and at *agreeing in substance* she stopped, the way a person stops who has heard their own name spoken in another room, and was still, and the narration of his own mind, which he could usually trust to tell him why the palace did what it did, said nothing at all about why she had stopped.

He did not pursue it. He had learned, over three years, not to pursue the things the palace did on its own, because the palace was wiser than he was about some things and showed him in its own time; it had shown him the colour of the cellar door was gone before he knew to grieve it; and it would show him this when he was ready, and he was not ready, and he knew he was not ready, and the not-being-ready was itself a thing he filed, in the study, under her name, which he had labelled a drawer with some months ago without letting himself finish the thought that went with the labelling. He finished the principle aloud, to the quiet room, and let it go quiet, and did not look at the head of the table again that night.

---

In the study, late, he wrote one line on a card, for the drawer where he kept the things he could not yet use.

*One fragment is a curiosity. Two is a pattern.*

He looked at it. He had four copies now, in four sets of hands, which was more than a pattern. It was a document, nearly whole, surfacing on a schedule, in three nations, in a war. He had Principle One, which said the record must be singular. He had Principle Two, which said he was an enemy by design. He had the torn page, which said arrange to be the one who restores it, and across the lake a man he had been in a glass cell with was, this month, arranging exactly that, through the trust of a man in Chronos, for reasons Isaiah would have shared if he had been standing where Marcus stood, and would condemn when he was standing where he himself stood, which was the trouble with it, which was always the trouble.

He did not know whom to tell.

He put the card in the drawer, and closed it, and sat in the good chair by the window until the courtyard went dark, with the four copies filed in his head and no one in the world he trusted to hold all four at once except a woman across the lake who had just sent him two of them without telling the man she loved, and an old man who sang, and himself.
