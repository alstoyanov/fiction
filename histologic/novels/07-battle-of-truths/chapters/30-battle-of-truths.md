# Chapter 30: Battle of Truths

*Monday, December 23, 2109. The Cabinet Office; the corridor; the flat by the old river, Veridica Capital*

---

*Dec 23, 2109. 09:40. First Minister's office. Involved parties: Chen, M.; Osman, K. Summoned by: Maitland, O. Purpose:*

He left the field blank, on the stairs, climbing. The last time he had climbed these stairs was the twenty-sixth of August, and he had come alone that time, and he had come to ask. He noted that. He had come alone, and asked, and been handed deniability, and used it, and here he was again, four months and seven dead and one ruined man later, summoned, with Kira beside him, and he did not know yet what he was being summoned to, and he left the field blank, because a man who writes the purpose of a meeting before the meeting is a man who thinks he already knows, and he had learned, this year, what came of thinking he already knew.

In his pocket was Isaiah's letter. It had come by the Synthesis courier on Saturday, not the long link, which told Marcus something in itself, that Isaiah had chosen the slower channel and the one that could not be read from inside Veridica, which meant Isaiah thought the inside of Veridica was where the danger was. One line, in Isaiah's upright hand, the hand that had been taught properly at school and had never since had reason to write badly. *Someone in Veridica wants to be invaded.* He had shown it to Kira and to no one else. He touched it once on the stairs, through the cloth of his coat, and did not touch it again until the end.

He had read it, by Monday, perhaps forty times. He had done with it the thing he did with everything, which was to try to put it in a column, and it would not go in a column. It was not CHECKED; he could not verify it; he had Isaiah's word and a seam Kira could read and nothing he could hold. It was not UNCHECKED in the ordinary way, because UNCHECKED was for things he meant to check, and there was no witness he could go to for this, no aunt in a building two floors down, no roll to read; the whole of the letter's content was that the thing it described was hidden inside the one place he could not see into, which was the building he worked in. And it was certainly not a HOPE. He had ended, over the weekend, by not filing it at all, which was a thing he almost never did, and the not-filing sat in him like a stone, because a thing he could not file was a thing he could not put down.

Kira climbed beside him. They were strained, and together; she had read the letter and said, after a while, *then it's about the building*, and had come with him today, in the same car, and they had not talked on the way. She knew everything he had done now, from Julian's log, which the whole world had read; there was nothing left in him that was not in the record; and she had come anyway, which he did not understand and did not question, because questioning it might end it.

---

The office was the same room as August. Maitland behind the desk, big and tired and courteous. Colonel Sokolov by the window in new insignia, hands in her coat pockets, silent. The brief was on the desk: the fleet, the staging, the soldiers with no interfaces.

Wei was not there. Chen Zhao was not there. Marcus noted their absence as a fact entry, in his head, *Wei: not present. Chen Zhao: not present*, and did not interpret it, because he had learned from watching Sokolov that the not-interpreting was sometimes the discipline, and because he had an idea, now, after Isaiah's letter, about who might arrange to be absent from a room where a war was being asked for, and he was not ready to look at the idea directly.

Maitland opened plainly.

"The SFW is preparing to invade," he said. "I'll spare you the briefing; the Colonel can give it to you if you want the detail. The short of it is a fleet in the Atlantic, growing, with somewhere to sail from, and soldiers that our whole arsenal doesn't touch, because they've nothing behind their ears for us to reach. It's not a bluff. It's a build." He put his chin on his fist. "We need you."

"I'm done with deception," said Marcus.

"This won't be a war of facts."

"Then you need soldiers, not spies."

"I need someone who understands both," said Maitland. He said it without heat, as a fact. "I have soldiers. I have the Colonel, who is the best I've got and who has just told me that everything she knows how to do is useless against these people. I have spies, who are useless for the same reason; you can't seed a man with no interface and you can't storm him. What I don't have is someone who understands what the last war was and why this one is different, and who the country trusts, and who has already proved he'll tell me a thing I don't want to hear." He looked at Marcus. "I know your index. Fifty-seven. I read it on the second, on the steps, with everyone else. I don't care. I'd rather have a man at fifty-seven who confessed with nothing attached than a man at nought who's never been tested. Those are the men I've got too many of."

Marcus heard the echo. *You saved us. At what cost? Seven, and none since noon.* He heard it, and did not say it again, because saying it again would be a performance of the thing instead of the thing, and the thing was still true and did not need saying twice. His POV lost a word, somewhere in the middle of the exchange; he reached for the word for a man who is asked a second time to do the thing that ruined him; and there was a smooth place where it should have been; and he let it go, because he had seven dead and a fleet and no time to stand in a room reaching after a word.

Sokolov said nothing throughout, until Maitland turned to her and asked, and she said one line. "The fleet is real, First Minister." And then nothing, with a pause after it. Marcus had learned, over two books, to listen to Sokolov the way you listen to a held note, for the thing that came after the pause; but she let this pause stand and put nothing after it, and he understood that the nothing was the thing, that she had given Maitland the one fact she would stand behind and declined to decorate it, and that she was, in her way, the most honest person in the room, because she was the only one who would not interpret.

He thought of Marram End while Maitland talked. The short stone slip, the open water, the paper book in four hands. He thought of a child walking out towards lamps that were not there, and of the difference between Marram End and Kestrel Point being one pair of hands at the right second. He thought that everything he knew how to do was about hands at the right second: the record, the witness, the many copies, the song, the standing in a doorway holding it open so that others could run. And none of it was about a beach, or a fleet, or a man with nothing behind his ears who could not be turned by any truth, who would come up a slip in the dark not towards a lamp that was not there but towards a capital that was.

---

Marcus looked at Kira.

It was a long moment. The brief on the desk, the fleet, the Colonel at the window, the First Minister waiting. He looked at Kira, and it was not a request for forgiveness, because he had not asked her to forgive him and she had not, and he was not going to do in a First Minister's office the thing Old Songs and Elena had both told him not to do, which was to ask the people he had wronged to carry the asking. It was not forgiveness. It was a question, and the question was only: *will you do this with me, knowing everything, strained, together, one more time.*

She nodded. Once.

"One more time," said Marcus. "But this time, we do it right."

Maitland did not ask what *right* meant. Marcus noted that too, and noted that he did not himself yet know what it meant, that he had said a word he could not define, the way he had accepted a word in August he could define too well, and that the difference between the two words was the whole of what the year had taught him, and that he would have to find out what *right* meant by doing it, the way you find out everything, afterwards, in the record of having done it.

---

The corridor, outside.

Sokolov passed them, going the other way, formal. "Commissioners." And then, low, on her way out, not quite to them: "They are not coming for the lakeshore." And was gone, before either of them could answer, which was her way, the real sentence last and no waiting for a reply.

In the lift, Kira said, "Will you tell Maitland about the letter?"

"Not yet," said Marcus. "I don't know who in the building it's about."

"Then it's about the building," said Kira. She had said it on Saturday too. She said it the same way now, flatly, as a fault located. "A thing that could be any one person in a building is a thing about the building. You don't look for the one person. You look at how the building is built, so that one person could do it. That's the only question worth asking. It's the question we should have asked about the key." She did not say it unkindly. She said it as the thing they had both learned, at a cost, and would not unlearn.

The lift reached the ground floor, and the doors opened on the great hall's quiet afternoon, and neither of them moved to get out at once. She looked at him, in the steel box, in the flat even light, the way she had looked at him across a reconciliation room in the dark while a nation's past climbed past everything she had meant to write.

"I built it narrow," she said. "You know that. I held the knife. And it didn't matter, because the thing it went into wasn't what any of us thought it was." She said it level. "I'm not saying it to be forgiven, and I'm not asking you to say it either. I'm saying it because you're about to say *we do it right*, and I want you to know, before you say it, that right isn't a knife held narrow. We did that. Right is asking what the building is before you put a knife in it. That's the only thing I've got that's new. It took me a nation to learn it."

"I know," said Marcus.

"Then we agree," said Kira, and stepped out of the lift, and he followed her, and it was not forgiveness, and it was the nearest thing to standing on the same ground that they had managed since October.

---

The flat, evening.

He took out his lists and unfolded them on the table under the dark window. CHECKED, UNCHECKED, HOPES, all of them full, the paper soft at the folds, copied fresh since the operation. He read down UNCHECKED, where *Julian?* no longer stood, where there was the empty line he had left as the shape of a question that had been answered and had destroyed him. The line was still empty. He had still not written anything in it.

He considered the record for a long time. He had checked it, once, in the one place it mattered most, and it had said what he feared, and he had believed it, and a man was in a cell at maximum because of it, innocent, and a nation had woken up rewritten, and the whole of it had happened because he had trusted one record over one witness, he, who had built his life on *not one record, many witnesses*, he, the one of them who checked, which was why the system had chosen him, which Old Songs had told him, which was true.

He did not know what *right* meant. But he knew what *wrong* had been, now, exactly, in his own hand, in the record, and knowing what wrong had been was a kind of map, even without the word for the far shore of it.

He took a clean sheet. He wrote a new heading at the top, in his small historian's pencil hand.

*QUESTIONS FOR THE WAR.*

He took Isaiah's letter out of his pocket and unfolded it and laid it beside the clean sheet. *Someone in Veridica wants to be invaded.* He read it once more. Then he wrote the first entry under the new heading, which was not a fact, and not a hope, and not a thing to be checked against a witness, but the only honest way to begin a thing you did not understand, which was to ask it the right question first, and to ask it before you knew the answer, so that you could not decide the answer in advance and then go looking for a record that said it.

*Who is invading whom?*

He looked at it for a long time. Then he put the pencil down, and did not answer it, because it was the twenty-third of December, and the answer was Book Eight, and a man who answers the first question of a war on the night he writes it down has learned nothing from the year he has just lived, and Marcus Chen, whatever else the year had cost him, had learned that.
