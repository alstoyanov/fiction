# Chapter 4: Chronos Believes

*Wednesday, August 21, 2109. The teaching hospital, the Chronos lakeshore city*

---

*06:00. Determination of the collective will, read on every screen and into every log. Probability: 0.91. Finding: that Veridica has attacked the memory of Chronos a second time, using credentials of the Chronos Temporal Archive. Response: corrective broadcasting resumes at noon. Expected admissions: Admitted:*

Yuki Tanaka left the last field blank and looked at it.

She had written the note at her kitchen table with the screen on the wall still showing the determination in its plain courteous type, and the newsreader's calm voice saying it a second time for anyone who had been in the shower. Behind her ears, she knew, the same words were going into four million logs on this shore of the province alone, timed to the nanosecond, so that in a hundred years anyone who wished could find the exact moment at which they had been told. It was the Chronos way. Even the beginning of a war was recorded with perfect courtesy.

She had expected it since Thursday. Everyone had. The Administration of Temporal Defence had called the night of the fourteenth *a second attack* within six hours, and Chronos did not use a word in its bulletins that it did not intend to act on. She had expected it, and she had still set her cup down very carefully when the first line came up, with both hands.

Eight months. The broadcasts had stopped on the seventeenth of December, and she had stood at this window that night and heard the city go quiet in a way it had not been quiet since the spring before, and she had not trusted it. She had been right not to. She found that being right was no comfort at all.

She finished her tea, and washed the cup, and went in.

---

The storm-trauma ward had been closed since February. They had reopened it on Monday, quietly, on a director's memo, because the director had read the same bulletins she had. Two of the night nurses had come back from other wards. The beds were made. The crash trolley had been checked. The four stabilisation crates were still in the corridor outside the ward doors where they had stood since the seventh of December, stacked two and two against the wall, gray, with the Administration's clinical seal across each lid and a printed label: *Pending review of the clinical record.*

Nobody had ever come to review it. The order had been suspended, and suspension in Chronos was a state like any other, logged and permanent until something else was logged on top of it. Nobody had come to take the crates away either. They had stood there all spring and all summer while porters steered trolleys round them, and people had stopped seeing them, the way people stop seeing a pillar.

Tanaka saw them. She stopped in front of them at ten past seven and checked the seals herself, one by one, with her fingers on the edge of each band to be sure it was whole. They were whole.

At half past seven Morrison came along the corridor with his coat over his arm, and stopped at the crates without being asked, and checked the seals after her, in the same order, with his steady hands. He did not say anything. She did not ask him to. He straightened up and looked at her once, and then went on towards the lifts and the fourth floor, and she stood a moment longer in front of the four gray boxes, and then went in to the ward.

---

At noon the storms came back.

She was on the ward. She did not feel it. That was the strangest thing, and she had never got used to it: a Veridican-born woman with a Veridican interface in a Chronos hospital, sitting at a nurses' station in the middle of a corrective broadcast, and feeling nothing at all, because the Chronos broadcasts were aimed at Veridican heads on the far shore. What came back over the lake was Veridica's ordinary signal, the ordinary history Veridica told itself every day, carried across the water on the old border overlap and arriving on the Chronos shore as a second story, where it met Chronos's own and the two of them fought in people's heads. It had always run both ways. Chronos's own broadcasts lit it up again.

By two the admissions had begun.

*14:20. Female, 41. Lakeshore district, the quay road. Brought in by her husband. Presenting: knows two dates for her own wedding, eleven months apart. Can describe each in detail: the weather, the flowers, her mother's hat. Asks her husband, repeatedly and with increasing distress, which one he attended. He does not know. He attended one. He says he cannot remember which. No focal signs. Prognosis*

She stopped there. She knew the prognosis. The woman would know both weddings for the rest of her life, and so, very probably, would the husband, and they would learn to live in a marriage with two beginnings, the way the people on the far shore had learned to live with two piers. *Prognosis: good, for this.* She had written that line a hundred times last winter. She left it unwritten now.

*15:05. Male, 9. Brought in by a neighbour. Found walking along the harbour wall towards a quay that is present in one history and not the other. Turned back by a fisherman. States, calmly, that he was going to the ferry. There is no ferry from that point in either history. Prognosis*

*15:50. Female, 66. Lakeshore. A street sign.*

*16:30. Male, 23. Knows his brother is dead. Knows his brother is in the waiting room.*

The brother was in the waiting room. She went out and saw him, and came back, and sat at the station for a minute with her hands folded on the desk, and then went on.

---

At half past three two officers of the Administration of Temporal Defence came up to the ninth floor.

She knew because the ward clerk told her, in the low pleasant voice the ward clerk used for things she thought a doctor ought to know and did not want to be overheard telling. "They're with the director. Since three. They've got a box."

At four Tanaka went up.

The ninth-floor corridor was cool, as it always was, and quiet, and it smelled faintly of cut stems from the ward at the end. The director's door was open. Inside, Dr. Soren Vale sat behind his desk with his long hands folded on the blotter, and across from him two officers in the plain gray of Temporal Defence sat very upright on the visitors' chairs, and on the desk between them was a ledger.

She knew the ledger. It was the 9S paper certification ledger, a tall book bound in green cloth, which lived in the locked cabinet in the ward office. Every death certified on the sealed ward went into it in ink, by hand, because on the sealed ward nothing went into a log.

"Dr. Tanaka," said Vale, and rose, as he always did, as if she were his senior. "Thank you for coming up. These officers are reviewing the province's unlogged records. All of them. The Administration's index was damaged last week, and they are being very thorough about what lies outside it."

"Of course," said Tanaka.

The senior of the two officers was a woman of about forty with a precise, unhurried manner, and a tablet on which every word in this room was now being written down by her interface, and by her colleague's, and by Vale's. Tanaka's own interface recorded nothing; it never had. Here, in this office, she was the only person present whose memory of the next ten minutes would belong to nobody but herself.

"Doctor," said the officer. "We're asking about one certificate." She turned the ledger a little towards Tanaka with one finger. The page was January. The entry was in Vale's hand, in ink, beautifully regular.

*KERR, Margit. Ward 9S. Date of death: 4 January 2109. Time: 17:52.*

"The record shows that at 16:20 on that afternoon officers of the border directorate asked at this hospital for Mrs. Kerr by name," said the officer. "And that within the hour she was certified dead, and removed by mortuary van. The director has told us that the death was expected, and that the timing was a coincidence. We're asking, for completeness, whether you were the attending physician."

Tanaka folded her hands.

She had been asked a great many questions in her life by people who were writing the answers down. She had learned to answer exactly the question that was asked, and to answer it truthfully, and to stop.

"I was her physician from August 2106," she said. "The certificate is Dr. Vale's."

The officer looked at her for a moment. Then she noted it. Her colleague noted it. Vale's log noted it, and so did the walls, in their way.

"Thank you, Doctor."

"Thank you," said Vale to the officers, warmly, "for your thoroughness. It is a great comfort to know that the Administration looks so carefully at what it cannot see."

---

Ward 9S still ran on paper.

She went in at five, through the plain doors at the end of the corridor, and felt the small soundless click behind the ears as the record let her go. Charts at the ends of the beds. The board by the station with its initials. The day-room screen showing a Synthesis concert, its sound low. The disinfectant, sharp and cold and faintly green.

The bed by the east window was made.

It had been made since the fourth of January. The sheets tight and white, turned down at the corner the way the night nurse turned them, the locker empty, the window full of the lake. Nobody had been assigned it. Dr. Vale had not said why, and nobody had asked him.

Tanaka did the round. She went from bed to bed in order and took each pulse and wrote each chart and asked each patient how they had slept, and she did not look at the bed by the east window for longer than she looked at any of the others. She was very careful about that. It was not difficult. She had spent her whole professional life looking at things for exactly as long as was clinically required.

---

At five past five Julian Kovač came to the storm ward.

She knew who he was before the clerk said his name. She had seen him in January from the far end of a ninth-floor corridor, a narrow man in a gray scarf walking away from her patients towards a lift. She had seen his photograph on every Chronos screen in December, the man who had published his own orders. She had read the Reply, in which one line was his.

He was Chronos's liaison to the witness networks now. Two members of his lakeshore networks had been admitted that afternoon, a man from the quay road and the woman with two weddings, and the networks looked after their own, so the liaison had come. He had a list of their names on a card, handwritten, and he held it out to the ward clerk as if he were not sure the clerk would accept a list on paper. The capitals sloped forward. She had seen that slope at the back of a book in a farmhouse kitchen.

He was coming apart.

She saw it the way she saw it in patients, from the door, before anyone spoke. It was not in his face; his face was courteous and attentive. It was in the precision. He gave the clerk the time of each admission to the second, unasked, correctly. He spelled each name. He apologised twice for the hour. He had forgotten his coat. It was not cold, and he had not needed one, but he kept touching the place on his arm where a coat would have been, as if he had left something there. He had not slept. Not last night, and not, she thought, much the night before. His log would know exactly how long. It was a strange thing, in this country, to look at a man and know that somewhere behind his eyes the precise figure for his exhaustion was being kept.

She took him to the two beds herself.

---

The storm-ward corridor was logged, like every corridor in Chronos that was not on the ninth floor. Everything he heard went into his log. Everything he saw. Her face, her voice, the time.

In January he had walked three men to the door of her ward at a quarter to four in the morning, under his own name, with his Archive card, and gone back down in the lift alone. One of the three had been seventy and dead on paper. Nobody had told her his name; she had known it the way she knew most things now, from a gray coat and a tune. And in the afternoon of that same day, officers had asked for a patient on her ward by a name that existed in one file in one office, and the man who had walked the three in was the only Chronos citizen who had been through those doors who could have—

She did not finish the thought. She had not finished it in January either. She kept it where she kept things.

He did not mention January. Neither did she. They walked down the logged corridor side by side, a little apart, and she understood that this silence was the only courtesy his country allowed him, and that she was being asked for it, and she gave it.

He said only public things. He asked after both patients by name and was told what the chart allowed. He said that his keepers' paper books agreed with each other and with nothing in the index. He said that the analysts at Temporal Defence had gone through the night of the fourteenth line by line, and that every analysis said it was the Archive's key.

"The Archive's," said Tanaka.

"Yes." He looked at the floor. "Every analysis says it was the Archive's key."

He said *the Archive's*. Not *mine*. She noticed it and did not know why she noticed it, and filed it, as she filed things she could not explain, unlabelled.

At the woman's bed he stood for a long time while she told him, with great precision and in great distress, about both her weddings. He listened to every word. He did not tell her which was true. He said, when she had finished, "I'll write both down, if you'd like. In handwriting. So that someone has them." She said that she would like that. He took out a small cloth-covered book from his jacket pocket and wrote, standing, with the book against his palm, slowly, in that sloping hand, and she watched him write her two weddings into it side by side, and stopped crying.

*Kovač, J. Not my patient. Visiting. Sleep: insufficient. Affect: exact. He asked after both patients by name and got neither name wrong. He asked after no one else.*

She did not examine the note. She wrote it and closed the folder.

At the ward doors he thanked her. He said, "Doctor," and inclined his head, the way his aunt was said to, and went to the lifts, and as the doors closed she saw him put his hand on his arm again, where the coat was not.

---

By evening the officers had gone.

She went up to the ninth floor at eight to sign the day's 9S charts, and found Vale at the window at the end of the corridor, beyond the ward doors, standing as he often stood at that hour, with his hands behind his back, looking out over the harbour. The lake had gone the colour of pewter. Somewhere out on it, too far to see, the far shore was receiving what this one sent.

He heard her and turned, stooped, courteous, his face entirely calm.

"They took a copy of the ledger page," he said. "Very properly. They signed for it."

"What did they want?"

He considered the question as if it were a clinical one, and gave it his full attention, and answered it.

"They are asking who else I have certified," said Dr. Vale. "I have told them: many people, over many years. It is a hospital."
