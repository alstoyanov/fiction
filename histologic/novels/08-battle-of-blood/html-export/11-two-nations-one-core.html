Chapter 11: Two Nations, One Core

Tuesday, March 18, 2110. The Factorepo Spire, the capital

At twenty to two in the morning Nikolai Volkov was alone on the war coordination floor, because Kira was off shift and asleep on the cot in the anteroom until six, and the night was quiet in the way the floor was quiet, which was not silence but the small continuous sound of thirty desks of machinery doing a nation's arithmetic while most of the nation slept.

The Judge coordinated the defence without a voice. Its panels gave probabilities, routes, shelter assignments, battery supply, the clock against every column on every road; and the lines of the enemy's advance still bent, as they had bent for three weeks, inward, toward the capital and the building Nikolai sat in. He had watched those lines come in a little further every night. He did not like being inside the thing they pointed at, and he had learned, on a causeway and in a white cell and on a rooftop he could no longer remember, that not liking a thing was not a reason to look away from it.

He had Wei's maintenance map, the one Wei had put on Kira's desk on the twenty-eighth. It showed the physical core: the racks, the power, the removal routes, the maintenance line keyed M that ran in from the transit tunnel. It did not show the logical structure, the way the data actually lay and moved, and Marcus had asked, on the seventh, how the enemy could be denied the core, and Kira had asked Nikolai to find out what the core now held. So he worked on the logical structure himself, from the source's own index, which he could read because he had once, as Pieter Lang, been given the run of it.

He traced the join into his notebook, line by line, because a thing you had drawn by hand was a thing you had checked.

Veridica's true source and Chronos's were one system, had been since the twenty-sixth of October, when a narrow image had gone into a thing that turned out not to be narrow; Book 7 had proved it, and tonight he mapped it. Chronos's true source lived here, in Veridica's core, in the Spire. Its backups restored from here. He wrote the sentence and underlined the figure after it, once: three hundred and thirty million people, Veridica and Chronos together, whose past was in this building. Whoever took the Spire intact took the record of both. Julian had said it in a message in March, Marcus had half-seen it in October, Kira had drawn the axes to it in February, and tonight Nikolai put it in a notebook as a thing with a number on it, and the number frightened him, which was the correct response to it, so he let it.

The join had two restore legs, as it should: one to Veridica's backup systems, one to Chronos's. Then he saw a third.

It was reserved and quiet. It carried no traffic. It was permissioned in a form he could not read, which was itself a thing, because he could read nearly every form the source used. He followed its endpoint, the way you follow a cable to find where it goes, and it resolved to nothing. Not to a Veridican address. Not to any address on the Chronos map he had rebuilt in October. It went somewhere that was not on either map, and he could not see where, and the not-seeing was clean, deliberate, a route that had been made not to be followed.

He checked it three ways, because a thing seen once was a thing that might be an error in the seeing. He queried the leg's endpoint against the national address map, and it resolved to nothing. He queried it against the Chronos map he had rebuilt in October, line by line, from the inside, as Pieter Lang, and it resolved to nothing there either. He checked the permission on it, and the permission was in a form he could not read, which should not have been possible, because he could read every form the source used, had spent a winter learning them; a form he could not read was a form made by someone with access he did not have, and there were very few people with access he did not have. He ran the query a fourth time, against nothing, against the whole of the address space of both nations, and the leg pointed out of it, past the edge of everything he could name, to a place that was not on any map he had.

He said it aloud, to the empty floor, because saying a thing aloud was a way of refusing to pretend he had not seen it.

"It goes nowhere I can see."

He did not guess where. He had spent his life not guessing, because a guess written down beside a fact started, in time, to look like a fact, and he had watched what that did to people. He wrote it in the notebook with the time, 03:05, and the one word Marcus used for a thing that was true and unexplained, in capitals: UNCHECKED. A third leg in the join, reserved, quiet, going nowhere he could see. He looked at it, and did not look longer than he had to, and went down to see the thing itself.

At half past four he took the service stair on Wei's map down to the cage-walk above the core, because a map was a drawing and a drawing was not the thing.

The cage-walk ran above the rack halls, a steel gantry with row lights below it, and the core was down there in the cold clean dark: rows of racks, the data in them showing as nothing at all, because a rack does not tell you where its data goes or what it holds or whose past it keeps. It was the quietest place he had ever stood. He counted the rows, because Kira would have counted the rows, and because counting was a thing to do with the part of him that wanted to be afraid. Thirty-one rows on this level. He wrote it down.

He thought, standing on the cage-walk, of the rooftop. The memory the cradle had taken from him at Ashford, the three boys on a parapet arguing about who was born first, which he no longer had and held only as a story his brother had written down for him in a primer, testimony of a thing that had happened to him and that he could not reach. A thing could be real and go nowhere he could see. He knew that better than anyone alive. He had a real past he could not follow to its source, and down there in the dark was the real past of two nations, and up here on the map was a leg of the join that went nowhere he could see, and he stood between them in the cold and did not make the three things into one thought, because they were not one thing, and a tired man at half past four turned everything into one thing if he let himself.

At ten past six Kira took over, with coffee, black, no sugar, and he showed her the join and the two-nation fact, and she took it the way she took a fault, levelly, and said the one word nobody wanted said, which was that whoever took the Spire took Chronos too.

He did not show her the third leg. It was in his notebook, filed UNCHECKED, and it was not a finding; it was a thing he had seen and could not follow; and he would not put a half-seen thing in front of Kira on a working morning, because Kira with a half-seen thing was Kira who could not sleep. He would tell her when it was more than a line.

She pointed at something else on the network schedule, a thing he had not looked at. Blocks on the national broadcast network, reserved from the twenty-fourth of March by Emergency Information, for the National Broadcast Directorate, labelled Civil stabilisation: district sequence. The content was sealed. The Judge had allocated the capacity without comment, as it allocated everything, and moved on.

"Reassurance," said Kira, reading it. "That's what Emergency Information's been calling it in cabinet. People have been frightened." She did not say it as if she believed it. She said it as if she were reading a label she intended to come back to. Then she filed it, and he filed it, and neither of them said the thing they did not have the words for yet, because the words for it had not been found.

At twenty past eleven Helena Kovač came up to the floor in person, in her dark coat, with her hands in her pockets, because paper should be carried and she did not trust it to a channel.

Julian's letter had come to the testimony intake by lake courier, in his sloping hand. Julian was recorded at maximum, she reminded Nikolai, so every word he wrote was already public in Chronos as he wrote it; the paper did not make it secret; it only made it a thing you could hold. She read it to Nikolai flatly, in her briefing voice, and then handed it over.

If they take your Judge, they take ours. I would like Chronos to be asked this time.

She added nothing to it. As she turned to go, she said, over her shoulder, in the same flat voice: "There is a file on my desk I do not like, Mr. Volkov. I will tell you about it when I have read it." And she went, with her hands in her pockets, down the stair, carrying the empty envelope because she carried everything.

Nikolai sat with Julian's letter in his hand, and read it again, the sloping hand, the forward-leaning capitals, a hand he had seen before on a card in the Reply, a hand like his own brother Adrian's, the Kovač hand. If they take your Judge, they take ours. I would like Chronos to be asked this time.

He had run the relay on the night of the twenty-fifth of October. He had been the one who said, in a cold room in the Chronos Archive, it's already there. The Awakening that had rewritten a nation had come through a line he held. Julian's sentence did not accuse him. I would like Chronos to be asked this time. It accused no one, and that was worse than an accusation, because an accusation you could answer and this you could only carry.

At seven in the evening he forwarded Julian's words to Kira's desk, exactly as written, with no comment of his own, because the words needed none and his comment would have been an apology and the apology was not his to make to Kira. Then he opened his notebook to the UNCHECKED line, the third leg, goes nowhere I can see, and looked at it once, and closed the notebook again, and did not know, at twenty past seven in the evening on the eighteenth of March, that he had just filed the one true thing about how the war would end, two months early, in a line he would not understand until it was over.
