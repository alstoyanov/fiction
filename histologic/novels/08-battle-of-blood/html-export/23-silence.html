Chapter 23: Silence

Friday, May 9, 2110. The Meridian Bridge and the capital, Veridica

They built it over four days in a basement, Nikolai and Maya, a small module that did not cut the capital's clock but made it wander: a fraction of a millisecond, out and back, never the same way twice, so that the Directorate's carrier could not re-lock onto the beat once it lost it. It would cost The Judge's war coordination about four minutes of degraded shell warnings when it went in, four minutes in which the machine would not know quite what time it was and so could not time a convoy to the second, and Nikolai had thought about the four minutes for a long time, because four minutes of bad shell warnings in a besieged city was a number with people in it.

"I'll take four minutes," he said, in the end, to Maya, and she wrote it down, because he said it the way a man signs a thing, and she kept a record of what he signed.

He kept a copy of everything, as always, three copies, in three places, one of them a place he did not let himself picture. His one flash of dark humour, over the module, in the basement: "If they shoot me, at least it will be for a clock." Maya did not laugh, but the corner of her mouth went, which was as much as either of them had in them that week.

She asked him why 03:12.

He could have given a technical answer; there was a technical answer, about the Directorate's re-lock windows; but that was not the real one. "Because it's theirs," he said. "Twelve minutes past three. It was the Chronos Gap. It was the Rewriting. It was the default on Wei's tools, the ones that were too clean. It's the minute they've used on two nations." He looked at his hands. "I'd like it back."

At twenty to three on Friday morning they were in the maintenance vault under the east abutment of the Meridian Bridge, where the capital's clock distribution feed entered the Directorate, in wet concrete with the cable trays overhead and the river loud above them and the shelling going on in the east. Maya had tapped the Directorate's carrier-lock monitor and read it aloud. Nikolai looked at his hands while he worked, and thought once of Alexei, who would have wanted to do this tonight, who would have grinned at a plan to steal a minute back from the people who stole it, and he put the thought away, because the thought was a door and he could not open doors tonight.

He waited for it in the wet concrete with the river loud above and the cable trays dripping and Maya's voice reading the monitor in the dark, and he thought, in the last minutes, about all the things that minute had been. Twelve minutes past three. The minute the Chronos Gap began, when four million people on a far shore lost seventeen minutes of their sleep. The minute the Rewriting began, when two million journals redrew themselves while a widow at Kestrel Point watched her own page come down. The default on Wei's tools, the ones that were too clean, that defaulted to this minute as if it were theirs, as if it were a thing they owned. It had been used, that minute, three times, on two nations, by a hand he had never been able to name. And he was going to use it once, now, by the people it had been used on, to give a city back its fear, which was the first good thing anyone had ever done with it, and he thought that was fitting, and that Alexei would have thought it fitting, and would have grinned, and he put the thought of Alexei away because the thought of Alexei was a door and he could not open doors tonight.

At twelve minutes past three exactly he applied the offset.

"Lock lost," said Maya, reading the monitor. "Oh-three-twelve and four seconds."

The Directorate tried to re-lock at thirty-one minutes past three, and the wander defeated it. It tried again at ten to five, and the wander defeated it again, because you cannot find a beat that will not hold still. The Judge's coordination dropped at twelve minutes past three and came back at sixteen minutes past, four minutes, as he had said, as he had signed, and in those four minutes he did not know what a convoy somewhere did without its timing, and he would not know, and he had decided to carry the not-knowing, and he carried it.

The city woke over hours. It was not a switch. He had told Maya it would not be a switch, and it was not.

At about four in the morning a shell landed near a street shelter, and for the first time in weeks, people screamed. He heard it reported on the net, in the flat coordination traffic, panic at shelter, north district, and he understood that the panic was the thing coming back, the ordinary human panic that a dose had taken away, and that the report of panic was, tonight, good news, which was a terrible thing to be true and was true.

At half past five a queue at a water point broke up into an argument, two people shouting about a place in a line, which had not happened in weeks, because people under the dose did not argue about places in lines, they smiled and waited, and now they argued, and the argument was theirs.

At six the volunteers stopped smiling.

Through the morning it came back, district by district, in the reverse of the order it had gone out: a man sat down in the road and asked where his son was and would not be moved; a woman stood in a doorway reading her own journal aloud to nobody, her own January, finding her doubt again in her own hand; people wept, at last, for the siege dead they had felt until this morning only as pride. Doubt came back, and grief came back, and fear came back, and it was theirs, every bit of it, horrible and theirs, taken back from a carrier that had taken it away.

He stood at the mouth of the vault as it got light and watched a little of it, because he had done the thing and a man should look at the thing he had done. A city waking from a dose was not a pretty thing. It was a city remembering, all at once, over a few hours, everything it had been given leave to forget: that it was surrounded, that its sons were dying at the coast, that the dead were dead and not merely serving, that nothing was sure. He watched a woman come out of a shelter into the gray and stop and put her hand to her mouth, and he understood that she had just remembered, in the space of a breath, a grief she had been carrying as pride for six weeks, and that the carrying-it-as-pride had been a mercy and the remembering-it-as-grief was the truth, and that he had just taken the mercy away and given the truth back, and that this was the right thing, and that the right thing looked, this morning, exactly like a cruelty, and that he would have to live with its looking like one.

At half past six the radio in the vault spoke, and it was Kira, from a north-district cellar: "They're reading." Nikolai thought of Marcus in the dark under the intake, waking the same way, reading his own book by a lamp among the folders; and of the six in the cradles on the calibration floor, who would wake with everyone else, if Reeves had told Maya the truth, and nobody yet knew whether he had.

At half past six the radio in the vault spoke again, Kira from the cellar, giving him the count of what the night had cost in coordination: the four minutes, as he had said, and in the four minutes two convoys mistimed and a shell warning late to one district, and whether anyone had died of the four minutes they would not know for days, because the dead of a mistimed warning did not announce themselves as the dead of a mistiming; they were only dead, among the siege's dead, and no one would ever be able to say which of them he had killed by stealing a minute back. He had signed for the four minutes. He had said I'll take four minutes, and Maya had written it down, and now the four minutes had people in them, unknown people, and he would carry them without ever knowing their number, which was, he thought, the worst way to carry a cost, not as names, which the old man could sing, but as a figure that could not be resolved into names, four minutes of a city, somewhere, someone. He looked at his hands. He had kept a copy of everything, as always, and there was no copy that would tell him who the four minutes had cost, and there never would be.

The war cabinet met at seven. By noon Maitland had suspended stabilisation broadcasting pending review, over Pell's objection, and mass correction stopped. The fault was logged as a relay fault in the capital's time distribution, and nobody had yet said otherwise, and Nikolai did not know how long nobody would.

Wei, a clerk at Emergency Information told Maya afterwards, had had his light on all night. He had watched the carrier fall on his panel at twelve minutes past three, and he had made no call, and he had gone home at seven. Nikolai heard it secondhand and did not interpret it. A man who watched a thing fall and made no call was a man who had watched a thing fall and made no call, and whether that was consent or indifference or a third thing Nikolai could not see was not a thing Nikolai would guess at, because guessing at Wei was a way of being led by Wei. Reeves, the same clerk said, had asked for the logs at twenty past three, which was the first thing Reeves would do, because Reeves wanted the data, and now Reeves had his population waking up and would want to know exactly how it looked.

At noon Nikolai and Maya walked out of the vault, up onto the Meridian Bridge, into the cold, into a city that was frightened for the right reasons.

It was a city of people who knew they were under siege, who knew their sons were at the coast, who had wept for their dead and were not sure of anything. The queues were loud. People argued. A woman two days from the dose stood at the bridge rail and looked at the water and did not smile. It was the worst the city had looked since the ring closed, and it was the best, because it was awake, because every frightened arguing grieving person on the bridge was a person again, holding their own doubt in their own hand.

Nikolai looked at it, and looked at his hands, and said to Maya the thing that was true and would not come apart into one thing or the other.

"It's worse," he said. "It's better."
