Chapter 16: Not Before

Wednesday, April 9, 2110. Elena's farmhouse, the border zone

He had been seventy-two since March, and the war had come as far as the stove.

Mikhail Petrov opened the damper two fingers, so the crack in the stove door glowed and did not smoke, and put the potatoes on, and hummed, and listened to the shelling a long way east, over the capital, a sound you felt in the ground more than heard. The corridor road was under fire in daylight now; the only way between the besieged capital and the farmhouse was the western corridor that Dmitri's volunteer company had held since the ring closed, and it closed itself at dusk, and so anyone who came out from the city came by day and stayed the night or did not come at all.

Vance sat at the table in a blanket, seventy-four, very frail now. The tremor shook the cup even with both hands around it, and she had stopped pretending it did not; she let the tea slop onto the wood and did not reach to mop it; a person who has decided to stop hiding a thing has a kind of peace in it, and she had that peace, and he was glad of it for her and sorry for what it meant.

Kira came up from the capital a little after noon, by the western corridor, in a Commission car with a volunteer driver and a shrapnel scar across the door, and a message from Dmitri, formal and without a single contraction: the corridor closed at dusk, and she was to be back through his lines before then or not at all. She brought the capital in pieces, and the old man took each piece without comment, humming low.

The city calm under the guns, she said, which he had heard from the refugees and did not like. Pell's first schedule, eleven hundred names. Marcus at nought point five nine, so that he could not risk more than a day visit and today not even that. Kovač gone from the intake. She laid it out the way she laid out a fault, in order, and he stirred the pot and listened and said nothing, and when she had finished she asked him the thing she had been sent to ask, by Marcus and by the siege both.

"There's a page," she said. "Vance told Marcus. We need it."

Vance looked up from the cup.

Vance looked up from the cup.

She had heard Maitland, Kira had said; she heard the broadcasts; she sat at this table every evening and listened to a war come closer, and she knew, better than anyone in the house, what was in the Spire and what it was worth to an enemy who understood it, and she had been refusing the page since the autumn, since the first time Marcus asked, and she refused it again now, but with a thing added, a thing she had not said before.

"I will give it," she said, precisely, "when they try to give it away. Not before." A pause, while the tremor went through the cup. "If I give it now, you will use it to decide."

"Give what away," said Kira, levelly, which was how she pressed, without heat. "Who is they?"

Vance did not answer. She held the cup to still it, and looked at the window, and the answer did not come, and Kira, who knew when a system had returned all it would return, did not ask a second time. She filed it. The old man watched her file it, the small stillness of a person putting a thing in a place to come back to, and he hummed one phrase of the Lakeshore Air, neither the rising yes nor the falling no, only the phrase that said I am here and I am not going to pretend I understood that either.

The corridor closed after dark, so Kira stayed, and before the singing, while the light went, the old man sat with Kira at the kitchen table and listened to her account of the capital, and she told him a thing she had not told the others, which was that she had read her own January, and found her fear thinner, and could not tell whether it was courage or the carrier, and had decided to trust the page and not the feeling. He nodded at that, because it was the thing he had spent his life on, the trusting of the written and sung thing over the felt one, the keeping of a truth in a form the machines skated over; and he told her, which he rarely told anyone, that this was why the songs had lived, that a tune was a thing a correction skimmed because it was rote, and that she had found, under a broadcast, the same shelter he had found under a hood, which was a thing you had written down before they got to you, that your own hand from before was the one witness they could not reach. She wrote that down, in her incident format, trust the page, not the feeling, and he was glad, because a thing she wrote down was a thing kept, and he was old, and the keeping was passing to the ones who wrote.

In the evening he sang "The Names" in the barn, with the refugees gathered in their coats, and the new dead went into it one verse each: a Chronos orderly from the field hospital, the woman from the coast cell who had fallen in the rail yard, whose name Elena's runner had carried west, and the names from the siege. He sang only what the song held; the evening kept the refrain and not the names; the record may forget them, the song will not forget; name them, and they stay. Kira sang the refrain with the barn, low and off the note, and did not try to mend it. The song was as long as the dead, and getting longer, and Alexei was not in it yet, because Alexei was alive, in a ruined barn on the coast, with a burn on his arm, and the old man did not know that this was the last evening the song would not have him.

At night, when Kira had gone up and the barn had gone quiet, Vance asked him to sit, with the lamp low and the stove ticking down, and she spoke to him the way she had spoken to him across a reading-room table when they were young, precisely, in short sentences, a thing she had decided and would say once.

The rest of her copy, she said, was where she had left it. She would not write it down. "You taught them to write everything down," she said. "By many hands. This one I want kept the old way." And he understood that she meant him, that she was going to give him the one thing she would give no one else, in the one form she trusted, which was the form he had kept the world in for forty years: a thing said aloud, mouth to ear, kept by a man and not a record.

She said it once, slowly, in rhyme, and he did not move while she said it.

Look where they swept the shelf of us, behind the number no one reads; the old man peeled his apple there — ask him the number. Ask him twice.

He said it back to her, once, word for word, because that was how you took a thing that was given mouth to ear; you gave it back, so the giver knew it had landed whole. She nodded.

"You understand," she said, "why I give it to you and not to them. They write everything down now, by many hands, which is right, which is the thing I should have done in 'sixty-six and did not. But a thing written down can be found, and this must not be found, not yet, not by the wrong hand, and there is only one way I know to keep a thing that cannot be written and cannot be found, and it is your way. You hum it. You do not look at it. You will have it in a year the way you have had everything for forty years, and when the hand that should find it asks you, you will know, because you have kept it cold and whole, and a thing kept cold and whole tells you, in the end, who it was for." She paused. "I learned that from you, Mikhail, across that table, before I learned anything worth knowing, and I did not use it for forty-four years, and I am using it now."

And for one line she was twenty-seven again, with ink on her fingers, arguing with him across the long table while Kerr pretended not to hear: "It scans," she said. "You always said mine didn't." And then she was not twenty-seven. "For when I am not here to be asked," she said, and that was the whole of it.

He did not open it. He had every word of it, and he let none of them open. He had forty years' practice at hearing a thing and not looking at it, because what you looked at got heat, and heat was what the machines searched for, and though there was no machine in this kitchen there was the habit, deep as breathing, of keeping a dangerous thing cold by not turning it over. He did not guess at the shelf, or the number, or the old man, or the apple. He did not follow any line of it into any memory it might have touched. He took it the way he took a tune, whole and unexamined, and set it down in himself beside the tunes, where it would keep without his looking at it, because a tune you do not stare at is a tune you still have in forty years, and a thing you worry at is a thing that wears away.

At twenty to one he went out into the yard, into the cold, with real fog coming off the lake and the far guns quiet for once, and he fitted the four lines to the Lakeshore Air.

It was the tune that already carried the ferry verses his grandmother sang, and the lullaby a dead mother sang to a boy who became a commissioner, and the essay a frightened young politician wrote and a young archivist hid in a melody so that the words would never be lost. It carried this too, easily, the way a strong old tune carries whatever a man needs it to carry, and when he had it fitted he sang it under his breath, with words, because here in the yard he was free, and a thing sung in freedom with words was a thing kept the way he meant to keep it.

He sang it through once more, alone in the fog, so that he would not lose it. The last line lifted at the end, as every phrase of that tune lifted, like a question. Nobody in the house heard it. He did not try to answer it. He did not try to work out what it asked. It was Vance's, given to him the old way, and he would hold it the old way, cold and whole and unexamined, until the day someone needed it and he would hear, then, where it pointed; and that day was not this day, and he went in, and banked the stove, and slept with four lines of a dead woman's riddle kept safe in him by the one method a lifetime had taught him, which was not to look at them at all.
