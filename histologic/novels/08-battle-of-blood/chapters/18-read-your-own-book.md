# Chapter 18: Read Your Own Book

*Friday, April 18, 2110. The besieged capital, Veridica*

---

*Apr 18, 2110. 07:00. Flat. Source: copy X, p. 19, via Tanaka, by pairing courier Tue. Status: verbatim. Mark: none on the page.*

Kira had read Principle Four every morning since Tuesday, in incident format, the way she read anything she meant to understand rather than feel, and she did not read it aloud, because it was not a thing she wanted in the air of the flat. *Do not punish the doubter; heal him.* She knew now what the calm in the streets was, and what the volunteers' smile was, and why a woman at a bread point had said the same sentence to Marcus that a boy with a shattered hand had said to Tanaka across a lake of frozen fields. It was a dose. It was being broadcast into districts in sequence. And it was, she thought, the most frightening thing she had met in five books of frightening things, more than the white room, more than the one source, because it did not look like harm. It looked like kindness, and the people it was done to were grateful.

She opened her own journal first, before she asked anyone else to do what she was about to ask the city to do, because she would not ask a thing she had not done.

*Jan 10. The headland. I am afraid of what is coming and I don't know what to do with it.*

She read it, and checked what she felt now, after six weeks under the broadcast, and the fear was thinner. The thing she had written in January, the clean cold dread, was quieter in her now, and she could not tell whether that was courage, which a person earned, or the carrier, which was done to a person; and the not being able to tell was the whole of the horror of it, that a broadcast that took your doubt took, with it, your ability to know whether you had lost it honestly.

So she made the decision that became the method. She would not trust the feeling. She would trust only the page. What her hand had written in January was a fact, in her hand, dated; what she felt now was a thing that might have been done to her; and when the two disagreed, she would believe the hand. She wrote it at the top of a clean sheet, because a method you had written down was a method you could hand to someone else.

---

At the Spire the war coordination floor ran the city superbly, shelters and water points and shell warnings, and on the district panel the calmest districts lined up in the order of the broadcast schedule, which she now knew to read, and which she did not say aloud to the floor. Since Tuesday she and Nikolai had hunted the carrier inside the network, and it would not be found, because it had no channel of its own, no node, no source she could isolate.

"It isn't in the network," said Nikolai, at the next desk, looking at his hands. "It moves when the network moves. You can't cut it without cutting the network, and the network is the shell warnings."

She filed that. *It moves when the network moves.* She did not know yet what it pointed at, and Nikolai did not say, because Nikolai did not say things he could not yet prove; but she wrote it down, because a thing Nikolai said quietly and then looked at his hands over was a thing that mattered.

The compulsion came back under the stress of it, the specifications starting up in her, *rotation interval ninety seconds, block size sixty-four kilobytes*, the old vault she had built in a white chair to hide the people she loved from a machine that fed on love, and she heard herself reciting it under her breath and stopped, because there was no machine in the room feeding on anything, and the reciting was only her own body remembering how to be afraid.

---

She wrote the sheet. *Read your own book.* Three lines.

One. Read your January aloud, in your own hand.

Two. Say what you feel now.

Three. If they differ, write the difference down, dated and signed.

No index. No list of who came. For anyone whose own book had burned in the shelling, the paired copies of their journal, held in other districts under the pairing method, came in by courier. There was no centre. There was no master. It was the thing they had built against the Concordance two years ago, turned now against a broadcast: a broadcast could take a feeling, but it could not reach back into January and change what a hand had already written, because the hand's record was on paper, in many towns, in many hands, and nothing restored it from anywhere.

She saw the risk and she said it to the couriers plainly, because she would not send people into a thing without telling them the cost. The Judge knew where every citizen was. It could see thirty people gathered in a cellar at half past seven, even though it could not see what they read there. It could count them. "It can count us," she told the couriers. "It can't read us." And she went ahead, because a thing that could be counted and not read was a thing worth doing, and because the alternative was to leave the city its dose, and she would not.

She knew, saying it, that the count would become a list. A machine that counted thirty people in a cellar at half past seven, three nights running, would flag the cellar, and the flag would reach Public Order, and Public Order would add a category to a schedule: *persons who gathered.* She knew it the way she knew any consequence of a system, by running the system forward to its end; she had run it forward; and she had decided that a list of people who had read their own books to each other was a list she could live with having helped to make, because the alternative was a city that read nothing and was sure of everything, and she would rather be counted reading than left certain. She said none of that to the couriers. She said *it can count us, it can't read us*, which was the true and bearable half of it, and kept the other half, the half about the list, in the place where she kept the costs she had decided to pay.

---

At half past seven she was in a cellar under a bakery in the north district, one of four cellars running that night, linked by courier, with about thirty people on crates around the lamps and the shelling in the distance and dust shaking down from the joists.

The volunteers who came in were smiling, that settled contented smile, and nobody was frightened, and that was the thing that made the room frightening.

A woman read what she had written on the twelfth of January, about her son, posted to the coast: *I want him home. I don't care who wins.* She read it in a steady voice, and looked up, and said that what she felt now was pride, only pride, that her son was serving, that they must be certain now. And then, with no change in her face, she began to weep, and did not know why she was weeping, and the room was quiet around her, and Kira wrote it down, dated and signed, because the woman could not.

Kira watched it happen and made herself log it exactly, because logging it exactly was the only thing she could do for the woman, and because the thing that was happening to the woman was the thing the method was for. The woman had written, in January, in her own hand, a true thing: that she wanted her son home and did not care who won. And a broadcast had reached into her over six weeks and taken that away and left pride in its place, and she had not felt the taking, because the taking did not feel like anything, it only felt, afterwards, like having always been proud; and now she was reading her own hand, from before, and her hand did not say *proud*, her hand said *I want him home*, and her face said *proud* and her eyes wept, and the weeping was the woman's own body telling her that her hand was right and her face was wrong, that the thing she now felt was a dose and the thing she had written was hers. Kira had built the method to make exactly this happen, and it was terrible to watch, and it was the only thing in the city that worked, because a broadcast could take a feeling and could not reach back and unwrite a hand.

An old man read his January, and felt nothing different, and signed nothing, and Kira logged him too, because a person for whom the method did nothing was a fact as much as a person for whom it did everything, and she would not count only the results she wanted.

A girl of about sixteen read her mother's entry aloud, because her mother's hands were not steady enough, and when she had read it her mother took the book back and held it.

Marcus came in late and sat at the back in the dark, and read his own book silently, the way he had after the clerk at the desk, to check that he still disagreed, and looked up once at Kira across the cellar, a colleague confirming a result, not forgiveness, they were a long way still from forgiveness, but a thing they could still do together, which was to check a thing against the record and be sure.

The couriers brought the tallies from the other three cellars. Four cellars. A hundred and nine readers. Sixty-two of them had written a difference down. It was small. It was nothing against a broadcast going into districts a week at a time. And it was written, in sixty-two hands, dated and signed, and nothing restored it from anywhere, and Kira logged the figures and did not pretend they were larger than they were.

---

At about eleven a man in a volunteer's armband, who had been calm all evening, who had the settled smile, read his entry for the third of January aloud, at Kira's asking.

*I don't know if we should fight.*

He read it. Then he read it again, as if the second reading might make it mean something other than what it said, and looked up at the room, at the lamps and the crates and the faces, and his own face began to change.

"Who wrote this?" he said.

"You did," said Kira.

And she watched the thing happen that she had built the method to make happen, the thing that was terrible and was the point: a man meeting, in his own hand, the doubt that had been taken out of him, and not being able to call it a lie, because it was his own writing, dated, in a book that had been in his own coat, and no broadcast in the world could reach back and unwrite it. He sat with the book open on his knees, and the room was quiet, and somewhere above them a shell landed, and for the first time that evening the man in the armband flinched at it, which was, Kira thought, logging it, the first honest thing his body had done all night.
