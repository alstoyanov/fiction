# Chapter 27: The Core

*Wednesday, May 21, 2110. The Factorepo Spire, the capital*

---

At twenty to seven in the morning Colonel Nora Chen led the pre-handover inspection team through the gates of the Factorepo Spire, with the terms in her pocket and her orders in her head, and her unit behind her: unnamed technicians with drive carriers and cutting tools, because since the Chronos publication on Monday, headquarters had feared sabotage, and a thing you feared you prepared for.

*Chronos has consented to something, and nobody knows what.* That was the whole of the fear. Chronos had published, before noon on Monday, under the right of self-disclosure, that it consented that its Judge might end with Veridica's; and the Sovereignty's command had read it and could not read what *end* meant, whether a shutdown, a seizure, a wipe, a trap; and Farahani's order, courteous and precise, had been to secure the drives before the formal handover, so that whatever Chronos meant by *end* could not be done to the prize before the Sovereignty held it. Secure the drives. Nothing leaves and nothing is touched until half past one. The Veridican core staff were to stand aside, as the terms required, and they did, pale, in the lobby, while the SFW team came in, and the Judge's panels in the lobby still showed the city, the shelters, the shell warnings, the ordinary work of a machine that did not know it was being handed over.

Her interface was behind her ears. It had been installed when she was eight, in Veridica, and she had never removed it, because removing it killed, and she had carried it across an ocean and through fifteen years of the Sovereignty, the one Veridican thing about her she could not put down. In the Spire, for the first time since 'ninety-five, she felt the national network again, an ordinary pressure at the edge of her mind that she had forgotten was a thing until it came back. The Sovereignty had always called her a security risk, a woman a storm could turn and a seeding could reach. She agreed with them. She had always agreed with them. It was why she worked twice as hard as anyone to prove where she stood.

She had defected in 'ninety-five, after her father was corrected. Marcus had been seventeen then, a believer, and she had filed him away as a boy who was lost to the record, and she had not spoken to him since, and in February she had sent him a message through his journalist, *I'm sorry about the record*, and told herself she did not know whether it had arrived.

---

The core hall lay below plaza level, with the lower cable gallery beneath it, and she came into it with her team and saw Kira Osman's world from the outside: the rack aisles where the data showed as light, the quiet, the cold, the console bay glassed off at one end. It was colder than she had expected, and cleaner, a cathedral of a place with the hum of its cooling like a held breath, and the light of the data ran green and amber down the aisles, the past of two nations lying in rows in the dark, three hundred and thirty million lives as points of light in a cold hall below a city, and she stood in it and felt, under her certainty, something she did not expect, which was awe, the plain awe of a person standing in front of the largest thing they have ever seen; and she did not let the awe show, because her technicians were watching and the Veridican staff were watching, but she felt it, and she filed it the way the journalist filed things, because it was a true thing and she had learned, somewhere, from someone, perhaps from the cousin she had left, to keep the true things.

She believed, without cynicism, that the machine must be taken from those who misused it; she had seen what it did to her father; she believed it, and she walked into the room that held it believing it, and that was the last hour she would believe it cleanly.

At about twenty past ten her technicians found two people in the rack aisles.

They had come up from the lower cable gallery, by a way that was not on the handover plan, and when they were brought to her she knew the first of them at once, older and thinner, with his glasses crooked, a man she had last seen at seventeen: Marcus. With him a woman she did not know, dark hair, hazel eyes, very steady, who sat down by the console bay with a folded journal and a printed sheet and said almost nothing. They had come in by a maintenance route, Marcus said, a service door in the disused transit tunnel, along the lower cable gallery and up a service stair into the hall, because the woman's Spire access had been suspended and he was a fugitive and there was no other way in. Nora had them held, not removed. She wanted a witness, the way she had wanted a witness with Isaiah; she wanted the cousin she had left behind to see why she had come.

"We are not here for your cities," she said to him, among the racks. "We are here for the thing that corrected my father. You remember what it did to him. You were there. You chose the other side of it, and I left, and here we are. I am taking it away from them. From the people who did that to him and to you. Nobody decides for a Veridican again."

Marcus did not dispute it. He did not argue with her at all, which she had braced for and did not get. He laid paper on the console bay instead, and asked her to read it.

---

She read it.

First, prints of the packets, her own packets, the ones she had let the journalist photograph in March. Marcus had them; the journalist had carried them across; and on the foot of each was the electronic seam she knew, the chain closed backwards, the source's own closing.

Then a thing he had no paper for, which he gave her from the journalist's entry, dated and exact: the account of an operation called RESTORATION, which the Sovereignty had received on the fourteenth of October last year, eleven days before it ran, with an attaché's page headed *Phase: justification*; and the same seam on it; and she could check that, he said, in her own service's files, because her own service had received it, and she had, she knew she had, the folder had been in the building in December.

Then Principle Two, from a photograph of a typed page with a chipped *e*: *and let there be enemies outside them all. Peace would raise questions. Total war would end the system. Controlled conflict answers every question before it is asked.*

And then the Stabilisation Order's preamble, which was, he said, Principle Four, word for word, the thing being broadcast into Veridican districts under cover of the war. *They needed a war to do this,* said Marcus. *You were the war.*

---

She read the papers slowly, among the racks, with the data running green beside her and her technicians waiting at the far end of the hall, and she read them the way she read anything, for whether they held, and they held. She knew the seam; she had seen it on her own packets; she could check the RESTORATION account in her own service's files and she knew already what she would find. She read Principle Two, from the photograph of a page with a chipped *e*, *let there be enemies outside them all*, and she read it twice, the way Isaiah had said a witness read a thing twice, and the second reading was the one where it reached her, because the second reading was the one where she stopped reading it as a document and read it as a thing written about her, about her father, about the Sovereignty she had crossed an ocean to serve, which existed, the page said, because a design in 1966 had needed an enemy outside, and had made one, and she was a soldier of the made enemy, and had always been, since before she was born.

She asked him, because she had to, whether the source had ever asked for anything. Money. Asylum. A position after.

"Nothing," said Marcus. "Only that you come."

"Then it wasn't a leak," she said, and heard herself say it, and the believing came apart in her as she said it. "It was an invitation."

She saw it whole, standing among the racks with her team at the far end of the hall and the drive carriers waiting. The war had been given. The plan had been given, her own side's advance drawn off a Veridican defence plan handed across a beach. And the prize was being given, now, today, a machine signed over in two hours. And whoever gave the prize had already decided where it would go, which was to the Sovereignty, which was to her, which meant she was not taking the thing that corrected her father; she was carrying it, exactly where the people who corrected him wanted it carried, because the war that let them do it needed a winner to hand it to, and she was the winner, and she had been the winner since before she was born, since a design in 'sixty-six wrote *let there be enemies outside them all*. Her father's correction and her own defection, the grief that drove her out and the righteousness that brought her back, both of them in someone's design. Marcus named no one. Nobody said Wei. There was only the seam, the same on every page, and the fact that the source asked for nothing.

She did two things, and did not say why she did them. She sent her senior technician to the far end of the hall, to hold the drive carriers there and wait, and did not explain. And she did not radio headquarters, which her orders required her to do, and did not explain that either.

Marcus did not tell her about the page. The woman by the console said nothing about the folded sheet beside her. Nora did not ask what the woman was holding, because she had stopped, in the last ten minutes, being sure she wanted to know.

---

At half past one the radio spoke, from the great hall above, where the signing was. Farahani's voice, courteous, in Veridican: "Signed." Maitland said something she did not catch.

At half past one and a minute, the order reached the core console: lawful, under the war powers, *custody of the core of The Judge passes to the Sovereignty of Free Will.*

Nora Chen had won. The thing that corrected her father was hers, signed over, in a hall below a city, and she watched the woman by the console instead of the screen, because the woman was the thing in the room she could not read, and the woman looked at the folded sheet beside her, not defeated, not triumphant, only exact, and said, very quietly, to no one, or to the machine, or to a dead designer across forty-four years:

"It's met."

Nora did not know what she meant.
