# Chapter 15: Siege

*Friday, April 4, 2110. The besieged capital, Veridica*

---

*Apr 4, 2110. Capital. Status: encircled (since Wed Apr 2). Western corridor to the lakeshore: open, under fire, held by Volkov, D. (volunteer company).*

Marcus wrote it at the kitchen table in pencil, before it was light, while the shelling came and went to the east, intermittent and conventional, the SFW's guns on the ring feeling along the districts. Kira had gone to the Spire at half past five. The ring had closed on Wednesday, and there was one road still open, west, toward the lakeshore, under fire, held by Dmitri's volunteer company; and a city with one road open was a city on a timer, and everyone in it knew the timer was running, and nobody, going out into the streets, seemed afraid.

That was the thing he had come to check. He went out into the cold to see the siege with his own eyes, because a siege on a screen was a thing someone had chosen to show you.

The Judge was running the shelters street by street, and the assignments were good; he watched a block empty into a cellar ahead of a round that landed where the block had been, the timing exact, the machine moving people out of the path of guns it could not see by the holes they tore in the telemetry. And the city was calm. The queues were orderly, and nobody pushed. The volunteers handing out bread and water were smiling, too many of them, all in the same way, a particular settled contented smile, and nobody in any queue was asking what happened next, which was the one question a besieged city asked, and it was not being asked.

A woman at a bread point gave him a loaf and said, pleasantly, "We must be certain now."

He had heard that sentence twice this week, from people who did not know each other, in the same words. He filed it under UNCHECKED: *the same words, three people.* He did not know yet that Tanaka had it in forty-one files across a lake of frozen fields; he knew only that a city under siege was repeating one sentence, and that a city that repeats one sentence is a city that has been given one, and he did not have the rest of it, and he marked the place where the rest of it would go.

He watched the queues for a while, because watching was the job, and the watching was worse than the shelling. A city under siege ought to be afraid. Fear was the correct response to a ring of guns and one road out; fear was health; a frightened city was a city still in possession of itself. This city was not afraid. It queued, and smiled, and shared, and did not ask what happened when the road closed, and the not-asking was the thing, because a besieged city that did not ask what happened next was a city whose doubt had been taken from it, and a city without doubt was a city that would do whatever it was told, calmly, gratefully, to the end. He thought of the field hospital on the eastern edge, and of Kira's note about reserved broadcast blocks, and of a man released from prison to direct a broadcast, and he did not yet have the one thing that joined them, and he walked on through the smiling queues with the loaf in his hand and his own doubt held tight, the last man on the street, it felt like, who was afraid.

---

At ten the war cabinet met, in the room below the First Minister's office, with the map panel showing the ring and the one western corridor and the dead so far as a number, modest and real, and growing.

Maitland was grieving and practical, a man keeping a city alive with his hands. Chen Zhao had his glass of tea, warm and grave. Wei was at the side table with his tablet. Pell had a folder.

Pell tabled the first schedule under the order. About eleven hundred names from the occupied coast, people who had accepted the Sovereignty's paper, and a second category marked *under review*: journal keepers. Marcus was not on it. Some of the names were depositors from Kovač's intake, and he noted that her refusal had delayed the transfer by an afternoon and changed nothing else, and that she had been right that it would, and had done it anyway.

Marcus argued against the order, plainly, in order, the way he argued, not making a speech, because a speech was a thing you could be moved to discount and a plain argument in order was harder to answer. The journals were the one record the enemy could not touch and the state could not adjust. "You are listing the people who write things down," he said. Ashford was on the eastern edge, beside the Chronos hospital the SFW had been shelling. He asked what the stabilisation broadcasts carried.

Wei answered, courteous, from the side table. "Reassurance, mostly. People have been very frightened, Marcus." And then, lightly, one beat of excess: "You should rest. Your numbers are up." The registry had not shown it yet. Marcus noted that Wei knew it before the registry did, and filed it, and said nothing.

Chen Zhao, fatherly, with his tea, championed the thing he called "restorative stabilisation," his one *restore* of the morning. "Nobody is being punished, cousin. It is a kindness. We are holding people's hands through a thing nobody should have to live through." Marcus heard the word *punished*, and heard, under it, the title Kovač had told him the file bore, *Correction Over Punishment*, and said nothing, because he did not have the text and would not pretend to.

Maitland closed it. "I have a city to keep alive," he said. "If it is calm while I do it, I will take calm. Argue it again when the ring is off." The order stood.

---

He thought, sitting in the cabinet, of what it would mean to argue and keep arguing. The index measured contamination, and the index was computed from the record, and the record agreed with the state, and so a man who argued against the state rose in the index for arguing, which meant that the measure of a man's wrongness was, exactly, the distance between the man and the government, dressed up as a distance between the man and the truth; and he had known that for years, had faced it in a white room, *anything corrupted by people can be corrected by people*; and he had thought the Commission and the Doctrine had put a check on it; and here it was again, under war powers, with the check suspended, the old machine that made disagreement into illness, running on a city. He argued anyway. It was the thing he was for. He had learned that a man who stopped arguing because arguing raised his index was a man who had let the index win, and he would not let it win, and he knew what it would cost, and he argued.

At twenty past one the index moved.

The Judge weighed his words in cabinet against a record that agreed with the state, and Kira sent him the public registry figure without comment, by the internal line: nought point five nine, up from nought point five seven. Custody was anything above nought point six. He wrote it down. His POV lost a word somewhere in the writing of it, the word for a number that is also a distance, the measure of how close a thing has come, and he reached for it and found the smooth place and let it go, because it was a word he could do without.

Leaving the cabinet room, a Public Order officer at the door looked at him a beat too long, and Marcus felt his own feet want to go faster, and did not let them. He did not run. It was an old rule, from a café by a fountain and a corridor in a Ministry, and the rule was that running proved the charge, that a man who ran was a man who had told the record he was guilty. He walked out at an ordinary pace, with the officer's eyes on his back, and it cost him something to walk, and he walked.

---

In the afternoon he went back by the Meridian Bridge, where the Directorate's windows were lit in daylight, and thought of Maya's note and of Isaiah's letter still in his coat, *Someone in Veridica wants to be invaded*, and of a cold house in a quiet district with two glasses and an open bottle, which Maitland had told him about on the eleventh of March and which he had not let himself close the question of. He had known, for three weeks, that the plan had leaked from the former Chancellor's table. He had not let himself finish the thought that led from *the plan leaked from the highest table* to *someone wanted the war* to *someone in the cabinet*, because the thought led into the room he sat in every morning, and he did not yet know which chair.

At ten past six, at the Cabinet Office front desk, the records clerk who had signed him in since 2107, a careful anxious man who had once apologised to Marcus for a smudged time on a form, signed him out, and said, without being asked, glowing:

"I've never been so sure of anything."

---

At half past eleven, in the flat, with Kira still on shift, Marcus took out his own journal and opened it at January, at the entries he had written before the landing, in his small pencil hand.

He read them slowly, one by one, to check that he still disagreed with the clerk at the desk, with the woman at the bread point, with the whole calm smiling city. He read his own doubt, written down in his own hand in January, when he had still had it clean. He found that he still had it. He was not sure of anything, and he was glad, in a way that frightened him, to be not sure, because being not sure was the one thing the city had lost and he had kept, and he could feel the calm pressing at the edges of him even as he read, the broadcast reaching him the way it reached everyone, and the only thing holding it off was a page of his own handwriting that said, in January, *I don't know.*

He wrote the day's entry. Then he read that one twice too, because a thing he had written today under the broadcast was a thing he no longer fully trusted himself to have written honestly, and he had learned that the only defence against a broadcast that took your doubt was to doubt even the record of your own doubt, to check your own hand against your own earlier hand, to be, for the duration, two men, one of them in January, keeping watch on the other.
