# Chapter 28: Truth Endures

*Wednesday, May 21, 2110. The Factorepo Spire, the capital*

---

*13:31. Custody order registered. Source: First Minister, war powers. Receiving authority: Sovereignty of Free Will.*

Kira wrote it in her incident format, on the console bay's paper pad, by hand, because she wanted a record of this that did not live in the thing she was about to end.

Under the order, on the console, was the field that every order touching the core carried, that had always been there, that was not a prompt and not a trap but simply how the core had worked for forty-four years: *Concurrence.* It was empty, and it waited, and she did not touch it yet.

Between half past one and thirty-five minutes past, the order propagated through the core, and she watched it as routine, the way she had watched a thousand propagations, legs of the join lighting as the order reached them. She did not know, watching, that in those same four minutes a third leg of the join opened too, and carried a full restore out to a place that was not on any map, and completed at thirty-five minutes past, invisible on the console she sat at; she would not know it for a week; the log of it survived somewhere she was not looking, and she was looking at the field marked *Concurrence*, and at Marcus, who finished reading Chronos's consent aloud into the core's order log at thirty-five minutes past, word for word, with its nanosecond timestamp and both names, Aubry's and Julian's, in front of Nora's technicians, so that it would be in the record that Chronos had been asked and had answered. Then he read Julian's line to himself, the line below the signatures, *this time you asked*, and did not say it aloud, and folded the sheet.

---

Nikolai had patched her handset to the receiver on the farmhouse dresser, so that the farmhouse could hear the Spire and she could hear the farmhouse, and she lifted the handset and said, to the farmhouse, "It's asking."

Vance's voice came back, thin, over the line, with Old Songs silent beside her.

"I didn't tell you so that you would ask me."

Kira looked at Marcus. He did not answer for her. "It's yours," he said, because it was; Chronos had been asked and had consented; the terms were signed and the city would have its ceasefire; and the only thing left in the whole of it that was undecided was a hand on a field, and the hand was hers, because a dead woman had chosen forty-four years ago that it would be someone's who had not decided, and Kira was the one who had not decided, who had only to concur or not.

She thought of the scope indicator in October, climbing past everything she wrote. One source. Everything that restored from it ending with it. A hundred and fifty million people across a lake, asked this time, and consenting. Her own capital, and Chronos's cities, and two Judges, and forty-four years of two nations deciding by a machine, all of it ending because a First Minister had signed to save a city and a woman at a console typed four sentences into a field.

In October she had held an abort she could not use, and had taken her hand off it and let a thing run that she had not chosen, because stopping it would have been worse; and that had been the worst moment of her life, to let a thing run that was not hers. This was the opposite, and she made herself feel the difference, because the difference was the whole of what two books had taught her. This was hers. There was no abort, and she did not want one. Chronos had been asked; the consent was on the console beside her, published, timestamped, impossible to forge; Maitland had decided to give the thing away and she was only concurring; and the concurrence was a thing she had chosen, with Chronos's yes in her hand and a dead woman's hand guiding her own, the one decision in forty-four years of this machine that had been asked of the people it would fall on and answered by them. She was not letting a thing run. She was ending a thing, on purpose, with consent, and that was a different act entirely, and she let herself know it was different, and typed.

At forty minutes past one she typed the front of the page into the field, word for word, from *The Judge must never become a weapon* to *the one I did not tell.* Her hands were steady. She had held an abort once that she could not use; this was the opposite; there was no abort, and she did not want one; the thing she did now she had chosen, with Chronos's consent beside her and Maitland's signature in the log and a dead designer's hand guiding her own, and she typed it to the end, and the field accepted it, and there was no confirmation and no undo. The cascade began. It could not be reversed. She logged it: *13:40. Concurrence: Osman, K. Cascade: begun.*

"What have you done," said Nora Chen, who had understood, a beat too late, what the woman by the console was holding.

"Nothing," said Kira. "Your general signed. I agreed."

The Sovereignty's radio came alive, headquarters ordering the drives cut out now, whatever was left of them, and Nora's technicians at the far end of the hall moved for the carriers and the cutting tools.

---

The cascade was not a bomb. There was no explosion. The light in the racks went out from inside, row by row, the data going dark where it lay; and then heat came off the racks, and a smell of burning storage, and the core began to burn out from within, as the front of the page had said it would, *a cascade that cannot be reversed*, consuming itself. On the console the join's display showed its legs going dark one by one, and Kira counted them dark as they went, because counting was what she had. At forty-four minutes past one the last leg went dark like the others, and she did not look at it twice, and did not know that it had gone dark not because the cascade had ended it but because there was nothing on it left to end, that it had emptied itself nine minutes before; she counted it dark with the rest and turned away.

The technicians had to come back through the hall's main door to reach the burning racks with their tools, and Nora stood in the doorway and held it against her own men.

Marcus moved to take it from her, because he held doors; it was the oldest thing about him; he held doors so that others could run. "Not you," said Nora, and there was the fire in the aisles behind her, the heat coming off the racks, and she stood in the doorway with her own technicians on the far side of it and held it, because the thing had to burn and they would stop it burning if they reached it, and she had stopped, in the last hour, believing in the thing she had crossed an ocean to take.

"Go," she said, to Marcus. "I'm sorry about the record."

It was the message she had sent through Isaiah in February, said now in person, at last, across a core that was burning; and Kira understood, watching it, that Nora was not holding the door to save the cascade, which needed no saving, which could not be reversed; she was holding it to keep her own men from dying in a fire for a thing that was already lost; she had worked out, in the last ten minutes, that the prize was gone, that there was nothing behind the door now worth a technician's life, and she was holding the door so that the technicians would fall back and live, against their own orders, against her own orders, a colonel keeping her men out of a burning room by standing in its doorway; and it was the cleanest thing Kira had seen anyone do in the whole war, and it was being done by a woman who had crossed an ocean to take the thing that was burning, and who had understood at the last hour whose war she had fought, and who would not come out of the doorway, because a door held is a door held, which was a thing Marcus understood, which was why he moved to take it from her, and why she would not let him.

Kira took Marcus's arm, and they went, out the way they had come, down the service stair to the lower cable gallery and on toward the transit-tunnel door, and behind them the technicians fell back from the fire as it came up the aisles, and Nora did not fall back, and the fire took the room.

---

On the service stair there was a Judge panel, on the landing, like every Judge panel in every home and court and checkpoint and Chronos kitchen in two nations; and as they passed it, it changed register for the last time.

There was no case on it. No probability. No timestamp. The panel that had shown nothing but determinations for forty-four years, odds and findings and the cold exact arithmetic of a machine, showed, at the last, a thing that was not a determination at all, a thing people would argue about for decades, whether a mind had written it or whether it was only the last line the designer left in the thing to be shown when it ended:

*Truth endures. I do not need to.*

Kira, who knew the architecture better than almost anyone alive, who had built parts of the Spire and read the source's own index, did not know whether a mind had written it. She read it on the landing, with the heat behind her and Marcus's arm under her hand, and she did not know, and she thought that not knowing was perhaps the truest thing the machine had ever made her feel, because for forty-four years it had never once left anyone not knowing, and now, ending, it had.

Over the handset, from the farmhouse, one voice was humming the Lakeshore Air. It was Vance; Kira knew it by the tremor in it, the hum shaking the way the cup shook; and the tune lifted at the end, like a question, and Old Songs was silent beside her, letting the designer hum her own machine out.

---

At fourteen hundred every panel in two nations was dark.

In Veridica and in Chronos, in the homes and the courts and the checkpoints and the Chronos kitchens, no machine decided anything. The thing that had decided for three hundred and thirty million people for forty-four years was ash in a hall below a city, and a woman who had come to take it was dead in the doorway of it, and the record of two nations was gone, and the people would have to decide for themselves, now, what had happened, by many hands, with no machine to tell them.

Kira stood on the service stair, with the page and the consent folded inside her journal, and wrote the last entry, by hand, in the dark, by the light of the dead panel.

*14:00. Judges: none.*

She did not write a status. There was no status. There was no machine left to assign one, and that was the whole of what they had done, and she left the field as it was, open, because an open field was the truest thing she had to write, and she had learned, from a doctor across a lake, that there were some lines you did not close until you could close them true.
