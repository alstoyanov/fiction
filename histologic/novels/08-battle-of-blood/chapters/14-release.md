# Chapter 14: Release

*Saturday, March 29, 2110. The capital, Veridica*

---

At twelve minutes and forty seconds past seven in the morning, Maya Reeves watched the Public Order list of releases under war powers go up on the public registry panel in the street below the flat, and she timestamped it, because she timestamped everything, and because a thing she had written the time against was a thing that had happened and could not be made not to have happened later.

The list was flat and administrative, names and reasons, the state doing its paperwork in public because the Doctrine said the record was open. She read down it, and near the bottom, between a quartermaster released to a supply post and a surgeon released to a hospital, she found the line.

*REEVES, Dr. Released under war powers (Order of 17 March 2110) to direct stabilisation broadcasting. Posting: National Broadcast Directorate.*

There was no first name. There never was. She had never in twenty-eight years seen her father's first name on a document, because he did not use it and had taught her, long before she understood it was strange, that a man's name was a thing he signed and he never signed anything.

She read the line twice. *Released under war powers. To direct stabilisation broadcasting.* The man who had done the worst thing in her life was out of prison, and he was out to do the work the state needed most, and she did not yet have a word for what the work was, only the shape of a cold that went through her as it had gone through her on the ninth floor of the Regional Factorepo three winters ago, the feeling of a stair missing in the dark.

---

She had last seen him three weeks ago, on the first of March, her regular monthly visit. He had been thinner, and courteous, and very interested in the war. He had asked her whether the coast had fallen "cleanly," using the word the way he used all his words, precisely, with a professional's interest in whether a thing had been done well. She had not answered. She found now, trying to remember the visit, that she could not remember whether he had smiled, and the not-remembering frightened her more than it should, because she had spent twelve years remembering exactly what her father was, and a gap in the remembering was a gap she did not trust.

She had visited him every month for three years, in the clean warm visiting room with its one bare tree in the yard, and every month he had been courteous and interested and pleased to see her, and every month he had told her about the letter opener, the thing he believed he had done to Singh, the seeded memory that was not his, and she had learned not to argue with it, because arguing with a seeded memory was arguing with a man about a thing that had been written into him and he could not unwrite. She had gone, month after month, because he was her father, and because a man no one visited was a man the world had decided to stop being troubled by, and she would not decide that about him even though he had given her every reason to. And now he was out, and the world needed him, needed the one thing he knew how to do, and the visiting room was empty on the first Saturday of the month for the first time in three years, and she did not know yet whether she would be glad of the empty Saturday or whether she would miss even the letter opener.

Nikolai came in off the night shift at half past eight, gray and quiet, and she showed him the line, and he went still, the Volkov way of going still that she had learned to read as listening with the whole body. He told her, low, about the reserved broadcast blocks he and Kira had seen on the eighteenth, *Civil stabilisation, district sequence*, from the Directorate, from the twenty-fourth of March. Neither of them said what they were afraid it was. They did not have the words for it. They had a father released to run a broadcast, and a broadcast reserved under a label nobody would question, and a cold going through both of them, and no name for the thing yet, and they sat at the kitchen table with it between them and did not name it, because naming a thing you could not yet prove was the beginning of becoming the kind of person who named things they could not prove.

---

At half past nine she walked to the Meridian Bridge, where the National Broadcast Directorate stood, a pale building on the water, with Public Order at the gate.

The queue for the water point on the bridge approach was patient and orderly, and the people in it were smiling, and she noticed that she had noticed it, and did not know why she had noticed it, and wrote it in her head under the date without knowing what it was. People in a queue under a siege, smiling. She filed it the way she filed a timestamp she could not yet place.

At the gate she gave the guard her Commission pass, and her father's name, and her own, and the guard was calm and kind and told her, with real gentleness, that her name was not on any list and that she could not go in, and that it was for her own peace of mind. *For your own peace of mind.* She heard the phrase and timed her own reaction to it, which was a thing she did, and noted that it had taken her a second and a half to understand that the guard was not being cruel, that the guard believed it, that the guard had been told the building did a kindness and had no reason to doubt it, because the guard, like the people in the water queue, like the whole smiling bridge, was calm in the particular settled way she had been noticing for a week and had no word for yet.

She waited on the pavement for two hours and fourteen minutes, timed, in the cold, with the river going by under the bridge, and she did not leave, because she had decided she would not leave until someone came out, and she was her father's daughter in exactly the ways she least wanted to be, which was that when she decided a thing she did it.

At forty-eight minutes past eleven the guard brought out an envelope. A plain envelope, a single sheet, in her father's small neat hand, and unsigned, because he had never in his life signed anything.

*They need someone who knows how. I am the only one who has done it.*

She read it standing on the pavement, by the water, in the cold. *Done it.* She knew what he had done: the article, his code in a carrier signal, the comment about bees on a fridge door, the thing that had put a man in prison for a murder her father had not committed and that her father had let happen. She did not know what *this* was, what *they need someone who knows how* meant, what a man who knew how to do a thing like Continuity would be asked to do at a national broadcast directorate in the third week of a war. Her anger came, and her grief came with it, in the same breath, the way they always came for her, together, so that she could never have one without the other; and she did not cry in front of the guard, because the guard was kind and she would not spend her father's gift of grief on a kind guard.

---

In the afternoon she walked back across the city, with the shelling distant and conventional to the east, and she counted the minutes between the rounds, because counting kept her hands still, and she passed the Cabinet Office and thought of Marcus inside it, arguing, and of Kovač, out of a job since the Friday before, and of the whole of it closing in.

Nikolai came in off the day and she told him about the envelope, and the sentence, *they need someone who knows how, I am the only one who has done it*, and Nikolai looked at his hands and said, quietly, that *done it* could mean more than one thing, and that they should be careful not to decide what it meant before they knew, because deciding what a thing meant before you knew was the beginning of being wrong about it; and Maya, who loved him for exactly this, for the refusal to run ahead of the evidence, wanted to shake him for it, because she already knew what it meant, she had known since the cold went through her at the registry panel, and being right and being made to wait for the proof was its own kind of cruelty. She did not shake him. She wrote the sentence in her journal, and the time she had read it, and the two hours and fourteen minutes on the pavement, and left it, the way he would have left it, as a fact without a conclusion, and hated that he was right to make her leave it so.

In the evening she took her shift on the war coordination floor, and on the network schedule the stabilisation blocks now covered four districts, and the content was sealed, and she could read only the timing and the origin at her clearance, and she read those. In the allocation history she found the thing that would matter, though she did not know yet how much: three of the four districts had been carrying a block of the same length, at the same hours, since the twenty-fourth of February, booked not under the new order but under Emergency Information's own powers, labelled only *public reassurance.* Only the fourth district had begun on the twenty-fourth of March. Three districts since the twenty-fourth of February. A month before the law.

She logged the district list, and the dates, and the times, without comment, because a comment was a thing she could not yet stand behind. Kira, beside her, read the note once and handed it back and said, "You'll want to keep that," and Maya kept it, because Kira did not say *you'll want to keep that* about things that did not matter.

---

At twenty to midnight, in the flat, she opened her journal to the month's page.

Every month since her father's conviction she had written the same line, about the murder he had not done, the one true thing she had to hold against everything else he was: *He didn't do it.* It had been the fixed point of three years of monthly visits, the thing she wrote so that it would be written, so that somewhere there was a record, in her hand, that her father was innocent of the one thing the record said he had done.

She wrote it now. *He didn't do it.*

She stopped. She looked at it. Then she crossed it out, with one line, so that it could still be read, because she did not cross things out so that they vanished; she crossed them out so that the change was visible; and under it she wrote the thing that was true now, in her own hand, under the date, by the light of the lamp, with the guns a long way off.

*He is doing it now.*
