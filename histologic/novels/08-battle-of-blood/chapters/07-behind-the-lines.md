# Chapter 7: Behind the Lines

*Wednesday, March 5, 2110. The occupied coast of Veridica*

---

*Cell strength: eleven. Present: nine. Alexei: here.*

Elena Volkov counted them in the loft above the net store before she let herself think about anything else, left to right along the dark under the roof beams, the way she counted a patrol, the way she counted trucks into a barn. Nine of eleven. Two out on the harbour watch. Alexei against the gable end with his boots off and his eyes shut, not asleep. She was forty-six, and hoarse, and she carried a notebook she had never written in, because a thing written down was a thing that could be found, and she had learned to keep her records in the one place the record could not reach, which was her own head, where every name and every face and every count she had ever been responsible for was still kept, in order, and hurt.

The coast had been under occupation for three weeks, and it was not what she had spent her life fighting, and that was the trouble with it.

The relays were dark. The Sovereignty had cut the coastal nodes on the landing day, and for the first time in their lives the people of the strip received no broadcast at all. They walked about inside a silence in their own heads that some of them found unbearable, old people mostly, who had never in sixty years been alone with their own thoughts and did not know how; and some of them slept, for the first time in years, a deep strange sleep, and woke frightened of how good it had been. Nobody in the strip was tracked. Not the fishermen, not the occupiers, not her cells. She moved through her own country invisible for the first time since she was young, and it should have felt like victory, and it felt like standing in someone else's house.

The Sovereignty was disciplined. That was the second trouble. They paid for bread, in a scrip the town was learning to use, and they flogged their own for theft, publicly, once, and after that there was no theft. Their patrols were courteous. They carried paper for everything, orders and maps and the tags on cords around their necks, and they did not put anything behind anyone's ears, and they did not seem to want to.

And at the town hall they were handing out pencils.

She had gone to see it herself, in a headscarf, in the queue, because a thing reported was a thing you had to check. The Sovereignty had set a table inside the hall door, and two soldiers at it, and a stack of cheap paper and a box of pencils, and a printed card in Veridican propped against the box: *WRITE YOUR OWN HISTORY.* Anyone who asked got paper and a pencil and a soldier's courteous nod. The queue went round the square. She had watched a woman she had recruited herself, in 2107, a careful frightened woman who had taken two years to trust a resistance that asked her to keep a journal, come out of the hall holding a pencil in both hands like a thing she had been given for nothing, like a gift, and Elena had stood in the queue and felt the whole of her life tilt.

This was what she had run a resistance for. People keeping their own record, by hand, unchecked by any Judge, in their own words, in their own town. She had sent people to Ashford for less. She had buried people who died trying to teach one street to do it. And the invader was giving it away at a table by the door, for nothing, with a courteous nod, and it worked, because it was true, because a pencil in a free hand was a pencil in a free hand whoever gave it to you.

---

Alexei opened his eyes against the gable end and said it, which was his gift, to say the thing nobody wanted said.

"What if they're the good ones, Aunt Lena."

He said it low, so only she heard, and he was not being clever. He was wry and quick and warm, the way he was warm, and under it he was tired in a way she had learned to watch, because Alexei tired was a man who stopped protecting himself.

"Then they'll forgive us," she said.

He thought about that, and the corner of his mouth went. "You don't believe that."

"No," she said. "But it's the right answer, and you asked it to hear the right answer, so there it is." She did not look round from the window. "They're not the good ones, Lyosha. There are no good ones. There are the ones who corrected my brother and the ones who are giving out pencils to take a machine, and if the pencil ones win they'll find a reason for a machine of their own, because everyone does, because the pencil is cheap and the machine is what you build when the pencil frightens you. The good ones are the woman I recruited in 2107 and the man who died at the culvert tonight and you, asleep against that wall. The good ones are small, and there aren't many of them, and they don't run countries. Go to sleep."

He shut his eyes again. She did not foreshadow anything to herself. He was alive, against a gable end, with his boots off, and she did not let herself do the thing a commander learned not to do, which was to count the living as if they were already names, and she went to the window to watch the square, where the queue for the pencils had not got any shorter in an hour.

---

They had been watching the coast road for eight days, and the courier rode it every second day, and on the eighth day they took him.

A motorcycle, and a light escort vehicle behind it, the same two every time, the same hour, because the Sovereignty was disciplined and the disciplined were predictable, which was the one weakness discipline had. The ambush was at the culvert where the coast road crossed between the salt marsh and the dunes, a blind dip with cover on both sides, and it went at twenty to six in the evening, in a raw wind off the marsh and fog coming up in the dunes, real weather, cold and ordinary. Elena ran it from the bank and counted it as it happened, the way she counted everything, so that afterwards she would have it right.

It was fast and flat and ugly, the way the good ones were. The escort's two soldiers were killed at the vehicle before they were out of it; she sent two of her people forward and it was done in seconds, and she did not look away from it, because looking away was a thing you did for yourself and not for the dead. She took their paper tags off the cords herself, afterwards, in the ditch, two names, and put them inside her coat, because they would go west, to the farmhouse, to the old man, because somebody had to keep them and the old man kept everyone. One of her cell was hit in the arm crossing the road, not badly. The courier, an officer, went down under three of them without firing and did not struggle, and when they stood him up he asked for water, in careful Veridican, and she gave it to him, because a prisoner who had asked for water and been refused was a prisoner who had learned a thing about you that you did not want him to know.

Chained to his wrist was a hard document case. They took him and it back to a ruined boathouse two kilometres inland, and after dark Alexei opened the case by the light of a shielded torch, with the chain cut, and Elena watched over his shoulder.

---

Inside was the SFW advance plan, in the Sovereignty's own script, with overlays she could not read, the roads inland drawn in and marked at intervals with hours. She could read a map without the words; the roads ran to the capital; the hours were a timetable; that much was plain.

And clipped behind it was the other thing, and it should not have existed, and she knew it at once, the way you know your own kind of handwriting in a stranger's drawer.

It was an annex, in Veridican. Not in Veridican words that the Sovereignty had translated; in the Veridican staff format, the form numbers in the corner, the flat dead phrasing of a military order, the grid notation Veridica used and nobody else did, the road designations off a Veridican map. And the batteries by number: she could see the numbers, one through nine, each at a grid reference, and against each of them, in the Sovereignty's margin script, a mark she had learned to read in eight days of their paper, the mark that meant *avoid*. Every *hold* on the Veridican document turned to *bypass* in the enemy's hand. Every *covered* turned to *avoid*. The annex was a Veridican defence plan, and the Sovereignty had read it the way you read a plan you have been handed, and gone around everything it held.

She did not know whose plan it was. She had never seen the coastal defence plan; she was a guerrilla commander in an occupied strip, not a staff officer; she had no idea whose hand had placed those guns or what the whole thing was for. But she knew a Veridican military document when she saw one, because she had spent thirty years learning to read the enemy who was now, this month, somehow, the friend; and a Veridican military document in the case of an enemy courier meant that someone in Veridica had put it there.

The boathouse was half fallen, open to the marsh on one side, and the wind came through it and found the torch and made the light jump, and the tide was out, so that the mud flats stretched away gray under the fog and the hulls of two long-dead boats lay in them like the ribs of things. The prisoner sat in the corner with a man of her cell beside him and did not speak. She had dressed the wounded man's arm herself, in the dark, by feel, before she let anyone light the torch, because a light in a boathouse on an occupied coast was a thing you spent carefully; and now she spent it, once, on the case, because the case was why a man had died at the culvert, and the case was the only thing that would make the dying worth a count.

She read the annex's first page twice, by torchlight, and did not read it aloud.

Alexei read it over her shoulder, because he had no shame and never had, and after a moment he said, quietly, "That's ours."

Elena wrapped the case back in the oilcloth and tied it, and wrote nothing down, because there was nothing she would write down about a thing like this.

"It was," she said.

She sent it inland at first light, by the cells' paper chain, hand to hand toward the capital, toward coastal command, with a cover slip that said only where it had come from and nothing of what it was, because what it was, was not hers to say, and belonged to whoever had placed the guns, and would reach them, if the chain held, and find them, and tell them the one thing she had understood in a boathouse by torchlight, which was that the war had been invited, and the invitation had been written in a Veridican staff office, in Veridican form, by a hand that numbered its guns the Veridican way, and had crossed a beach into an enemy's case, and that somewhere behind all of it was a Veridican who had wanted the Sovereignty to know exactly where the door was.
