# Chapter 1: Questions for the War

*Wednesday, February 12 – Thursday, February 13, 2110. The capital, Veridica*

---

*Feb 12, 2110. 05:20. Capital. Alert: enemy landing, Atlantic coast. Involved parties:*

Marcus Chen stopped there, with the pencil on the page, because he did not know who they were yet.

The national alert had woken him at twenty past five, the long two-note tone that came through the interface and not the ear, so that he had sat up already knowing the words before the screen in the kitchen spelled them out. *Enemy landing. Atlantic coast. Remain in place. Await instruction.* Kira was awake beside him before he was; she slept the way she did everything now, lightly, with a part of her counting. They did not say much. There was not much to say to a sentence like that at twenty past five in the morning. He left the entry open, with its empty last field, because an entry with a false completion was worse than an entry with an honest hole, and he had learned the difference the hard way.

At six the Spire called Kira in. She was to help run the war coordination floor, The Judge's defence outputs, with Nikolai; they had both held the room since the ring of this had started to tighten over the winter. She dressed fast and drank the coffee he made her standing, black, no sugar, and at the door she looked at him for a moment, strained and steady, the way she had looked at him for the eight months since October, and said only, "Keep the list."

"I have it."

He did. It was in his inside pocket, folded, with the sheet behind it that he had carried since January, in Isaiah's upright hand, one line, which he had shown to no one but her. *Someone in Veridica wants to be invaded.* He touched the pocket after she had gone, once, and then made himself stop, because touching it was a thing he did instead of knowing what to do about it.

---

Wednesday the city was orderly, and that was the first thing he wrote down that surprised him.

There were sirens, and queues, and the screens in every street carrying the alert and then the first bulletins, and nobody panicked. He walked across the central district in the cold to see it for himself, because a thing on a screen was a thing someone had chosen to show you and a thing in a street was a thing you had checked. The queues at the water points and the ration points were long and quiet. People stood in them reading the screens and talking low and did not push. On the big panels at the junctions The Judge was already at work: the coastal evacuation unrolling in sequence, town by town, each one timed, the roads inland laid out in colours, green for clear and amber for moving and the long red lines where columns had the road. It knew where every citizen was, because every citizen carried the telemetry that told it, and because it knew, it could move them without jams, pour one town onto a road the minute the town ahead had cleared it, hold a junction empty for ninety seconds and fill it again. He stood at a junction and watched a whole coast-road town come through in the order the machine had set, carts and cars and people on foot, moving like water finding its level, and he admired it, and he noticed that he admired it, and he wrote both things down.

He stood at the junction a long time, in the cold, and watched the town come through, because he had learned that the thing you had to watch was the thing you most admired, since the thing you admired was the thing that would use you. A family passed him with a handcart, a man and a woman and three children and a grandmother, the cart piled with bedding and a birdcage and a clock, and they did not look afraid, because the screen on the corner was telling them, in The Judge's calm green, exactly which road to take and exactly when, and a family told where to go by a voice that had never once been wrong did not feel the fear of a family choosing in the dark. He thought that this was the whole of it, in one handcart: the machine took the fear by taking the choosing, and it was a mercy, and it was the thing he had spent five years learning to be afraid of, and both were true, and he wrote *both* in his head and did not write it on the list, because the list was for questions and this was not a question yet.

The first bulletin came at eight in the evening, on every screen. *Dead since 04:40: 612, of whom 88 civilians. Enemy dead: no telemetry. Not recorded.*

He read the last line twice. *Enemy dead: not recorded.* The Judge counted what reported to it, and nothing on the far side of that beach reported to anything. The enemy did not die into the record. They died, and the record did not know it, and so, in the one account the nation trusted, they had not died at all. He thought of the old man at the farmhouse, who sang the names of the dead off paper because the record would not keep them, and he thought that the old man was about to have a great deal of work.

---

Thursday at ten the war cabinet met.

It met in the long room beside the First Minister's office, with the map panel filling one wall, and Marcus had been summoned to it, which he had not expected. Four men sat at the table when he came in. First Minister Oskar Maitland was behind it with his chin on his fist, big and tired and well tailored, a man who had not slept and had decided long ago it was not worth mentioning. Minister Chen Zhao sat at his right with a glass of tea steaming at his elbow, sixty-nine and silver-haired and grave, and he looked up and said, "Cousin," warmly, as if a war were a family occasion he was glad to see Marcus at. Thomas Wei was at the side table with his tablet, courteous, graying at the temples, the thin pale line across the back of his left hand faded now almost to nothing; he was Head of Emergency Information, and he inclined his head to Marcus and said nothing, because he waited to be asked. And at the far end, in a dark suit, with a flat folder squared to the table's edge, was Undersecretary Iver Pell, who had moved to Public Order over the winter and looked, Marcus thought, like a man who had been given exactly the department he had always wanted.

Marcus noted, as a fact entry, that Wei and Chen Zhao, who had both been absent from the room on the twenty-third of December when Maitland first spoke of this war, were both present now. He did not know who in the building Isaiah's letter was about. He said nothing.

Sokolov reported from the coast by link, her face on the small panel at the end of the map, in the flat register of an order. The enemy was ashore in strength and moving inland. The coastal batteries were engaged. The evacuation was proceeding on The Judge's schedule. She mentioned no gap, and no battery by number, and when Maitland asked her the obvious question, how they had chosen their landing ground, she said, "They came for the undefended beach, First Minister. Any attacker would," and that was all, and Marcus, watching her face on the panel, thought that she said it the way he left a last field blank: truthfully, and not wholly.

"You understand The Judge," Maitland said to Marcus, when the coast report was done. "Better than anyone in this room who isn't running it. I want you where it's being read. Civilian adviser to this cabinet. You'll hear everything and advise on everything, and you'll tell me when it's reading us wrong."

"For the minutes," said Pell, pleasantly, not looking up from his folder, "the proposed adviser's contamination index is zero point five seven."

"I know," said Maitland. "I read it."

"Our cousin," said Chen Zhao, with a small smile into his tea, "has always been expensive. And worth it."

"I'll do it," said Marcus.

He heard himself say it, and he noted, as a fact entry, that he had said yes to a thing he had not been given time to weigh, because the room had been built so that a man said yes in it; Maitland tired behind the desk, Chen Zhao warm at his elbow, Pell with his index for the minutes, Wei waiting to be asked; it was the same room, nearly, as the room in which he had asked for RESTORATION in another winter, the same deep chairs and green-shaded lamps and long south window, and he had learned in that room that a yes said quickly in it was a yes the room had wanted, and he said it anyway, because the alternative was to leave the reading of The Judge to men who read its numbers as certainties, and he would rather be in the room, at nought point five seven, than leave it to Pell.

"Good," said Maitland, and that was the appointment, and nobody wrote anything down, because the war cabinet kept no minutes a historian would ever read, which Marcus also noted.

---

The panel showed him what The Judge could do, and he spent the morning learning the shape of it, because a tool you did not understand was a tool that used you.

It had the whole national network, every road and relay and every citizen's position, and it fought with all of it. It timed convoys to the minute and cleared the roads ahead of them. It routed the ambulances not to the nearest hospital but to the one with a bed, and it knew which had a bed because it knew where everyone was. It assigned shelters street by street as the shelling moved. Its outputs never changed their register: formal, probabilistic, exact, a determination and a number and nothing else. *Probability of enemy advance on the coast road within twenty-four hours: 0.88.* It did not advise and it did not argue; it stated odds, and the men in the room read the odds, and Marcus watched how they read them. Pell read every number as a certainty, a thing decided; Maitland read them as odds, things that might fall the other way; and that difference, Marcus thought, was the whole of the difference between the two men, and he wrote it down.

And it had a limit, which he made himself note as carefully as the power, because the power was seductive and the limit was the truth. The enemy was dark to it. The Sovereignty's soldiers carried nothing that reported, and so on the great lit map of the nation they did not appear. The Judge inferred them. It drew them from the shape of what they did to people who did report: a town that went silent, a relay that stopped, a column of Veridican telemetry that broke and scattered. The empty places on the map, the places where the light of the citizens went out and did not come back, were where the enemy was. The machine that could see every soul in Veridica was blind to the one army in the country, and it hunted that army by the holes it left in everything else. Marcus looked at the map and thought that this was the truest picture of the war anyone would get: a nation of light, and moving through it, felt only by its absence, a thing with no light at all.

There was one debate. An SFW broadcast was expected that evening, carried on the open bands, and Pell wanted it blocked at the relays so that no Veridican would hear the enemy's voice. Wei said, mildly, from the side table, the one thing he said all morning: "Let them hear it. People fight better when they know who is speaking." Maitland agreed, and that was the end of it, and Marcus noted the exchange and did not try to read what was under it, because he had learned that trying to read what was under Wei was a way of being led.

Under *QUESTIONS FOR THE WAR* the first entry still stood alone, from December: *Who is invading whom?* He added nothing to it in the cabinet. He watched, and listened, and kept the list.

---

Thursday evening Kira came home late from the Spire, gray with tiredness, and they watched it together on the screen in the kitchen, standing, because neither of them had the patience to sit.

At nine o'clock every screen in Veridica went to a single image. A man in a plain field uniform, perhaps fifty-five, sat at a bare table with a wall behind him and nothing on it. He spoke Veridican, carefully, with an accent that put the stress a little wrong, a man who had learned the language precisely and used it precisely and did not pretend it was his. He gave his name and his rank, nothing more of either: General Omid Farahani, of the Sovereignty of Free Will. And then he said one sentence, and looked at the camera while he said it, and the broadcast ended.

*"We are not here for your cities."*

The screen went back to the national bulletin. Kira stood with her arms folded, looking at the place where the man's face had been.

"Then what are they here for," she said. It was not a question to him. It was the room's question, hanging.

Marcus took the list out of his pocket and unfolded it on the counter and wrote the second entry under the first, in his small pencil hand, under *Who is invading whom?*

*Then what are they here for?*

He looked at the two lines for a while. Then he folded the list and put it back, and he did not take out the sheet in Isaiah's hand that was folded behind it, though he thought about it, because he did not yet know who in the building it was about, and a thing you could not check was a thing you kept folded until you could.
