# Prologue: Landfall

*Wednesday, February 12, 2110. The Atlantic coast of Veridica*

---

At a quarter past four in the morning Colonel Yevgenia Sokolov stood in the observation bunker on the headland and wrote the date in the watch log, in ink, in the paper register she kept because she had always kept one, and nothing else about it, though it was the headland where she had stood with the three commissioners on the tenth of January and told them where a war would come.

She did not note that. A watch log recorded what a sentry could see, and a sentry could not see the past. She wrote *12 Feb 2110. 04:15. Wind SSE, rising. Sea heavy. Sea fog on the swell, patches. Visibility to the waterline, no further.* Then she put the pen in the clip and picked up the glasses and looked out at the dark where the sea was.

It was real weather, and she was glad of it, because weather was the one thing in her profession that was only itself. The wind came off the water hard enough to find the gaps in the concrete and lay a cold line along the back of her neck. The sea ran heavy under it, gray going to black, with the fog sitting on the swell in long low banks that the wind tore and remade. Below her the beaches were a paler dark between two darker shapes: the headland to the north, where Battery 4 was dug in, and the long spit to the south, where Battery 5 was. Between them, directly below the bunker, the beach ran down clean into the surf, and no gun covered it.

That was deliberate. It was in her plan, which was the nation's plan now, adopted in January when they gave her coastal command. She had placed nine batteries along forty kilometres of coast, numbered one to nine, and she had sited their arcs to overlap everywhere but here, below the headland, where the two nearest could not quite reach the sand between them. She had left the gap on purpose, as an economy, because behind this one beach there was no open country to pour an army into: only salt marsh, and a single causewayed road running inland through it, and she meant to hold the road rather than wall the beach. A wall costs guns all along its length. A road costs two batteries on the ridge behind it, masked, waiting, and she had those: 7 and 8, dug in two kilometres inland, that had not fired and were not meant to be seen. You did not try to stop a landing on the sand. You let it onto the sand and broke it in the throat of the marsh.

She had a phrase for it. She did not think the phrase. She had used it once, aloud, to one person, in December, and she kept it out of the bunker the way she kept her own feelings out of a report, because a phrase was a thing that could be quoted and a report was a thing that could not. She thought instead: *hold the road.* That was the whole of the plan a watch officer needed.

With her in the bunker were a signals officer at the field set and two staff at the plotting table, and she did not need their names in the log, so she did not write them. They were quiet. They had been quiet since the alert came down at midnight, which was the right kind of quiet, the kind that did its work and did not fill the air.

---

At twenty to five the signals officer said, "Colonel," and she had the glasses up before he finished it.

Lights on the horizon, bearing east-south-east. First a few, low and white and unsteady in the fog, and then, as the fog tore and held and tore, a line of them, more than she could count at that range, strung along the dark where the sea met the sky.

She lowered the glasses and wrote it in the register in exactly those words. *04:40. Lights on the horizon, bearing ESE. Numerous. Closing.* She read the line once and did not add to it, because there was nothing to add that a sentry could see. She lifted the glasses again.

By five past five the lights had resolved. Through the glasses, in the gray that was not yet light, she could make out the ships standing off, big slow shapes, and coming away from them under the wind the low broad shapes of landing craft, many of them, in ragged lines, butting into the swell and throwing it up white.

She had read, in the brief, how a landing worked, because reading how a thing worked was a discipline and she did not go into a thing she had only been told about. She knew the names of the craft and the rate they came in and the depth they grounded at and the number of men each carried. She had not known, until she watched it, how slow it was, how a thing she had read as a rate on a page was, on the water, an agony of slowness, craft after craft climbing the swell and dropping behind it and climbing again, so that for whole minutes the sea seemed to hold them still and only the white water at their bows said they moved at all. A thing you had read was a thing you thought you knew. A thing you watched was a different thing, and she wrote that down too, in a way, by writing nothing, by letting the glasses stay up a few seconds longer than a report needed.

"Signal the batteries," she said. "Stand to. Fire on my order, not before."

The signals officer spoke into the set. On the plotting table a staff officer moved markers she did not look at. She knew where the batteries were; she had put them there, walked every one of the nine sites herself in January, in the cold, with the plan in her head and a notebook in her coat, and argued the Ministry out of two of them and into one it had not wanted. She carried the whole of it the way she carried everything, as a map she could read in the dark, and she did not need the plotting table, and she let the staff officer have it, because a staff officer needed a thing to do with his hands in the first hour of a war as much as a gun crew did.

---

At twelve minutes past five she gave the order she had built her whole career to give, and it did nothing.

"Bring the units up."

On the beach roads behind both batteries the transmitter vehicles came up out of their revetments, the gray Border Security vans with the masts folding up off their roofs, and began to broadcast. This was the fact-storm programme, her programme, twenty years of it, the thing she had run on the lakeshore against Chronos and against three brothers she had arrested in a fog and let go in another: a false history of a stretch of ground, pushed over the true one, so that a man standing on a beach would *know*, with the certainty that came from inside and could not be argued with, that there was no beach, that the coast here was drowned, that the sand under his boots was a field of winter wheat, that the way inland was a road that ran where no road ran. She had seen it empty a district. She had seen it kill. She had watched men walk off the end of a quay toward a pier their own heads told them was there.

She watched now, through the glasses, as the first wave came off the craft into the surf and up the sand.

They did not look at the road that was not there. They did not stop at the drowned coast. They came up the beach at a steady walk, bent against the wind, spreading as they came, with the flat unhurried movement of men doing a thing they had rehearsed, and not one of them so much as glanced at the field of wheat her units were laying over the sea behind them.

*No interfaces.* She had known it. Everyone had known it; it was the whole of the brief; the Sovereignty of Free Will put nothing behind its soldiers' ears, and a broadcast needs something to land on, and there was nothing. She had known it as a fact for a month. She watched it now as a thing happening on a beach below her, her life's work switched on and pouring itself into heads that could not receive it, and she wrote in the register, flatly, *05:14. Enemy infantry ashore under broadcast. Effect: none observed.*

Then her own soldiers flinched.

The gun crews at Battery 5 were on the spit, within range of the unit broadcasting behind them, and they had interfaces, because they were Veridicans, and the broadcast had them. Through the glasses she watched a loader stop with a shell halfway to the breech, his head turning to look at a field where the sea was. A sergeant put both hands flat on the concrete of the emplacement and held them there, the way you hold on to a thing that is real when the world has stopped being. One man sat down. On the whole beach, in that minute, the only people who could perceive the contradiction her units were broadcasting were the people she had sent to stop the landing.

"Units down," she said. "All units. Now."

The signals officer sent it. On the beach roads the masts folded. She wrote it in the register without a word of comment, because there was no comment a watch log took: *05:24. All transmitter units ordered down. Interference with own forces.* She had spent twenty years perfecting a weapon, and in the first hour of the first real war of her life she had switched it off because it blinded her own men and the enemy could not see it at all.

---

After that it was the old kind of war, the kind her grandfather might have fought, and she found she knew it less well than she knew the other.

The batteries fired. Four and five had the beach from the flanks, and the shells went out into the gray and the fog over the surf, and she could not see what they did, because the fog was so thick now in the first light that it could have been night. She put the glasses down; there was nothing to see with them. She listened. The guns were a steady concussion she felt in the concrete more than heard, and under it, carried on the wind in snatches, the smaller flatter sound of the infantry's weapons as the first wave reached the dunes.

At one point the fog thinned for a minute over the spit and she saw a single gun crew at Battery 5 serving its piece: five men in the white half-light, moving in the worn economical way of men who have done a thing ten thousand times, loading and firing and loading, in fog so thick the gun ahead of them in the line was a gray idea and the sea below was a sound. They did not know, those five, whether they were hitting anything. The fog ate the fall of shot; the spotters had nothing to spot; the battery fired on a bearing and a range off the plot and trusted the arithmetic, and the arithmetic was hers, and she did not know either whether it was hitting anything, because the fog was a wall between the gun and the thing it was firing at, and a war fought through a wall was a war fought on faith, which was a thing her whole career had been built to remove from the world and had not removed from this morning.

They served the gun because serving the gun was the thing in front of them. One man knelt at the ready rack and passed the shells up, and another took them and set them, and the breech took them, and the gun threw itself back on its mounting and came forward again, and the men did it and did it, their faces wet, their movements small and exact, five men in a room of fog doing the one thing they knew how to do and not knowing whether it mattered; and she watched them for the minute the fog gave her, and then it closed again and took them, and she wrote nothing about it, because it was not a thing a watch log held, and she kept it instead in the place where she kept the things she would not write down, beside the open bottle she did not yet know was open, and the gap she had left on purpose that the enemy had come through.

At nine minutes to six the signals officer said, "Colonel. We've lost two units. Behind Five. Overrun before the crews could burn them."

"Intact?"

"We think so, Colonel. The crews are reporting back, but the vans—" He stopped. "They didn't have time."

"Log it," she said, and wrote it herself as well, because she did not trust a thing she had not written in her own hand: *05:51. Two transmitter units overrun on the beach road behind Battery 5. Captured, condition believed intact. Crews withdrawn.* She looked at the line for a moment. A transmitter unit was a weapon that worked on anyone with an interface, and the people it worked on, now, were all of them Veridican. She did not follow the thought. She filed it where she filed things, which was nowhere she would have to look at them before she had time.

---

The light came up gray and the fog began, slowly, to lift, and by a little after six she could see the beach again, and the war was ashore.

The first wave was up off the sand and into the dunes and moving inland, in good order, more of it landing behind. And it had come ashore, she saw, where she had known from the plot it would come, where the reports put it, below the headland, on the one stretch of beach that no gun covered: in the gap between Battery 4 and Battery 5. It had not tried the beaches the batteries held. It had not probed the flanks and been driven to the centre. It had come straight for the gap, the whole weight of the first wave, as if the gap had been marked on a chart and handed to the man who steered the leading craft.

She lifted the glasses and looked at it for a while. Then she put them down and took up the pen.

She wrote the report in the register in the report's own flat voice, the only voice she trusted for a thing like this, the voice that stated and did not interpret: *06:04. First wave ashore in strength, sector between Battery 4 and Battery 5. Enemy did not attempt the covered beaches. Advancing on the marsh road.*

She read the line once. There was a question under it, the obvious question, the one a staff officer at the plotting table would ask in a minute and she would have to answer: how did they know where the gap was. She did not write the question, because a watch log did not ask questions, and she did not let herself think past it, because thinking past it was not a thing a sentry did, and she was, this morning, only a sentry, writing down what she could see. She initialled the entry, *Y.S.*, and handed the register to the signals officer to send up the line to command, and picked the glasses back up, and watched the Sovereignty of Free Will come ashore through the hole in her own plan, in the cold, in the real and lifting fog.
