# Chapter 13: Artillery

*Wednesday, March 26 – Thursday, March 27, 2110. SFW-held farmland east of the capital*

---

Since Monday night the Sovereignty's guns had been shelling the Chronos field hospital at the capital's eastern edge, and the tents were clearly marked, and Tanaka's count had come west through Nikolai on Tuesday with no request attached to it: four dead, all Chronos staff, eleven wounded. Elena had read it once, in the loft, by the lamp, and said, "Tonight," and that was the whole of the planning conversation, because the rest of it was Alexei's.

The guns were four towed pieces in a disused rail freight yard two kilometres east of the hospital, with a crew asleep in two railway wagons and sentries on a fixed round, and the only way in was a drainage culvert under the embankment, knee-deep in cold water, and Alexei had crawled it twice in the dark to count the sentries before he brought the team.

He took six of Elena's people, all from the coast cells, in the resistance's dark clothing, with Veridican engineer explosives the cells had lifted on the coast in the first week. Elena stayed at a ruined barn two kilometres back with the radio, because she ran operations and he ran the ground; that was the division between them and had been for years, and he did not resent it, because Elena on the radio counting was worth more than Elena in a culvert, and she knew the ground too well to be the one who crawled it.

While they waited in the culvert for the round to come to the far point of its arc, Alexei hummed the Lakeshore Air under his breath, so low it was more breath than sound, the vault Old Songs had given him in Ashford's library a lifetime ago. *They look for what you love. So I hide what I love in what I've memorised.* There was nothing to hide tonight, and nothing hunting for it; the Sovereignty had no interfaces; nothing he had ever learned about storms and seeding mattered against men who could not be reached by either. This was a war of mud and timing and a sentry's boots, the oldest kind, and he found, humming in the cold water, that he was good at it, better than he was at the new kind, and that being good at the oldest kind of war was not a thing a man should be glad of and he was glad of it anyway.

---

The yard was a long rail freight yard gone to weeds, with the four towed guns sited along a loading dock and the crew asleep in two wagons on a siding and the ammunition stacked under tarpaulins between. He lay in the culvert mouth with the water to his chest and watched it for an hour before he moved, because watching was the half of the work that kept you alive, and he counted everything: the sentries, two of them, on a round that took four minutes; the lights, which were few, because the Sovereignty kept a dark yard, being disciplined; the distance from the culvert to the dock, which was forty metres of open weed; and the gap, the one gap, between the near wagon and the gun line, where for a count of thirty each round neither sentry could see the dock.

He counted the round three times, and it did not vary, because the Sovereignty was disciplined, and discipline was a clock, and a clock was a thing you could set a watch by and a charge by. Two sentries, a fixed path, the same gap at the same interval between the near wagon and the gun line. He put his team in, two at a time, in the gaps, with the charges on the breeches and on the ammunition stack, and his own hands were steady at the work, because his hands had always been steady at the work; it was afterwards they shook.

At ten to two a sentry changed his round. He walked it one wagon's length short, the first variation in three nights, and the timing broke, and a man of Alexei's who was crossing the open to the gun line was caught between the stack and the wagon with nowhere to be.

A flare went up. Then the automatic fire from the wagons, the crew awake and firing before they were properly out of their blankets, and in the white light of the flare the woman from the coast cell who was crossing the open went down and did not get up, and two more of his people were hit, one of them badly, and the charges were set and not yet fired, and the whole of it came apart in the space of a breath, the way the bad ones did.

Alexei got the others moving back toward the culvert with the wounded, and stayed.

He stayed at the embankment alone, because the charges had to fire and the crews had to be kept off them until the timers ran, and there was no one else to do the keeping, and it was the thing he was for. He fired up at the wagons to hold their heads down, and moved, and fired, and the reader of his own life, if his life had a reader, would have been sure here, because this was the shape of it: the Phantom, the last man out, the rearguard holding the line while the others crossed. A round tore through his pack and spun him half round. Another took the sleeve of his jacket and burned a line along his arm. And then the ammunition stack went up, all of it at once, a white wall of it, and threw him flat on the embankment with his ears gone and the breath knocked out of him and the four guns in the yard torn apart in the light of their own ammunition.

He lay in the mud and the ringing for a moment, and his thought, when it came, was simple and almost disappointed: *not yet, then.*

He got up. He walked back through the culvert, through the cold water to his waist, with his arm burning and his pack ruined and his ears singing, and he was alive.

---

At twenty to four he came up the field path to the ruined barn, and Elena counted him and the others in, left to right, the way she counted everything.

"Six," she said. "I sent seven."

"The rail yard," said Alexei. "By the stack. She was crossing the open when the sentry changed." He told her where the woman had fallen, exactly, because Elena would want it exactly, and Elena wrote nothing down, because she never wrote anything down, and he knew that meant she had it, in the place where she kept them all, and would carry it.

She dressed the burn on his arm herself, in the barn, by the lamp, her hands quick and her face very still, the way her face went when a thing had cost and she would not let it show. He sat on an upturned crate, soaked through, shaking with the cold now that it was over, the way he always shook after, and he laughed once, because laughing was the thing that let the shaking be cold and not fear.

"I keep thinking this is the one," he said.

She did not answer at first. She tied off the dressing, her hands quick and exact, and he watched her face do the thing it did when a thing had cost and she would not show it, which was to do nothing, to go still and careful, the way Dmitri's face went still, because they were all of them, he thought, people who had learned to put the cost somewhere the enemy could not read it. Then, hoarse, not looking at him: "Don't."

"Don't what."

"Don't say it like it's a joke you'll win. 'This is the one.' You've said it on every job since the strip. One of these days it'll be the one, Lyosha, and I will have heard you say it forty times and not believed you the forty-first, and I will not forgive myself." She pulled the dressing tight. "So don't."

"Aunt Lena." He did not call her that in front of the cells. He said it now, quietly, because it was the two of them and the lamp and the cold, and she let him, which she did not always, and that was its own kind of answer.

He thought, because thinking about his brothers was a thing he did when he was shaking, what Dmitri would say about the timing. Dmitri would have planned for the sentry. Dmitri planned for everything; it was the whole of Dmitri; and he would have had a man watching the sentry and a signal for a changed round, and the woman from the coast cell would not have been in the open when the round came short. It annoyed Alexei that this was true. It annoyed him the way his brother's rightness had always annoyed him, since they were boys on a parapet, and the annoyance was a warm thing in the cold barn, and he held on to it, because it was Dmitri, and Dmitri was a steady cold weight at the edge of him even now, at the front, kilometres away, holding a road.

---

He thought, in the barn, with the dressing tight on his arm and the shaking going out of him, about the woman from the coast cell who had fallen in the rail yard, whose name he did not know well, who had crossed the open ground when the sentry came short, and he made himself remember her face, because Elena would remember it and he would not let Elena carry it alone, and because the old man would want the name, and a name went west to the old man and into the song, and that was the one thing he could do for her now, which was to be sure her name got to the man who kept names. He would send it with the next courier. He had sent names before. He had carried people out of a burning country for ten years and some of them had not made it and he had learned to turn the not-making-it into a name on a slip, because a name on a slip was a thing the old man could sing, and a thing the old man sang was a thing that stayed, and staying was the only victory the dead were allowed.

At twenty past five the radio spoke.

It was Nikolai, from the war coordination floor in the Spire, his voice thin and careful through the set. Isaiah had sent word, through one of the coast cells, that he wanted to come across the lines, and he was bringing something.

Alexei sat on the crate, soaked, with his eyes shut and his arm throbbing under the dressing and the ringing still in his ears, and he smiled, because of course he did, because it was Isaiah and it was the shape of a thing he knew how to do.

"Of course he does," said Alexei.

And he did not know, sitting on the crate in the cold with the shaking going out of him, that the one he was for, the extraction, the man at the fence, was not the woman who had fallen in the rail yard tonight, and was not himself tonight, and was waiting for him four weeks on, at an overpass on the eastern ring road, with Isaiah's coat full of the other side's secret and a gun below on the embankment; and that the thing the reader had been sure of tonight, and been wrong about, would be true then, and he would be the last man out, and he would not come up a field path afterwards shaking and alive. He did not know it. He only knew that Isaiah wanted to come across, and that bringing a man across a line was the whole point of him, and that he was glad, in the cold barn, to have a reason to be the Phantom one more time.
