# Chapter 22: Dad

*Monday, May 5, 2110. The National Broadcast Directorate, the capital*

---

At twenty to two Nikolai waited in the van at the east end of the Meridian Bridge with an earpiece, and Maya walked to the gate of the National Broadcast Directorate on a work order he had raised through war systems: *clock distribution fault, relay hall 2*, with her name on it as the Commission technician. She timed herself in. *In at 14:06.*

The hall was calm. There were Public Order officers at the doors, and they were pleasant, and everyone in the Directorate was pleasant, and she noticed, going through, the thing she had learned to notice, which was how people typed: slowly, and certainly, without ever backspacing, as if no one in the building had ever written a wrong word.

They took her through relay hall 2, and past the calibration floor, and she saw the cradles.

There were six of them, hoods on jointed arms over reclining chairs, and in each chair a person, lying back with a calm face, breathing slowly, not asleep and not awake. Reference minds, she understood, without being told: people from the list, against whom the carrier was tuned, so that what went out into the districts was shaped to a living standard. She knew the shape of a cradle from Isaiah's article and from her father's code, and she felt the stair-missing lurch of it, the cold, and she made her face do nothing, because the Public Order officer at the end of the aisle was pleasant and watching.

She looked at the six faces, because she could not help looking, and they were ordinary faces, a man of fifty, a woman of thirty, a boy who could not have been twenty, each of them lying back under a hood with the small settled smile she had seen on the bridge and in the water queue, the face of a person who was being held certain so that a city could be tuned to their certainty. She timed her own breathing, because it had gone wrong, and brought it back, and did not let the officer see that it had gone wrong. They were instruments. Her father would have called them instruments; they were the standard the carrier was measured against; and that was the thing about her father that she had spent twelve years trying to hold and never managing, that he did not hate anyone, that there was no cruelty in him, that he looked at six people under hoods and saw six instruments and meant them no harm at all and would have been puzzled to be told he had done any, because an instrument was not a thing you harmed, it was a thing you read.

---

At nineteen minutes past two her father came down the stairs from the glass office above the relays.

He was courteous and unhurried, in a gray suit, with clean hands, fifty-eight and thinner than at the prison, and he looked at her and his face did a thing that she realised, watching it, she had come precisely to see: it was glad.

"Maya," said Dr. Reeves. "You came in properly. Through a work order." A soft, delighted laugh, the laugh she remembered from the fridge door and the bees. "I'm proud of you."

He showed her the floor the way a colleague shows a visitor round, pleased with it, pleased with the scale of it. A wall panel of the capital's districts, each with a curve climbing on it. "Interesting, isn't it," he said. "Nobody has ever done a city. Districts, yes. A ward. A wing. Never a city. We don't know what a city does, coming up or going down. It's the first time." He said *first time* the way another man might say a thing he loved.

She asked him whether he had known what was at the front of the order, the preamble.

"I heard it said in meetings," he said. "Years ago. Before Ashford. I always assumed it was the Ministry's own. It was so obviously right that nobody ever needed to sign it, and nobody ever did." He had never known who wrote it, he said, and had never asked, because a true thing did not need an author; and he did not name the manuscript, because he did not know there was one; he only knew a sentence that had been in the air of certain rooms for thirty years, and had seemed to him, always, simply correct.

---

"How do I stop it," said Maya.

He considered her with real interest, the terrible precise interest he gave to everything. He had no patriotism in him and no justification; that was the horror of him; he did not defend the thing or regret it; he was curious about it, the way he had always been curious, and his candour was worse than any cruelty would have been.

"I'd like to see what happens if you turn it off," said Dr. Reeves.

He told her the weakness, in his own words, as a man explains a thing he finds elegant.

"They gave me a week," he said. "A week to put a city on a carrier that took me years to build for one wing. So I cut a corner. The carrier has no clock of its own. There wasn't time to give it one. It rides the national clock, the time signal every interface keeps, that keeps everything in step. If the capital's clock stayed exactly true, the carrier would run for ever." He smiled, the kindest smile she had ever seen on anyone. "Shift the clock under it. Even a little. A fraction, never the same way twice, so it can't find the beat again. The carrier arrives out of step, and the interfaces take it for noise, and discard it. The dose stops." He tilted his head. "Nobody should die of it. Probably. I don't know. That's the interesting part. I've never seen a population come off it. Nobody has."

"Why are you telling me," said Maya.

"Because I've never seen a population wake up," said her father. "Nobody has. And I would like to know what it looks like. And you are the only person who will tell me, because you are the only person who both can do it and will come back and say." He said it without any shame at all. He wanted the data. He had always wanted the data.

"You know I'll use it," she said. "You know I'll turn it off."

"I know," he said, pleased. "That's the experiment. I could not do it myself; I am the one person who must not; they watch me do everything. But you can, and you will, and you'll come and tell me what a city looks like coming off the carrier, because you are my daughter and you cannot leave a thing un-measured any more than I can. I taught you that, I think. It's the only thing of mine you kept." He said it fondly, as a gift, the worst gift he had ever given her, which was to tell her that the one thing she had of him was the thing that made her able to do the thing he could not. She timed it: *14:41.* In her ear, Nikolai said nothing at all, and she was glad of the nothing, because if Nikolai had said anything she might have wept, and she would not weep in her father's broadcast directorate on the calibration floor with the officer at the end of the aisle.

He walked her back toward the stairs, past the wall panel with its curves, past the relay racks, unhurried, a host seeing a guest to the door of a thing he was proud of. He pointed out one curve, a district's, and told her it was the first to come up and the cleanest, and that he would have liked longer with it, that a week was no time at all for a city, that the thing wanted a year to do properly; and she listened to her father talk about correcting a city the way another man might talk about a garden he had not been given time to plant right, and she understood that he was, in his way, happy, happier than she had seen him in twelve years, because he had been given a city, and a city was the thing he had always wanted and never had, and the war had given it to him, which was the whole of why the war had been wanted, by someone, somewhere above her father, who had known that a man like her father, given a war, would ask for a city, and be grateful.

On the way out she asked what happened to the six in the cradles.

"They'll wake with everyone else," said Reeves. "If you do it." He said it without interest, because the six were not the experiment; the city was the experiment; the six were instruments, and an instrument was not a thing you wondered about.

She did not thank him. On the stairs her anger came and her grief came with it, in the same breath, together as they always came, and she did not let him see either, and she thought of her journal on the twenty-ninth of March, *He didn't do it*, crossed out, *He is doing it now.*

---

At two minutes past three, at the door, he held it for her, courteously, the way he held every door.

"You still write that I didn't do it," he said.

She stopped in the doorway. He knew, from her monthly prison visits, that she had written it every month for three years, the one true thing she had held against him, that he was innocent of the Singh murder, which he was. *You still write that I didn't do it.* It was a question, and it was almost gentle, and it was the nearest thing to asking for absolution that a man who did not believe in absolution could make.

"You didn't," said Maya. "You did this."

She walked out onto the Meridian Bridge, into the cold, with the river going by under it and the carrier's weakness in her head, and she did not look back. She timed that too: the moment she was through the door, the moment she was on the bridge, the moment she was out of his sight, each of them, because timing things was what she had instead of a father, and it kept her hands still.
